# Polybius — The Histories, Book XVIII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 18 Chapter 1: The War with Philip

I. Affairs in Macedonia and Greece

When the appointed time came, Philip sailed out from Demetrias into the Melian Gulf with five light galleys and one pristis, the ship on which he himself was sailing.

With him were the Macedonians Apollodorus and Demosthenes, his secretaries; Brachylles from Boeotia; and Cycliadas the Achaean, who had been driven from the Peloponnese for the reasons I mentioned earlier.

With Titus were King Amynander and Dionysodorus from Attalus,

and from the leagues and cities there were Aristaenus and Xenophon for the Achaeans, Acesimbrotus the admiral for the Rhodians, Phaeneas the general for the Aetolians, and many other public men besides.

When they had come near Nicaea by the sea, Titus and his party took their stand on the very edge of the shore, while Philip brought his ship close in and remained on board.

When Titus told him to come ashore, Philip rose from his seat on the ship and said he would not disembark.

When Titus then asked whom he was afraid of, Philip answered that he feared no one except the gods, but that he distrusted most of those present, especially the Aetolians.

The Roman commander was surprised and said that the danger was equal for all and the moment a common one; but Philip replied that he was mistaken.

If anything happened to Phaeneas, he said, the Aetolians would have many others to serve as generals; but if Philip were killed in the present crisis, the Macedonians would have no one to be their king.

Everyone thought this a heavy-handed way to begin the conversation. Even so, Titus told him to speak about the matters for which he had come.

Philip replied that it was not for him to begin, but for Titus; therefore he asked Titus to state plainly what he must do in order to have peace.

The Roman commander answered that what he had to say was simple and perfectly clear.

Philip must withdraw from the whole of Greece, restore to each state the prisoners and deserters he held,

hand over to the Romans the places in Illyria that he had occupied since the settlement in Epirus, and likewise restore to Ptolemy all the cities he had taken after the death of Ptolemy Philopator.

## Book 18 Chapter 2: Congress of Nicaea

After saying this Titus stopped speaking, turned to the others, and told each man to state what instructions he had received from those who had sent him.

Dionysodorus, the envoy of Attalus, spoke first. He said Philip must give back the king's ships that had been captured in the naval battle off Chios, together with the men taken on them, and must also restore unharmed the sanctuary of Aphrodite and the Nicephorium, which he had damaged.

After him Acesimbrotus, the Rhodian admiral, demanded that Philip withdraw from the Peraea, which he had taken from them, and remove his garrisons from Iasus, Bargylia, and the city of the Euromians,

and that he restore the people of Perinthus to their civic union with Byzantium, and also give up Sestus, Abydus, and all the trading-posts and harbors in Asia.

After the Rhodians the Achaeans demanded Corinth and the safety of Argos.

After them the Aetolians said first, like the Romans, that he must leave all Greece, and second that he must restore to them unharmed the cities that had formerly shared in the Aetolian confederacy.

## Book 18 Chapter 3: Speech of Alexander Isius

When Phaeneas, the Aetolian general, had spoken, Alexander, called Isius, a man thought to be experienced in affairs and a capable speaker, took up the argument.

He said that Philip was neither honestly seeking peace now nor bravely conducting war when war was required, but that in conferences and meetings he laid traps, watched for opportunities, and behaved like a belligerent, while in actual war he took a course that was both unjust and thoroughly ignoble.

Instead of meeting his enemies face to face, he fled about setting cities on fire and plundering them, and by this policy, even while defeated, he spoiled the prizes that properly belonged to the victors.

Yet the earlier kings of Macedon, he said, had not followed this policy, but the opposite one. They fought one another continually in the open field, but only rarely destroyed and ruined cities.

This was plain, he said, both from the war in Asia that Alexander fought against Darius and from the struggles among Alexander's successors, when they all fought Antigonus for possession of Asia.

In the same way those who succeeded them continued to follow this principle down to the time of Pyrrhus.

They readily risked battle against one another in the open and did everything to master each other by force of arms, but they spared the cities, so that the victors might rule them and receive honor from those who were brought under their power.

To destroy the very things for which a war is being fought was, he said, madness, and violent madness at that; and this was exactly what Philip was doing now.

For while hurrying from the passes in Epirus he had destroyed more cities in Thessaly, though he was Thessaly's friend and ally, than anyone who had ever made war on the Thessalians had destroyed.

After speaking at length on this theme, he ended with the following questions.

Why had Philip seized Lysimacheia, a city aligned with the Aetolians and governed by a magistrate appointed by them, expelled that official, and held the city with a garrison?

Why had he enslaved the people of Cius, who were likewise members of the Aetolian confederacy, though he was supposedly a friend of the Aetolians? And on what grounds was he now holding Echinus, Phthian Thebes, Pharsalus, and Larisa?

## Book 18 Chapter 4: Philip's Response

When Alexander had finished, he fell silent. Philip brought his ship nearer the shore than before, stood up on deck, and said that Alexander's speech had been delivered in the true Aetolian and theatrical style.

Everyone knew perfectly well, he said, that no one willingly destroys his own allies; but circumstances and critical moments often force leaders to do many things contrary to their own intentions.

While the king was still speaking, Phaeneas, who was extremely weak-sighted, interrupted Philip and said that he was talking nonsense: he ought either to win by fighting or to do what the stronger side ordered.

Philip, though he was in a bad position, still could not give up his usual habit. Turning toward Phaeneas, he said, "That, Phaeneas, is obvious even to a blind man." He was quick-witted and naturally good at mocking people.

Then he turned back again to Alexander and said, "You ask me, Alexander, why I took possession of Lysimacheia.

It was so that, through your negligence, it would not be laid waste by the Thracians, as in fact it now has been, once I was forced by this war to remove my troops from it—not a garrison occupying the city, as you say, but men guarding it.

As for the people of Cius, I did not make war on them. Prusias was at war with them, and I helped him destroy them; and you were the cause of it.

For many times both I and the other Greeks sent embassies to you, asking you to repeal the law that gave you the right to carry off spoil from spoil, but you said you would sooner remove Aetolia from Aetolia than repeal that law."

## Book 18 Chapter 5: Philip Retorts On His Accusers

When Titus expressed surprise at what this meant, the king tried to make it clear to him, saying that the Aetolians had a custom of plundering not only those against whom they themselves were at war, together with their territory,

but, even when other peoples were at war with one another while being friends and allies of the Aetolians, it was no less permitted to the Aetolians—without any common decree—to assist both of the warring parties at once and to plunder the territory of both alike,

so that among the Aetolians there were no fixed boundaries of friendship or of enmity; instead, they stood ready to be the foes and enemies of everyone who had a quarrel about anything.

"On what grounds, then," he asked, "are they entitled to bring charges against me now, if I, being a friend of the Aetolians but an ally of Prusias, took some action against the Cians in support of my own allies?

But the most outrageous thing of all is that these men set themselves up as rivals of the Romans and order the Macedonians to withdraw from the whole of Greece.

To utter such a thing is arrogant in general; yet, when Romans say it, it is bearable, but when Aetolians say it, it is not bearable. And from what kind of Greece," he said,

"do you order me

to withdraw, and how do you define its limits? For most of the Aetolians themselves are not Greeks: the nation of the Agraeans, that of the Apodoti, and likewise that of the Amphilochians are not part of Greece.

Or do you grant me leave to keep those?"

## Book 18 Chapter 6: Philip's Answer to the Rhodians and Attalus

When Titus laughed, Philip said, "Well, let that be enough on my part as regards the Aetolians. But as for the Rhodians and Attalus—before an impartial judge it would be reckoned more just that they should give back to us the captured ships and the men than that we should give them back to these people.

For we were not the first to lay hands on Attalus and the Rhodians; it was they who did so to us, as is generally admitted.

Nevertheless, since you bid me, I restore the Peraea to the Rhodians, and to Attalus the ships and such of the men as have survived.

As for the destruction of the Nicephorium and the precinct of Aphrodite, there is no other way I can restore them, but I will send plants and gardeners to take charge of caring for the place and of growing back the trees that were cut down."

When Titus laughed again at this mockery, Philip turned to the Achaeans and first enumerated the benefactions that had been conferred upon them by Antigonus, and then his own.

Next to these he set forth the magnitude of the honors that had been bestowed on them by the Achaeans.

Finally he read out the decree concerning their defection and their going over to the Romans; using this as his pretext, he said much against the Achaeans, charging them with faithlessness and ingratitude.

Even so, he said he would give back Argos, but that regarding Corinth he would take counsel with Titus.

## Book 18 Chapter 7: A Retort of Flamininus

Having discussed these matters with the others, he turned to Titus, saying that his argument was really with him and with the Romans, and asked whether he thought he ought to withdraw only from the cities and places he had himself acquired among the Greeks, or also from those he had inherited from his forefathers.

When Titus fell silent, Aristaenus on behalf of the Achaeans and Phaeneas on behalf of the Aetolians were ready to answer him on the spot.

But as the hour was now drawing to a close, their speech was cut off by the lateness of the time, and Philip requested that they all hand him in writing the terms on which the peace would have to be made; for, being there alone, he had no one with whom to deliberate,

and therefore he wished to give himself time to reflect on the demands being imposed.

Titus was not displeased to hear Philip jesting; but, not wishing to seem so to the others, he gibed back at Philip, saying:

"Naturally, Philip, you are alone now—for you have destroyed all the friends who would have given you the best advice." At this the Macedonian gave a bitter, sardonic smile and said nothing.

And so for the time being they all,

having handed Philip their own positions in writing in accordance with what had been said before, departed, arranging to meet again the next day at Nicaea.

But on the morrow Titus and his party all came to the appointed place at the agreed hour, while Philip did not appear.

## Book 18 Chapter 8: The Dispute Referred To the Senate

When the day was already far advanced and Titus and his party had nearly given up hope, Philip arrived late in the afternoon, appearing with the same companions as before,

having used up the whole day—as he himself claimed, because of his difficulty and embarrassment over the demands being imposed, but as it seemed to everyone else, because he wished to use the lateness of the hour to shut out the accusations of both the Achaeans and the Aetolians.

For he had seen, when he was leaving on the previous day, that both of these parties were ready to grapple with him and to lodge complaints against him.

For this reason, on this occasion too, as he drew near, he asked the Roman general to confer with him privately about the matters at hand, so that there should not be merely a war of words between the two contending sides, but that some conclusion might be reached on the points in dispute.

When he repeatedly urged and pressed this request, Titus asked those present with him what he ought to do.

When they bade him meet with Philip and hear what he had to say, Titus took along Appius Claudius, who was then a military tribune, told the others to withdraw a little way from the sea and stay there, and ordered Philip himself to come ashore.

The king, taking with him Apollodorus and Demosthenes, disembarked, and on meeting Titus conversed with him for a considerable time.

What was said by each of them on that occasion is hard to tell; but after Philip had departed, Titus did at least make plain to the others what the king had offered:

namely, to give back Pharsalus and Larisa to the Aetolians, but not to give back Thebes; to yield the Peraea to the Rhodians, but not to withdraw from Iasus and Bargylia; to hand over Corinth and the city of the Argives to the Achaeans;

to declare that he would surrender to the Romans his holdings in Illyria together with all the prisoners; and to restore to Attalus the ships and such of the men captured in the sea-battles as still survived.

## Book 18 Chapter 9: They Agree to Send Envoys to the Roman Senate

Everyone present was dissatisfied with the settlement and insisted that Philip must first carry out the general requirement—namely, to withdraw from the whole of Greece—and that otherwise the piecemeal concessions amounted to nothing and served no purpose.

Seeing the dispute among them and at the same time fearing their accusations, Philip asked Titus to postpone the meeting until the next day, since the hour was already drawing toward evening; for he would either persuade them or be persuaded by what was being urged.

Titus agreed, and after arranging to come together at the shore near Thronium they parted for the time being; and on the following day they all arrived at the appointed place on time.

Philip, after speaking briefly, asked them all, and especially Titus, not to break off the settlement, now that most of them at least had been brought into a disposition toward agreement,

but, if it were possible, to reach an accord among themselves about the points in dispute; and, if not, he said he would send an embassy to the Senate and either persuade it concerning the matters at issue, or do whatever it might command.

When he put forward these proposals, all the rest declared that they ought to press on with the war and pay no attention to his requests.

But the Roman commander said that he too was well aware that Philip was unlikely to do any of the things being asked of him;

yet, since granting the favor the king requested in no way hindered their own operations, he said he would grant it as a concession.

For none of the things now being discussed could be ratified without the Senate in any case, and the approaching season was well suited for testing the Senate's mind:

since the armies were unable to accomplish anything during the winter anyway, to set this time aside for referring the present circumstances to the Senate was not out of place but advantageous for everyone.

## Book 18 Chapter 10: Pleadings Before the Senate

When they all quickly agreed, seeing that Titus was not opposed to referring the matter to the Senate,

it was resolved to allow Philip to send an embassy to Rome, and likewise that each party should send envoys of their own to address the Senate and bring charges against Philip.

Since the affair of the conference had proceeded for Titus according to his wishes and in line with the calculations he had made from the start, he at once went on weaving the continuation of his design, carefully securing his own position and giving Philip no advantage whatsoever.

For having granted him a two-month truce, he ordered him to complete the embassy to Rome within that time, and commanded him to withdraw his garrisons from Phocis and Locris immediately.

He also made careful provision on behalf of his own allies, so that during this period no wrong of any kind should be done to them by the Macedonians.

Having put these terms in writing for Philip, he then carried out the remaining part of his plan himself.

He sent Amynander off to Rome at once, knowing him to be easily led and likely to follow readily the friends he had there, in whichever direction they might draw him, while also creating a certain impression and expectation because of his royal title.

After him he sent his own envoys: Quintus Fabius, who was his wife's nephew, Quintus Fulvius, and along with these Appius Claudius, surnamed Nero.

On behalf of the Aetolians the envoys were Alexander Isius, Damocritus of Calydon, Dicaearchus of Trichonium, Polemarchus of Arsinoe,

Lamius of Ambracia, Nicomachus the Acarnanian—one of those who had fled from Thurium and were now settled in Ambracia—and Theodotus of Pherae, an exile from Thessaly,

who was living in Stratus; on behalf of the Achaeans, Xenophon of Aegium; on behalf of King Attalus, Alexander alone; and on behalf of the Athenian people, Cephisodorus and his colleagues.

## Book 18 Chapter 11: The Greeks Ask for Help Against Philip

These envoys arrived in Rome before the Senate had settled the matter of the consuls appointed for that year—whether it would be necessary to send both to Gaul, or one of the two against Philip.

But once the friends of Titus had made sure that both consuls would remain in Italy because of the fear of the Celts, all the envoys came before the Senate and brought sharp charges against Philip.

Most of their accusations were similar to those they had earlier stated to the king himself;

but they all tried diligently to implant this idea in the Senate: that so long as Chalcis, Corinth, and Demetrias were under the control of the Macedonian, it was impossible for the Greeks to entertain any notion of freedom.

For what Philip himself had said, they declared, was only too true—when he said, rightly observing it, that the aforementioned places were the "fetters of Greece."

For the Peloponnesians could not breathe freely while a royal garrison sat in Corinth, nor could the Locrians, Boeotians, and Phocians take heart while Philip held Chalcis and the rest of Euboea,

nor indeed could the Thessalians or Magnesians ever get a taste of freedom while Philip and the Macedonians held Demetrias.

Therefore Philip's offer to give up the other places was merely for show, a means of escaping the present crisis: for on whatever day he wished, he would easily bring the Greeks back under his power, if he held the aforementioned places.

For this reason they urged the Senate either to compel Philip to withdraw from these cities, or to hold to the policy already adopted and prosecute the war against him vigorously.

For in truth the greatest part of the war had already been accomplished, the Macedonians having already been twice defeated and most of their supplies on land used up.

Having said this, they appealed to the Senate neither to cheat the Greeks of their hopes for freedom nor to rob themselves of the noblest distinction.

So the envoys from the Greeks spoke to this effect and in similar terms; but the envoys from Philip, who had prepared to make their case at greater length, were checked at the very outset.

For when they were asked whether they would give up Chalcis, Corinth, and Demetrias, they replied that they had no instructions concerning these places.

Cut short in this way, they thus ended their speech.

## Book 18 Chapter 12: Greece Assigned to Flamininus

The Senate, then, dispatched both consuls to Gaul, as I said above, and voted that the war against Philip should continue, giving Titus authority over Greek affairs.

When this was quickly made known in Greece, everything had turned out for Titus according to his wishes—chance having contributed in a small way, but the greater part of it all having been managed through his own foresight.

For the aforementioned man was exceedingly shrewd, as much as any other Roman;

indeed, he handled with such skill and good sense not only public undertakings but also private interviews that he left no room for anyone to surpass him.

And yet he was still quite young—he was not more than thirty years old—and he was the first to cross over into Greece with an army.

## Book 18 Chapter 13: Wise Patriots or Traitors?

For my own part, the errors that men commit have often, and on many counts, struck me with wonder—but none so much as those that concern traitors.

I therefore wish to say what the occasion calls for on this subject.

And yet I am well aware that the topic is in some respects hard to examine and hard to define, for it is no easy matter to declare whom we ought truly to consider a traitor.

Clearly we should not at once count as traitors those men who, in a time of calm, enter into a partnership of affairs with certain kings or princes;

nor, again, those who under the pressure of circumstances shift their own native cities away from existing arrangements toward other friendships and alliances—not these either.

Far from it: for men of this kind have often been the authors of the greatest benefits to their own countries.

And so as not to fetch our examples from far off, what I am saying can readily be grasped from present events themselves.

For had Aristaenus not at that moment, and opportunely, swung the Achaeans away from their alliance with Philip toward that of the Romans, the nation would plainly have been utterly and entirely destroyed.

As it is, quite apart from the safety that each man gained at that very crisis, this man and that policy of his were by common consent held to be the cause of the Achaeans' growth as well;

and for this reason all honored him not as a traitor but as a benefactor and savior of the country.

The same account would hold for all the others too who, amid the pressures of the times, pursue a policy and conduct very like theirs.

## Book 18 Chapter 14: Comparison with Philip II and Demosthenes

On this very ground one might, while praising Demosthenes on many counts, find fault with him in this:

that he flung a most bitter reproach, carelessly and without proper judgment, at the most distinguished of the Greeks.

For he declared that in Arcadia the circle of Cercidas, Hieronymus, and Eucampidas had become traitors to Greece, because they were allied with Philip; and in Messene the sons of Philiades, Neon and Thrasylochus; and in Argos the circle of Myrtis, Teledamus, and Mnaseas;

and likewise in Thessaly the circle of Daochus and Cineas, and among the Boeotians the circle of Theogeiton and Timolas.

Together with these he reckons up still more besides, naming them city by city—and yet all the men just mentioned had much to say, and that plausibly, in defense of what was right for their own situation, and most of all those of Arcadia and Messene.

For it was they who, by drawing Philip into the Peloponnese and humbling the Lacedaemonians, first of all enabled all the inhabitants of the Peloponnese to breathe again and to conceive the idea of freedom;

and then, by recovering the territory and the cities that the Lacedaemonians had seized in their hour of opportunity from the Messenians, the Megalopolitans, the Tegeans, and the Argives, they unquestionably increased the power of their own native lands.

In return for this they were bound not to make war on Philip and the Macedonians, but to do everything in their power that tended to his glory and honor.

Now if they had been acting in this way while also admitting a garrison from Philip into their countries, or while abolishing the laws and stripping their fellow-citizens of freedom and free speech for the sake of their own greed or domination, they would have deserved that title.

But if, while preserving their obligations to their own countries, they merely differed in their judgment of affairs, holding that the same thing was not in the interest of the Athenians and of their own cities, then surely there was no reason for Demosthenes to call them traitors on that account.

The man who measures everything against the interest of his own particular country, and who thinks that all the Greeks ought to keep their eyes fixed on the Athenians—and, failing that, brands them as traitors—seems to me to be ignorant and to be straying far wide of the truth [in what he has done].

And this is all the more so since the events that then befell the Greeks bore witness not that Demosthenes had taken good forethought for the future, but rather Eucampidas, Hieronymus, Cercidas, and the sons of Philiades.

For the upshot of the Athenians' resistance to Philip turned out to be that they tasted the greatest of calamities when they stumbled in the battle at Chaeronea;

and had it not been for the king's magnanimity and love of glory, their misfortunes would have gone even further, thanks to the policy of Demosthenes.

But thanks to the men named above, security and ease from the Lacedaemonians were secured for the Arcadians and Messenians in common, while for their own native cities in particular many advantages followed besides.

## Book 18 Chapter 15: The Fate of Traitors

To whom, then, might one reasonably apply this name? That is indeed hard to define.

One would come closest to the truth by directing it at those men who, in moments of complete crisis, hand their cities over to the enemy—whether for the sake of their own safety and advantage, or out of a quarrel with their political opponents;

or, by Zeus, again at all those who, by admitting a garrison and making use of outside support to serve their own ambitions and designs, place their countries under the power of stronger men.

All such men one might with fair measure rank under the name of treason.

To them nothing truly profitable or honorable has ever followed—quite the contrary, by everyone's admission.

And here one may well marvel, returning to the point I made at the outset: with what end in view, or by what reasonings, do they rush toward such a calamity?

For no one who has betrayed a city, an army, or a fortress has ever yet gone undetected; and even if at the very moment of the act a man escapes notice, the time that follows has made them all plain to see.

Nor, once found out, has any of them ever led a blessed life; rather, as a rule, they meet the punishment they deserve at the hands of the very men they sought to gratify.

For generals and princes often make use of traitors out of self-interest; but once they have squeezed everything from them, they then treat them as traitors, just as Demosthenes says—

and very reasonably, reckoning that a man who has put his country and his original friends into the hands of their enemies could never become loyal to themselves nor keep faith with them.

And yet, even if such men escape the hands of these, they do not easily escape the vengeance of those whom they have betrayed in violation of treaty.

But if at some point they slip past the designs of both alike, still the reputation they bear among the rest of mankind follows them as an avenger throughout their whole life, conjuring up many fears, false and true alike, both by night and by day, and abetting and pointing the way for all who are plotting some harm against them;

and in the end it does not even let them, in their sleep, find forgetfulness of their crimes, but forces them to dream of every kind of plot and reversal, since they are conscious of the estrangement they have earned from everyone and of the common hatred they bear.

Yet, for all that things stand thus, no one who ever needed a traitor was at a loss for one—save in the very rarest of cases.

From which one might reasonably say that the human race, reputed the most cunning of animals, gives much ground for being held the basest of them.

For the other animals, slaves to their bodily appetites alone, come to grief through these alone; but the human race, for all its endowment of reason, errs no less through its failure to reason than through its nature.

But let this much suffice on the subject. —

## Book 18 Chapter 16: Attalus in Sicyon

King Attalus had even before this been held in exceptional honor by the city of the Sicyonians, ever since he redeemed for them the sacred land of Apollo at no small cost in money,

in return for which they set up his colossal statue, ten cubits high, beside the Apollo in the marketplace.

On this occasion, when he again gave them ten talents and ten thousand medimni of wheat, their goodwill was intensified many times over, and they voted him a golden statue and enacted a law to offer sacrifice in his honor each year.

And so Attalus, having received these honors, set sail for Cenchreae. —

## Book 18 Chapter 17: Attalus and the Boeotians

The tyrant Nabis left Timocrates of Pellene in charge of the city of the Argives—since he trusted him above all others and employed him for his most conspicuous affairs—and returned to Sparta. A few days later he sent off his wife,

giving her instructions that, once she reached Argos, she was to set about raising money.

On her arrival she far outdid Nabis himself in brutality.

For she summoned the women, some individually, some by families, and inflicted on them every kind of torment and violence,

until she had stripped from nearly all of them not only their gold jewelry but also their most costly clothing. —

At greater length Attalus, in his speech,

reminded them of the valor their ancestors had shown of old. —

## Book 18 Chapter 18: Roman and Greek Palisading

Titus, unable to determine where the enemy were encamped but knowing for certain that they had entered Thessaly, ordered all his men to cut stakes to carry along with them, ready for whatever the moment might require.

To the Greeks this seems impossible to do, but for the Romans it is easy.

For the Greeks can barely manage even their own sarissas on the march and scarcely endure the fatigue they cause,

whereas the Romans, having slung their shields from their shoulders by leather straps and carrying only their javelins in their hands, can take on the additional task of transporting the stakes.

There is also a great difference in the stakes themselves.

The Greeks reckon the best stake to be one that has the most numerous and largest shoots growing all around the stem,

while among the Romans the stakes have two or three prongs, or at most four—and they pick those that have these on one side only, not running off in every direction.

As a result, carrying them becomes thoroughly easy—for a single man carries three or four stacked one upon another—

and their use in service is exceptionally secure. For the Greek stake, when set in front of the camp,

is in the first place easy to tear out: since the part that grips and is held fast by the ground is a single one, while the shoots branching from it are many and large, two or three men can take hold of the same stake by its shoots and pull it up easily.

Once that happens, a gateway is at once created, because of the stake's size, and the stakes beside it are loosened too, since in such a palisade the interlacing and overlapping of the stakes with one another is slight.

With the Romans the opposite occurs. For they set the stakes interwoven with one another from the start, so closely that it is not easy to tell which prongs belong to which of the stems buried in the ground, nor which shoots belong to which prongs.

Consequently it is impossible to slip a hand through and grasp one, since the prongs are dense and pressed against each other, and moreover have been carefully sharpened;

nor, even when grasped, is it easy to pull out. For in the first place nearly every stake has the full self-supporting strength its grip on the ground gives it,

and in the second place, the man dragging on a single prong is forced—because of the interlacing—to lift many others along with it as they yield to him; while for two or three men to get hold of the same stake is altogether unlikely.

And even if at some point a man does prevail and pull out one or another, the gap left is imperceptible.

Therefore, since there is a great advantage in the fact that such a stake is both readily found, easily transported, and secure and durable in use,

it is plain that if any of the other military practices of the Romans deserves emulation and imitation, this one does too—at least in my judgment.

## Book 18 Chapter 19: Flamininus and Philip Nearing Each Other

In any case, Titus, having made these preparations for whatever the moment might require, advanced with his whole army at a walking pace, and when he was about fifty stades from the city of the Pheraeans he pitched camp on the spot.

On the following day, just before dawn, he sent out scouts to reconnoiter and search for any clue by which they might learn where the enemy were and what they were doing.

Philip, at the same time, learning that the Romans were encamped near Thebes, broke camp from Larisa and advanced with his whole army, making his march in the direction of Pherae.

When he was about thirty stades away, he encamped on the spot for the time being and gave orders, while it was still early, for everyone to see to the care of their bodies;

then, just before dawn, he roused his force and sent ahead those who were accustomed to march in front of the army, with orders to cross the ridges above Pherae, while he himself, as day was breaking, moved the army out of the palisaded camp.

The advance parties of both armies came within a little of colliding with one another near the passes;

for, catching sight of each other in the gloom from a very short distance, they halted, and both quickly sent word, reporting to their commanders what had happened and asking what they ought to do.

*** [The orders were] to fall back to the camps below and to recall those advance parties as well.

The next day both sides sent out, for reconnaissance, about three hundred each of their cavalry and light troops; among them Titus also dispatched two squadrons of Aetolians, because of their familiarity with the terrain.

These met one another on the near side of Pherae, toward Larisa, and engaged fiercely.

As Eupolemus the Aetolian and his men fought vigorously and kept calling on the Italians to join the action, the Macedonians began to be hard pressed.

For the time being, after skirmishing for a long while, they separated and withdrew to their respective camps.

## Book 18 Chapter 20: Both Sides Advance on Scotusa

On the following day, both sides, dissatisfied with the country around Pherae because it was overgrown and full of stone walls and garden plots, broke camp.

Philip directed his march toward Scotusa, intending to provision himself from that city and afterward, once he was fully equipped, to take up positions suited to his forces;

but Titus, suspecting what was coming, moved his army at the same time as Philip, hurrying to destroy the grain in the territory of Scotusa first.

Since high ridges lay between the two lines of march, neither could the Romans see where the Macedonians were heading, nor the Macedonians the Romans.

So both sides, having completed that day's march—Titus to the place called Eretria in the region of Phthiotis, and Philip to the river Onchestus—made camp where they were, each unaware of the other's encampment.

On the next day they advanced and encamped, Philip at the place called Melambium in the territory of Scotusa, and Titus near the Thetideum in the territory of Pharsalus, still unaware of each other.

Then a storm of rain and tremendous thunder came on, and it happened that the whole mass of air from the clouds settled down upon the earth the next day, just before dawn, so that on account of the darkness that fell over everything men could not even see what was at their feet.

Nevertheless, Philip, eager to carry out his purpose, broke camp and advanced with his whole army.

But being hampered on the march by the fog, after covering a short distance he led his force into a palisaded camp and sent off his rearguard, with orders to seize the summits of the hills lying in between.

## Book 18 Chapter 21: The Macedonians Send for Help

Titus, encamped near the temple of Thetis and at a loss as to where exactly the enemy might be, sent out ten squadrons of cavalry along with about a thousand light-armed troops, urging them to advance and scout the countryside with caution.

As these men pressed forward toward the ridges, they blundered without realizing it into the Macedonian outpost, because the daylight was too dim to see clearly.

At first both sides were thrown into confusion for a short while, but soon they began to test one another, and each party also sent messengers back to their own commanders to report what had happened.

When, in the course of the clash, the Romans were being worn down and were suffering badly at the hands of the Macedonian outpost, they sent to their own camp to ask for reinforcements.

Titus then gave a brief exhortation to the Aetolians under Archedamus and Eupolemus, as well as to two of his own tribunes, and dispatched them with five hundred cavalry and two thousand infantry.

When these troops joined the men who had been skirmishing from the start, the engagement immediately took on the opposite character.

For the Romans, taking fresh heart from the hope this relief gave them, threw themselves into the fight with twice the vigor,

while the Macedonians, though they defended themselves bravely, were now in their turn hard pressed and overwhelmed on every side. They fell back to the heights and sent to the king for help.

## Book 18 Chapter 22: Skirmishes Before the Main Battle

Philip, who for the reasons already given had never expected a full-scale engagement to occur that day, happened to have sent a large part of his men out of camp to forage.

But when he learned from the messengers what was happening, and as the mist was now beginning to clear, he gave his orders and sent out Heracleides of Gyrton, who commanded the Thessalian cavalry, and Leon, the commander of the Macedonian horse, and with them Athenagoras at the head of all the mercenaries except the Thracians.

When these forces joined the men at the outposts, and the Macedonians were thus reinforced by a powerful body, they bore down hard on the enemy and once again drove the Romans back from the heights, reversing the situation.

What chiefly prevented them from routing the enemy outright was the determined spirit of the Aetolian cavalry, who fought with great fury and reckless daring.

For the Aetolians, to the same degree that they fall short in their infantry—both in equipment and in formation for full-scale battles—surpass the other Greeks in cavalry when it comes to fighting in detached actions and individual combat.

And so on this occasion, since these men checked the enemy's onset, the Romans were no longer driven all the way back to the level ground, but after withdrawing only a short distance they wheeled about and stood their ground.

When Titus saw that not only his light infantry and cavalry had given way, but that because of them his whole force was becoming unsettled, he led out his entire army and drew it up close to the hills.

At that same moment, one Macedonian after another came running from the outpost to Philip, shouting: "King, the enemy are fleeing! Do not let the chance slip by. The barbarians cannot stand against us. This is your day; this is your moment!" So the result was that Philip,

although he was not pleased with the ground, was nevertheless drawn into the engagement. For the hills just mentioned are called Cynoscephalae; they are rough and broken and rise to a considerable height.

For this reason Philip, foreseeing how unsuitable the terrain was, had at the outset been entirely unwilling to commit to battle there; but now, spurred on by the excessive confidence of those bringing the reports, he gave the order to lead his army out of the camp.

## Book 18 Chapter 23: Flamininus Rouses His Troops

Titus, having drawn up his whole army in succession, at one and the same time supported the men fighting in the vanguard and went along the ranks encouraging his troops. His exhortation was brief,

but it was vivid and well understood by those who heard it. For pointing plainly to the enemy before their eyes, he said to his soldiers:

"Are these not the very Macedonians, men, whom you—when they held the passes into Eordaea in their own Macedonia—drove off from that commanding ground in open battle under Sulpicius, killing many of them?

Are these not the Macedonians whom you, when they held the seemingly hopeless rough country of Epirus, forced out by your own valor and compelled to throw away their weapons and flee, until they had made their way back into Macedonia?

Why then should you feel any fear, when you are about to fight on equal terms against these same men? Why dwell anxiously on what has gone before, instead of taking from those very events the opposite lesson and feeling confident now?

Therefore, men, urge one another on and charge into the fight with all your strength. For if the gods are willing, I am convinced that this present battle will swiftly come to the same end as the engagements that have gone before."

Having spoken thus, he ordered the right wing to remain in position with the elephants in front of it, while with the left wing, supported by the light infantry, he advanced boldly against the enemy.

And the Romans fighting in the vanguard, now reinforced by the supporting infantry legions, wheeled about and bore down on their opponents.

## Book 18 Chapter 24: The Battle of Cynoscephalae

Meanwhile Philip, when he saw that the greater part of his own army had now taken up position in front of the camp, personally took the peltasts and the right wing of the phalanx and advanced, making a swift climb up the hills.

He gave instructions to Nicanor, nicknamed "the Elephant," to see that the remaining part of the army followed close behind, step by step.

As soon as his leading files reached the crest of the ridge, he at once deployed them into line by the left and seized the high ground in advance. For since the Macedonians who had been fighting in the vanguard had pushed the Romans well down the far slopes of the hills, he found the heights unoccupied.

But while he was still bringing the right wing of his army into line, the mercenaries came back, hard pressed and overpowered by the enemy.

For when the heavy-armed troops joined the Roman light infantry—as I said just now—and supported them in the fighting, the Romans, gaining as it were a decisive counterweight in this assistance, pressed heavily on the enemy and killed many of them.

The king, at the start when he was first arriving, was overjoyed when he saw the light infantry engagement taking place not far from the enemy's camp.

But when he saw the tide turn and his own men giving way and in need of help, he was forced to come to their aid and to stake everything on the moment, even though the greater part of his phalanx was still on the march and only now climbing the hills.

Receiving the men who had been engaged, he gathered them all onto the right wing, both infantry and cavalry, and ordered the peltasts and the phalangites to double their depth and close up their ranks toward the right. When this had been done,

and as the enemy were now coming to close quarters, the order was given to the phalangites to lower their pikes and advance, and to the light infantry to extend the flank.

At the same moment Titus, after receiving his vanguard troops into the gaps between the maniples, launched his attack on the enemy.

## Book 18 Chapter 25: The Battle

When the two sides collided with great force and an overwhelming roar—both armies raising their war cry together, while those outside the fighting shouted encouragement to the combatants—the scene was terrifying and charged with the tension of struggle.

Philip's right wing was faring brilliantly in the engagement, since it was making its charge from higher ground, was superior in the sheer weight of its formation, and held a great advantage in the nature of its equipment, which suited the present circumstances.

But as for the rest of his forces, the part next to those already engaged was still at a distance from the enemy, while the part on the left wing, having only just completed the climb over the heights, was just coming into view on the ridges.

Titus, seeing that his own men could not withstand the charge of the phalanx but were being crushed on the left wing—some already dead, the rest slowly giving ground and retreating—and that what hopes of survival remained lay with his right,

quickly rode over to that wing. Observing that of the enemy's force one part was locked in close combat with those fighting, another was at that very moment descending from the heights, and another was still posted up on the heights, he put the elephants in front and led the standards against the enemy.

The Macedonians, having no one to give them orders and unable to close up and assume the phalanx's proper formation—both because of the difficulty of the terrain and because, in following those already engaged, they were in the disposition of a marching column rather than a battle line—

did not even let the Romans come to close quarters with them, but, panicked by the elephants alone and torn apart, they gave way.

## Book 18 Chapter 26: Philip's Defeat and Flight

Most of the Romans, then, pursued these men and cut them down.

But one of the military tribunes who were with them, having no more than twenty maniples, made up his own mind at the critical moment what needed to be done, and so contributed greatly to the overall victory.

Seeing that Philip's division had pushed far ahead of the rest and was bearing down with its weight upon the Roman left, he left the men on the right, who were by now plainly winning, wheeled around against those still fighting, came up behind, and charged the Macedonians in the rear.

Since it is impossible for phalanx troops to wheel about and defend themselves man by man, he pressed hard, killing those in his path, who were unable to help themselves,

until the Macedonians were forced to throw down their arms and flee—for the troops they had been facing, who had been giving ground, now turned about and joined in the attack on them. Philip, in the early stages,

as I have said, judging from the success of his own part of the field, was convinced he was winning a complete victory.

But when he suddenly saw the Macedonians flinging away their arms and the enemy attacking their rear, he withdrew a short way from the danger with a few cavalry and infantry and surveyed the whole situation.

Realizing that the Romans, in their pursuit of his left wing, were already drawing close to the heights, he turned to flight, gathering on the spur of the moment as many Thracians and Macedonians as he could.

Titus, following the fugitives and coming upon the left-wing units of the Macedonians just as they were reaching the heights in the passes, at first halted, since the enemy held their pikes upright—

which is the custom of the Macedonians whenever they surrender or go over to the other side.

Afterward, on learning the reason for what was happening, he held back his men, deciding to spare those who had lost heart.

But while Titus was still considering this, some of the men in his vanguard fell upon them from higher ground, laid hands on them, and killed most of them; only a few escaped by throwing away their arms.

## Book 18 Chapter 27: Philip Retreats, the Romans Plunder

With the battle now brought to an end on every part of the field and the Romans victorious, Philip began his retreat toward Tempe.

The first night he camped near the so-called Tower of Alexander; the next day he advanced to Gonni, at the entrance of the pass into Tempe, and there waited, wishing to take in those who came through safe from the rout.

The Romans, after pursuing the fugitives a certain distance, turned back: some set about stripping the dead, others gathering up the prisoners, but the majority rushed off to plunder the enemy's camp.

There they found that the Aetolians had got there first, and, thinking they had been robbed of the spoils that were rightly theirs, they began to find fault with the Aetolians and to complain to their general that he assigned the dangers to them but had handed the spoils over to others.

For the moment they returned to their own encampment and bivouacked there. The next day they both collected the prisoners and what was left of the spoils and marched on toward Larisa.

The Romans lost about seven hundred men; of the Macedonians, all told some eight thousand died, and no fewer than five thousand were taken alive.

Such was the outcome of the battle fought in Thessaly at Cynoscephalae between the Romans and Philip.

## Book 18 Chapter 28: The Macedonian Phalanx

In my sixth book I left standing a promise that, when a suitable occasion arose, I would draw a comparison between the equipment of the Romans and the Macedonians and likewise between the tactical systems of each, showing in what respects they are worse and in what respects better than one another. Now, on the basis of the actual events, I will try to carry that promise through to completion.

For since in earlier times the Macedonian system, proving itself through its very deeds, prevailed over the systems of Asia and of Greece, while that of the Romans prevailed over all the nations of Africa and of western Europe,

and since in our own times there has been not just one but repeated trials of strength between these formations and these men against one another,

it would be both useful and worthwhile to investigate the difference, and to ask why it is that the Romans win and carry off the prize in the contests of war,

so that we do not, like the foolish among men, merely speak of fortune and congratulate the victors without reason, but, knowing the true causes, may give the Roman commanders the praise and admiration they rightly deserve.

As for the contests the Romans fought against Hannibal and the defeats they suffered in them, there is no need to say more: it was not because of their equipment, nor their tactical system, but because of Hannibal's resourcefulness and shrewdness that they met with those reverses.

This we made clear in our account of the battles themselves.

Our argument is supported, first, by the conclusion of the war: for once the Romans got a general whose ability matched Hannibal's, victory soon followed upon what has just been said.

And second, Hannibal himself, rejecting the equipment his men had had from the start, immediately after his victory in the first battle armed his own forces with Roman weapons, and continued to use these for the rest of the time.

Pyrrhus, too, made use not only of Italian arms but also of Italian troops, placing a maniple and a company of his phalanx alternately in his battles against the Romans.

Yet even so he could not win; somehow the outcomes of his engagements always turned out doubtful for them.

It was necessary to say these things first, so that nothing should appear to contradict my conclusions. Now I return to the comparison before me.

## Book 18 Chapter 29: A Well-Formed Phalanx is Irresistible

It is easy to gather from many considerations that, so long as the phalanx keeps its peculiar character and full strength, nothing can stand against it face to face or hold its ground against its charge.

For each man, with his weapons, occupies a space of three feet when the ranks are closed up for battle, and the length of the sarissa, by its original design, is sixteen cubits—though in its actual, practical adjustment, fourteen.

Of these fourteen, four are taken up by the gap between the soldier's two hands and by the counterweight of the shaft behind, which balances the part thrust forward.

It is clear, then, that the sarissa must project ten cubits in front of the body of each hoplite whenever he advances against the enemy with the weapon leveled in both hands.

The result is that the sarissae of the second, third, and fourth ranks reach farther forward than those of the fifth, but even the fifth rank's sarissae project two cubits beyond the men in the front rank—provided the phalanx keeps its proper character and density, closed up both from front to rear and side to side,

as Homer suggests in these lines:

Shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man;
the horsehair crests on their bright ridges touched
as the men bent forward—so closely did they stand, packed against one another.

And if this is described truly and rightly,

it is clear that in front of each man in the front rank five sarissae must project, each differing from the next by two cubits in length.

## Book 18 Chapter 30: Roman Soldiers in More Open Order

From this it is easy to picture the charge and the leveled line of the whole phalanx—what it is likely to look like and how much force it has—formed sixteen deep.

Of these ranks, all those beyond the fifth cannot bring their sarissae to bear in the fighting. For this reason they do not level them man by man for the thrust,

but hold them tilted upward over the shoulders of the men in front of them, in order to protect the upper part of the formation; for, by the dense mass of their sarissae, they ward off any missiles that, flying over the front ranks, might fall upon the men behind.

These rear men, moreover, by the sheer weight of their bodies pressing forward during the advance, push against those ahead of them. This makes the charge irresistible and makes it impossible for the front ranks to turn back.

Such being the arrangement of the phalanx, both as a whole and in its parts, we must now describe, by way of comparison, the special features and differences of the Roman equipment and of their whole order of battle.

The Romans, too, stand with their weapons in a space of three feet.

But since in their style of fighting each man moves individually—covering his body with the long shield, which he shifts constantly to meet the point from which a blow is coming, and fighting with the sword by cutting and thrusting—

it is obvious that the men need a loose, open interval of at least three feet from one another, both front to rear and side to side, if they are to be effective for what is needed.

The result is that one Roman stands opposite two of the phalangites in the front rank, so that he faces and fights against ten sarissae.

Once the lines have closed to hand-to-hand fighting, it is impossible for one man to cut through all these in time, nor is it easy to force a way past them—especially since the rear ranks of the Romans can contribute nothing to the men in front, neither for added force nor for the work of their swords.

From all this it is easy to see that, as I said at the start, nothing can withstand the charge of the phalanx face to face so long as it keeps its peculiar character and strength.

## Book 18 Chapter 31: Cumbrous Nature of the Phalanx

What, then, is the cause of the Romans' victories, and what brings ruin on those who rely on the phalanx?

It is that in war the times and places for action are not fixed, whereas for the phalanx there is only one suitable time and one kind of ground on which it can do its work.

Now if there were some necessity compelling the enemy to come down to the time and place that suit the phalanx whenever a decisive engagement was at hand, it would be reasonable—by the argument just given—to expect that those who use the phalanx would always win the day.

But if the enemy can avoid this, and do so easily, how can the formation just described remain anything to fear?

Moreover, it is agreed that the phalanx needs ground that is level and clear, with no obstacles upon it—I mean such things as ditches, gullies, ravines, ridges, and riverbeds.

For all the things just mentioned are enough to break up and dissolve such a formation.

And that it is practically impossible—or, failing that, altogether rare—to find ground extending sometimes twenty stades, sometimes even more, with no such obstacle, everyone would also admit.

But suppose such ground has been found. If the enemy will not come down into it, but instead march around plundering the cities and territory of the allies, what use is a formation of this kind?

For if it stays on the ground suited to itself, it cannot help its friends—indeed, it cannot even save itself.

The convoys of its supplies will easily be cut off by the enemy, who hold the open country unopposed;

while if it leaves its own ground in order to take some action, it will be easy prey for the enemy.

And further, even if a general does come down into level ground, but does not commit his whole army at once to a single charge of the phalanx and one decisive moment, but holds back from battle even briefly at the very moment of the engagement, it is easy to foresee what will follow from what

## Book 18 Chapter 32: How the Romans Fight Against a Phalanx

the Romans now do. For there is no longer any need to judge what I am saying by argument; it can be judged from what has already happened.

The Romans do not make their line equal in length to the enemy's and then engage all their forces at once, front to front, against the phalanxes. Instead, part of their forces is held in reserve, while part closes with the enemy.

Then, whether the phalangites press back the men attacking them, or are themselves pressed back, in either case the phalanx's peculiar order is broken.

For whether pursuing those who give way or fleeing from those who press on, they leave behind the remaining parts of their own army;

and once this happens, the enemy's reserves are given the gap and the very ground that the phalangites had been holding. They no longer need to attack from the front, but can fall in upon the phalangites' flank and rear.

When it is easy to guard against the phalanx's strong points and advantages, but impossible to guard against its weaknesses, how can the difference between the two systems fail to be great in actual practice?

Furthermore, those who use the phalanx must march through country of every kind and pitch camp; they must seize favorable positions in advance, besiege others and be besieged themselves, and meet with unexpected appearances of the enemy.

For all these are parts of war, and they tip the scales toward victory—sometimes wholly, sometimes greatly.

In all of these the Macedonian system is awkward, and at times useless, because the phalangite can be of service neither in a unit nor as an individual. The Roman system, by contrast, is highly serviceable.

For every Roman, once he has armed himself and set out for action, is equally adapted to any place, any time, and any emergency.

He is, moreover, ready and in the same fit condition whether he has to fight together with the whole army, or with part of it, or in a single maniple, or even by himself.

Therefore, since the Roman system is so far superior in usefulness down to the smallest part, its outcomes too follow the Romans' intentions far more reliably than those of others.

I thought it necessary to discuss this matter at some length, both because at the very time it happened many of the Greeks, when the Macedonians were defeated, regarded the event as beyond belief, and because afterward many would be at a loss to understand why and how the phalanx formation is inferior to the Roman style of arming.

## Book 18 Chapter 33: Philip's Conduct After the Battle

Philip, having done everything he could in the battle but having been utterly defeated, took up as many of the survivors from the fighting as he could and set off himself through the Vale of Tempe into Macedonia.

On the night before his departure, however, he sent one of his bodyguards to Larisa with orders to destroy and burn the royal correspondence. In this he acted as a king should, not forgetting his duty even in the midst of catastrophe.

For he knew well that, if the Romans got hold of these records, they would furnish them with many pretexts against both himself and his friends.

It has perhaps already happened to others, too, that they could not bear their power with human moderation in times of success, yet in times of reversal stood firm with caution and good sense;

but this was true above all in Philip's case. This will become clear from what I am about to relate.

For just as I clearly set out his early impulses toward what was right, and again his change for the worse—when, why, and how it came about—and gave a documented account of his actions during that period,

in the same way I must now make plain his change of heart and the good judgment with which he adapted to the setbacks dealt him by fortune and seems to have made the most prudent use of the circumstances that confronted him.

As for Titus, after the battle he made the appropriate arrangements regarding the prisoners and the rest of the spoils, and then proceeded toward Larisa. —

He was also irritated by their boastfulness, when he saw them claiming the victory as their own and filling Greece with reports of their valor.

## Book 18 Chapter 35: Comparative Incorruptibility of Romans

As for me, had I been speaking of earlier times, I would have confidently made a general pronouncement about all Romans, that they would do no such thing—I mean before they undertook wars overseas, while they still abided by their own customs and principles.

In the present age, however, I would not dare to say this of them all; yet privately I would venture to declare of a good many men at Rome that they are able to keep their integrity in this respect.

By way of evidence, two universally acknowledged names *** so that I may not seem to be claiming the impossible.

Take Lucius Aemilius, the man who defeated Perseus: when he became master of the Macedonian kingdom—where, apart from all its other furnishings and resources, more than six thousand talents of silver and gold were found in the treasuries alone—

so far from coveting any of it, he refused even to set eyes on it and had others handle the matter, even though in his own life he was by no means well supplied with means but rather fell short.

At any rate, when he died not long after the war, his sons by birth, Publius Scipio and Quintus Maximus, wanted to repay his wife her dowry of twenty-five talents; and they were so hard-pressed that they could not have managed it at all had they not sold off the household goods, the slaves, and on top of these some of the property as well.

And if to anyone this account should seem incredible, it is easy to gain assurance on the point:

for although many things are disputed among the Romans, especially on this very matter because of their rivalries with one another, nevertheless anyone who inquires will find that what I have just said is agreed upon by all.

Then again there is Publius Scipio, the son by birth of this Aemilius and the grandson by adoption of Publius called the Great: when he gained possession of Carthage—reckoned the wealthiest city in the world—he took absolutely nothing from it into his private estate, neither by purchase nor by acquiring anything in any other way,

even though he was not at all well off in life but only of modest means, as Romans go.

And not only did he keep clear of the wealth of Carthage itself, but he allowed nothing whatever from Africa to be mingled with his private property.

Once more, in the case of this man too, anyone who genuinely inquires will find his reputation on this point undisputed among the Romans.

## Book 18 Chapter 36: Congress at Tempe Begins

But on these matters I will take a more fitting occasion to enlarge at greater length. Titus, having fixed a day to meet Philip, wrote at once to the allies, making clear when they should appear at the conference; and he himself, after some days, came to the pass of Tempe at the appointed time.

When the allies had gathered and a council had been formed from their own number, the Roman general rose and called on each to state the terms on which peace should be made with Philip.

King Amynandros, then, spoke briefly and moderately and ended his remarks:

he asked that they all take thought for him, so that, once the Romans had withdrawn from Greece, Philip would not vent his anger upon him; for the Athamanians, he said, were always easy prey for the Macedonians, owing both to their weakness and to the nearness of their territory.

After him Alexander the Aetolian rose. As to the fact that Titus had assembled the allies to deliberate about the settlement and was now inviting each to state his view,

he praised Titus; but on the whole matter he said Titus was mistaken and going astray if he was convinced that by coming to terms with Philip he would leave either peace secure for the Romans or freedom secure for the Greeks—for neither of these was possible.

Rather, if he wished to fulfill both his country's purpose and the personal promises he had made to all the Greeks, there was, he said, only one settlement to be made with the Macedonians: to drive Philip from power.

And this would be quite easy to do, provided he did not let the present opportunity slip by.

After arguing at greater length in support of this proposal, he ended his speech.

## Book 18 Chapter 37: Debate In the Congress At Tempe

Titus then took up the argument and said that Alexander was wide of the mark—not only regarding Rome's policy, but also regarding his own intentions, and above all regarding the true interests of the Greeks.

For the Romans, he said, did not utterly destroy those against whom they had first gone to war.

Proof of this claim could be found in their dealings with Hannibal and the Carthaginians: although the Romans had suffered the most terrible things at their hands, and afterward had it fully in their power to do whatever they wished against them, they nonetheless adopted no irreparable measures against the Carthaginians.

And in fact, he himself had never held the view that one must wage war against Philip without any possibility of settlement; on the contrary, had Philip been willing to do what was demanded of him before the battle, Titus would readily have come to terms with him.

He said, therefore, that he was amazed that men who had all taken part at that time in the conferences about a settlement should now be so utterly implacable.

"Or is it obviously because we have won? But that is the most senseless reasoning of all.

For good men ought, while at war, to be formidable and full of fight; when defeated, noble and high-minded; but when victorious, moderate, gentle, and humane. Yet you are now urging the very opposite.

Moreover, it is in the Greeks' own interest that the power of Macedon be greatly humbled, but by no means that it be removed altogether,"

—for they might soon enough come to learn the lawless violence of the Thracians and Gauls; this, he said, had already happened more than once.

In sum, he declared that he and the Romans present judged that, if Philip undertook to do everything the allies had previously demanded, they would grant him peace, subject also to the approval of the Senate; but the Aetolians were free to decide for themselves about their own affairs.

After this Phaeneas wanted to argue that everything done before would be in vain, since Philip, if he slipped through the present crisis, would soon make this the starting point for fresh troubles all over again;

but Titus, on the spot from his seat and heatedly, said: "Stop your nonsense, Phaeneas. For I will manage the settlement in such a way that Philip, even should he wish it, will be unable to wrong the Greeks."

## Book 18 Chapter 38: Philip Comes to the Conference

And so on these terms they parted for the day. On the next day the king arrived, and on the third, when everyone had gathered for the conference, Philip came in and skillfully and shrewdly cut short the hostility of all of them.

For he said that he conceded and would carry out everything that the Romans and the allies had previously demanded, and that on the remaining points he would refer the decision to the Senate.

When he had said this, all the rest fell silent, but Phaeneas of the Aetolians said: "Why then, Philip, do you not restore to us Larisa Cremaste, Pharsalus, Phthiotic Thebes, and Echinus?"

Philip told them to go ahead and take them over, but Titus said they should take none of the others—only Phthiotic Thebes;

for when he had approached the Thebans with his army and called on them to entrust themselves to Rome's protection, they had refused. Therefore now, he said, since they had been subdued in war, he had the authority to deal with them as he saw fit.

Phaeneas and his party were indignant and argued that the cities ought to be theirs: first, because they had now fought alongside the Romans, they should recover the cities that had formerly belonged to their league;

and second, in accordance with their original treaty of alliance, by which, of whatever was captured in war, the movable goods were to be the Romans' and the cities the Aetolians'. To this Titus replied that they were mistaken on both counts.

For the alliance had been dissolved at the time when they made their settlement with Philip and abandoned the Romans; and even if the alliance were still in force,

they were entitled to recover and take over not any cities that had voluntarily put themselves under Rome's protection—as all the cities in Thessaly had now done—but only those that had been taken by storm.

## Book 18 Chapter 39: Peace Terms Agreed On

The rest of the congress was pleased with what Titus said, but the Aetolians heard it with resentment, and there was being born what proved to be the beginning of great evils.

For out of this dispute and this spark there blazed up, not long afterward, both the war against the Aetolians and the war against Antiochus.

The decisive factor in Titus's eagerness to come to terms was that he had learned that Antiochus had set out from Syria with an army, directing his advance toward Europe.

For this reason he was anxious that Philip, fastening on this hope, might decide to hold his cities and drag out the war, and that then, when another consul arrived, the credit for the whole achievement might be deflected onto that man.

So it was conceded to the king, as he requested, that upon obtaining a four-month armistice he should at once give Titus the two hundred talents and his son Demetrius as a hostage, along with some others of his friends, and that concerning the whole settlement he should send envoys to Rome and refer the decision to the Senate.

For the time being they then parted, having given each other pledges about the whole matter, on the condition that, if the terms of the settlement were not ratified, Titus would return to Philip the two hundred talents and the hostages.

After this everyone sent envoys to Rome, some to support and others to oppose the settlement.—

## Book 18 Chapter 40: Foolish Credulity

Why on earth is it that, though we are all deceived by the same means and by the same people, we cannot give up our folly?

For this kind of trickery has already been practiced often by many.

And that it should succeed among others is perhaps not surprising; but it is astonishing that it should succeed against those who are themselves the very source of such knavery.

The cause, however, is that men do not keep ready at hand the saying so well put by Epicharmus:

"Stay sober and remember to distrust: these are the joints of the mind."—

Medion, a city near Aetolia.

Polybius, Book Eighteen.

## Book 18 Chapter 41: Asia: King Attalus I

Polybius says in his eighteenth book: Attalus came to the end of his life. Concerning him it is right—just as we are accustomed to do in the case of others—now to add a fitting word as well.

For from the start he possessed no other external advantage toward kingship than wealth alone—

which, when managed with intelligence and daring, truly provides great usefulness toward every undertaking, but, without the qualities just mentioned, by its nature becomes for most people a cause of evils and, in a word, of ruin.

For it breeds both envy and conspiracies, and it carries the greatest tendencies toward the corruption of body and soul. Very few indeed are the souls that are able by the power of wealth to thrust such things away.

Therefore the greatness of spirit of the man we are speaking of is worthy of admiration, because he set out to use his resources for nothing else but the winning of a kingdom, than which nothing greater or nobler can even be named—

a man who laid the foundation of the design just mentioned not only through generosity and goodwill toward his friends, but also through his achievements in war.

For after defeating the Gauls in battle—the most formidable and most warlike nation in Asia at that time—he made this his beginning and then for the first time showed himself a king.

Having obtained this honor and lived seventy-two years, of which he reigned forty-four, he lived a most temperate and dignified life toward his wife and children;

he kept faith with all his allies and friends, and he died in the very midst of the noblest of deeds, while struggling for the freedom of the Greeks.

And greatest of all, having left four sons in the prime of life, he so well ordered the affairs of his realm that the kingdom was handed down to his children's children without a single dispute.

## Book 18 Chapter 41a

II. Affairs of Asia

They tried to prevent Antiochus from sailing along the coast, not out of hostility toward him, but because they feared that, if he strengthened Philip, he would become an obstacle to the freedom of the Greeks.

King Antiochus was especially eager to gain Ephesus because of its strategic position: it seemed to give him, by land and by sea alike, a commanding post against Ionia and the cities on the Hellespont, and for the kings of Asia it offered an exceptionally useful defensive base against Europe.

## Book 18 Chapter 42: Italy: Treaty with Philip Confirmed

III. Affairs of Italy

When Claudius Marcellus had taken up the consulship, there came to Rome the envoys from Philip and those from Titus and the allies, to deal with the treaty with Philip.

After much discussion in the Senate, the Senate resolved to confirm the agreement;

but when the proposal was brought before the people, Marcus—himself eager to cross over into Greece—spoke against it and made great efforts to break off the treaty.

Nevertheless, the people, in accordance with the policy of Titus, ratified the settlement.

Once this had been carried out, the Senate immediately appointed ten men of distinction and sent them out, together with Titus, to manage the affairs of Greece and to confirm to the Greeks their freedom.

The envoys from the Achaeans, Damoxenus of Aegium and his colleagues, also made representations in the Senate concerning an alliance;

but since objection was raised for the moment—because the Eleians were disputing with the Achaeans over Triphylia, the Messenians (who were then allies of the Romans) over Asine and Pylus, and the Aetolians over the city of the Heraeans—the deliberation was deferred to the ten commissioners.

Such, then, was the state of affairs in the Senate.

## Book 18 Chapter 43: Greece: Murder of Brachylles

IV. Affairs of Greece

In Greece, after the battle, while Titus was wintering at Elateia, the Boeotians, being eager to bring back the men of theirs who had served in Philip's army, sent an embassy to Titus concerning their safe return.

Wishing to draw the Boeotians toward goodwill for himself—because he was already looking ahead to Antiochus—he readily consented.

When all had quickly been brought back from Macedonia, among them Brachylles, the Boeotians at once made him a boeotarch, and in like manner they honored and advanced the others who were thought to be friends of the house of Macedon no less than before.

They also sent an embassy to Philip to thank him for the return of the young men, thereby spoiling the favor done by Titus.

Observing this, Zeuxippus and Peisistratus and all who were regarded as friends of the Romans were distressed, foreseeing what was to come and fearing for themselves and their families;

for they knew clearly that, if the Romans withdrew from Greece while Philip remained at their flank, constantly lending strength to those who opposed them politically, their position in Boeotian public life would be in no way safe.

Therefore, having come to an understanding among themselves, they sent an embassy to Titus at Elateia.

Meeting with him, they put forward many and varied arguments on this matter, pointing out the popular feeling that was now already directed against them and the ingratitude of the masses.

And at last they ventured to say that, unless they did away with Brachylles and overawed the multitude, there would be no safety for the friends of the Romans once the armies had departed.

When Titus had heard all this, he said that he himself would not take part in this deed, but that he would not prevent those who wished to act;

and in general he told them to speak about these matters with Alexamenus, the general of the Aetolians.

When Zeuxippus and his colleagues complied and held discussions with him, the said Alexamenus was quickly persuaded and, agreeing to what was proposed, assigned three Aetolian and three Italian young men to lay hands on Brachylles.

For there is no witness so fearsome, nor any accuser so formidable, as the conscience that dwells in each man's soul.

## Book 18 Chapter 44: Decree of the Senate on the Peace with Philip

At about this time the ten commissioners arrived from Rome, through whom the affairs of the Greeks were to be managed, bringing the decree of the Senate concerning the peace with Philip.

The main provisions of the decree were these: that all the other Greeks, both those in Asia and those in Europe, should be free and use their own laws;

that those subject to Philip, and the garrisoned cities, Philip should hand over to the Romans before the festival of the Isthmian games;

that he should set free Euromus, Pedasa, Bargylia, and the city of the Iaseans, and likewise Abydos, Thasos, Myrina, and Perinthus, removing his garrisons from them;

that concerning the liberation of the Cians, Titus should write to Prusias in accordance with the decree of the Senate;

that Philip should restore to the Romans, within the same period, all the captives and all the deserters, and likewise the decked warships, except for five vessels and the sixteen-banked ship; and that he should also pay a thousand talents,

half of these at once, and half in installments over ten years.

## Book 18 Chapter 45: The Freedom of Greece

When this decree had been made known throughout Greece, everyone else was confident and overjoyed; only the Aetolians, vexed at not obtaining what they had hoped for, ran the decree down, claiming that it had substance only on paper and not in fact.

And from the very wording of the document they drew certain plausible arguments to unsettle those who would listen to them, such as the following.

They said that there were two distinct provisions in the decree concerning the cities garrisoned by Philip: one ordering Philip to withdraw his garrisons and hand the cities over to the Romans, and the other ordering him to withdraw his garrisons and set the cities free.

Now the cities to be set free, they argued, were specified by name, and these were the ones in Asia; while the cities to be handed over to the Romans were obviously those in Europe—namely Oreus,

Eretria, Chalcis, Demetrias, and Corinth.

From this it should be plain to everyone that the Romans were taking over the "fetters of Greece" from Philip, and that what was happening was a change of masters, not a liberation of the Greeks.

This, then, was harped on incessantly by the Aetolians.

Titus, meanwhile, set out from Elatea with the ten commissioners, put in at Anticyra, and at once crossed over to Corinth; arriving there, he sat in council with them and deliberated about the whole settlement.

But since the Aetolians' slander was spreading and was believed by some, Titus was compelled to make many varied arguments in the council,

showing that, if they really wished to win for themselves the entire credit of the Greeks' goodwill and to be universally trusted as having made their original crossing not for their own advantage but for the freedom of Greece, they would have to evacuate every position and set free all the cities now garrisoned by Philip.

This was the source of the deadlock in the council: with regard to all the other cities the matter had been settled beforehand at Rome and the ten commissioners had explicit instructions from the Senate, but in the case of Chalcis, Corinth, and Demetrias they had been given discretion on account of Antiochus, so that they might keep an eye on circumstances and decide about the cities just mentioned according to their own judgment;

for it was clear that the aforementioned king had long been fixing his attention on affairs in Europe.

Nevertheless, Titus persuaded the council to free Corinth at once and put it into the hands of the Achaeans, in keeping with the original agreements, but he held on to the Acrocorinth, Demetrias, and Chalcis.

## Book 18 Chapter 46: Proclamation At the Isthmian Games

These decisions having been reached, the festival of the Isthmian games came round, and since the most distinguished men from nearly all the world had gathered in anticipation of how things would turn out, much varied talk circulated throughout the whole assembly.

Some claimed it was impossible for the Romans to give up certain places and cities; others determined that they would give up the places that seemed most prominent, but would keep those of lesser appearance that could nonetheless render the same service.

And in their cleverness in arguing against one another, these speakers immediately gave themselves away.

While people were in this state of uncertainty, and the crowd had gathered in the stadium for the contest, the herald came forward and, having called for silence by means of the trumpeter, proclaimed the following announcement:

"The Senate of Rome and Titus Quinctius, proconsul and commander, having defeated King Philip and the Macedonians in war, hereby set free, without garrison, without tribute, and governed by their ancestral laws, the following: the Corinthians, Phocians, Locrians, Euboeans, Phthiotic Achaeans, Magnesians, Thessalians, and Perrhaebians."

At the very first words a tremendous burst of applause arose, so that some did not even hear the proclamation, while others wanted to hear it again.

The greater part of the people, disbelieving and seeming to hear what was said as if in a dream, so unexpected was the event,

every one of them on a fresh impulse shouted for the herald and trumpeter to come out into the middle of the stadium and repeat the announcement—because, as it seems to me, the people wanted not only to hear but also to see the speaker, in their disbelief at what was being proclaimed.

But when the herald came forward into the center and, by means of the trumpeter, hushed the uproar, then announced exactly the same words as before, such an outburst of applause broke out that those hearing of it now could scarcely grasp what it was like.

When at last the applause subsided, no one paid the slightest attention to the athletes any longer; everyone was talking, some to one another and some to themselves, as though they were out of their minds.

Indeed, after the contest, in the excess of their joy, they nearly killed Titus with their thanks:

some wanting to look him in the face and hail him as their savior, some eager to grasp his right hand, and most of them throwing wreaths and ribbons upon him, until they very nearly tore the man to pieces.

And although this show of gratitude seems to have been excessive, one could say with confidence that it actually fell far short of the magnitude of the deed.

For it was a wonderful thing that the Romans, and their leader Titus, adopted this policy, undertaking every expense and every danger for the sake of the freedom of Greece; and it was a great thing too that they brought to bear a force equal to their resolve;

but the greatest thing of all was that nothing in Fortune struck against their undertaking—rather, absolutely everything ran together to one favorable moment, so that by a single proclamation all the Greeks who inhabit Asia and those who inhabit Europe became free, without garrison and without tribute, living under their own laws.

## Book 18 Chapter 47: The Commissioners Make Detailed Arrangements

When the festival was over, the commissioners first dealt with the ambassadors from Antiochus, charging him to keep his hands off those cities in Asia that were autonomous and to make war on none of them, and to withdraw from all those formerly subject to Ptolemy and Philip that he had now taken over.

Along with this they warned him not to cross over into Europe with an army; for no Greek was henceforth to be made war upon by anyone or to be enslaved to anyone.

And in general, they said that some of their own number would come to visit Antiochus.

So Hegesianax and Lysias, having received these replies, returned;

and after them the commissioners summoned all who had come from the nations and cities, and made plain to them the decisions of the council.

As for the Macedonians, they declared the people called the Orestae autonomous, because they had come over to the Roman side during the war, and they freed the Perrhaebians, the Dolopes, and the Magnesians.

To the Thessalians, along with their freedom, they also assigned the Phthiotic Achaeans, with the exception of Phthiotic Thebes and Pharsalus;

for the Aetolians made a great point of their claim to Pharsalus, asserting that it ought to belong to them under the original treaties, and likewise to Leucas.

But the members of the council deferred the decision about these cities back to the Senate, to be settled with the Aetolians, while allowing them to keep the Phocians and Locrians within their league, just as they had held them before.

Corinth, Triphylia, and the city of the Heraeans they gave back to the Achaeans. Oreus, and also the city of the Eretrians, most of them thought should be given to Eumenes;

but when Titus referred the matter to the council, the resolution was not ratified, and so after some time these cities, together with Carystus, were freed by the Senate.

They also gave to Pleuratus Lychnis and Parthus, which were Illyrian towns subject to Philip.

And they granted to Amynander the right to hold whatever strongholds of Philip's he had seized during the war.

## Book 18 Chapter 48: The Commissioners Carry Word Throughout Greece

Having settled these matters, the commissioners divided themselves up.

Publius Lentulus sailed to Bargylia and freed its people; Lucius Stertinius went to Hephaestia and Thasos and the cities along Thrace and did the same;

Publius Villius and Lucius Terentius set off to visit Antiochus, while Gnaeus Cornelius and his colleagues went to King Philip.

Meeting him near Tempe, they discussed with him the other matters about which they had instructions, and advised him to send envoys to Rome to seek an alliance, so that he might not appear to be lying in wait for an opportunity and watching for the arrival of Antiochus.

When the king agreed to what they suggested, they parted from him at once and went to the assembly of the Thermica;

and coming before the people, they urged the Aetolians at some length to remain faithful to their original policy and to preserve their goodwill toward the Romans.

Many came forward, some finding fault with the Romans in mild and measured terms for not having shared the fruits of success in a spirit of partnership, nor kept the original agreements,

while others abused them, declaring that the Romans would never have set foot in Greece, nor defeated Philip, had it not been for the Aetolians themselves.

Gnaeus and his colleagues declined to make a defense against each of these charges, but urged them to send an embassy to Rome, on the grounds that they would obtain full justice from the Senate—which, being persuaded, they did.

And such was the outcome of the war against Philip.

While Antiochus' undertaking was advancing just as he wished, and Antiochus himself was present in Thrace, Lucius Cornelius and his colleagues put in by sea at Selymbria.

These men were envoys dispatched by the Senate to arrange the settlement between Antiochus and [Ptolemy]—

## Book 18 Chapter 51: The King's Reply

finished speaking. The king began his reply by saying that, first of all, he was at a loss to understand on what grounds they were disputing with him over the cities of Asia; for it befitted anyone in the world rather than the Romans to do so.

Secondly, he asked that they not meddle at all in the affairs of Asia, since he, for his part, did not in the slightest interfere with the affairs of Italy.

He had crossed into Europe with his forces, he said, in order to recover the Chersonese and the cities of Thrace; for the rule over these regions belonged to him more rightfully than to anyone else.

For this dominion had originally been that of Lysimachus, but when Seleucus made war on him and prevailed in that war, the whole kingdom of Lysimachus passed to Seleucus as spear-won territory.

Then, during the periods when his own ancestors were distracted by other concerns, first Ptolemy and then Philip had torn these regions away and appropriated them for themselves.

He himself, however, was now not acquiring them by exploiting Philip's difficulties, but recovering them by exercising his own just claims.

As for the people of Lysimacheia, who had been unexpectedly driven from their homes by the Thracians, he was doing the Romans no wrong in bringing them back and resettling them;

for in doing this, he said, his aim was not to lay hands on the Romans, but to prepare a dwelling-place for his son Seleucus.

As for the autonomous cities of Asia, they ought to obtain their freedom not by a command from the Romans, but by his own favor.

And with regard to Ptolemy, he said that he himself would settle matters to that king's satisfaction; for he had resolved to establish with him not merely friendship, but, along with friendship, a tie of kinship as well.

## Book 18 Chapter 52: Antiochus Demands a Rhodian Court

When Lucius and his colleagues took the view that the representatives of the Lampsacenes and the Smyrnaeans ought to be called in and given a hearing, this was done.

Present on behalf of the Lampsacenes were Parmenio and Pythodorus, and on behalf of the Smyrnaeans, Coeranus. As these men spoke their minds freely,

the king grew annoyed at the appearance of having to defend himself before the Romans against those who were contesting matters with him, and, cutting Parmenio short, he said:

"Have done with all this talk; for it is not before the Romans, but before the Rhodians, that I am willing to have our disputed points decided."

And so, on these terms, they broke up the meeting, by no means satisfied with one another.

## Book 18 Chapter 53: Egypt: Fall of Scopas

Many people long for bold and glorious exploits, but few dare actually to lay a hand on them.

And yet Scopas had far finer resources than Cleomenes for taking such a risk and showing such daring.

For Cleomenes was caught in advance and shut up to rely solely on the hopes he could place in his own household servants and friends; yet even so he did not abandon those hopes, but tested them to the utmost of his power, valuing an honorable death above a life lived in disgrace.

Scopas, on the other hand, although he had both a strong arm to help him and the right opportunity—since the king was still a child—was caught while he was still hesitating and deliberating.

For when Aristomenes and his men learned that he was gathering his friends at his own house and holding council with them, they sent some of the royal guards to summon him to the council.

But he was so out of his wits that he neither dared to carry out any of his next steps nor, when summoned by the king, was able to bring himself to obey—which is the most extreme failing of all—

until Aristomenes and his men, recognizing his irrationality, stationed the soldiers and the elephants around the house,

and sent Ptolemy son of Eumenes with a band of young men, ordering him to bring Scopas in—peacefully if he was willing to obey, and if not, by force.

When Ptolemy made his way into the house and announced that the king was summoning Scopas, at first Scopas paid no attention to what was said, but kept staring fixedly at Ptolemy for some time, as if menacing him and marveling at his audacity.

But when Ptolemy stepped up and boldly seized hold of his cloak, then Scopas called on those present to come to his aid.

Since, however, the young men who had come in were many, and someone made clear to him the force surrounding the house outside, he gave way to the circumstances and went along with his friends.

## Book 18 Chapter 54: Scopas and Dicaearchus Punished

As soon as Scopas entered the council chamber, the king first stated the charge against him briefly; after the king came Polycrates, who had just arrived from Cyprus, and last of all Aristomenes.

In all other respects the accusation made by them all was much the same as what has just been described, but to the previous charges there was now added the council he had held with his friends and his refusal to obey when summoned by the king.

On these grounds not only did all the members of the council condemn him, but so did the foreign envoys who were present.

When Aristomenes was about to make his accusation, he brought in many other distinguished men from Greece, as well as the ambassadors sent by the Aetolians to negotiate a settlement, among whom was Dorimachus son of Nicostratus.

When these speeches had been delivered, Scopas in turn tried to offer some pleas in his defense; but since no one paid him any attention, because of the senselessness of his conduct, he was immediately led off to prison along with his friends.

Then, when night came, Aristomenes had Scopas and all his relatives and friends put to death by poison;

but Dicaearchus he subjected to the rack and the scourge, and so made an end of him, exacting from him a penalty that was fitting and shared on behalf of all the Greeks.

For this was the Dicaearchus whom Philip, when he set out to violate his treaties against the Cyclades and the cities on the Hellespont, appointed commander of the whole fleet and director of the entire operation.

Sent out to commit such open impiety, he not only thought he was doing nothing strange, but in the extravagance of his madness supposed that he would strike terror even into the gods as well as into men.

For wherever he anchored his ships he set up two altars, one to Impiety and one to Lawlessness, and on these he sacrificed and to these he bowed down as though they were divine powers.

For this reason it seems to me that he met with the punishment he deserved from both gods and men: having set the course of his own life against nature, fittingly he met an end against nature too at the hands of destiny.

As for the rest of the Aetolians, the king let all those who wished to return to their own country depart with their possessions.

## Book 18 Chapter 55: Anacleteria of Ptolemy Epiphanes

Scopas's love of money had been conspicuous even while he was alive—for in his greed he far surpassed all other men—but after his death it became even more apparent through the quantity of gold and the goods that were found in his possession.

For taking as his accomplice the savagery and the drunkenness of Charimortus, he had utterly plundered the kingdom to its very foundations.

Now once the courtiers had settled the affair of the Aetolians satisfactorily, they at once set about performing the Anacleteria of the king. His age did not yet make this pressing, but they thought that affairs would gain a certain stability and a fresh start toward better progress if the king were regarded as having now come into full power.

So, making their preparations on a magnificent scale, they carried out the proceedings in a manner worthy of the dignity of the kingdom, with Polycrates thought to have contributed the most toward this undertaking.

For the man just mentioned, even in the reign of the king's father, while he was still young, was reckoned second to none at court either in trustworthiness or in achievement, and likewise so under the present king.

For having been entrusted with Cyprus and its revenues at times that were dangerous and unsettled, he not only preserved the island for the boy-king, but also amassed a considerable sum of money, which he had now come bringing to the king, after handing over the governorship of Cyprus to Ptolemy of Megalopolis.

Having for these reasons won great approval and wealth in the years that followed, he afterward, as he advanced in age, drifted completely into licentiousness and a debauched life.

A reputation rather similar to his was inherited in old age by Ptolemy son of Agesarchus as well.

Concerning these men, when we come to the proper occasions, we shall not hesitate to set out plainly the disgraceful things that attended their exercise of power.
