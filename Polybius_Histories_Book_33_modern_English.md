# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXXIII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 33 Chapter 1: War Against Attalus Prevented

Olympiad 156, year 1. Affairs in Italy.

While it was still winter, the senate heard the report of Publius Lentulus and his colleagues on the matter concerning Prusias, since they had only recently arrived from Asia; and it also summoned Athenaeus, the brother of King Attalus.

The case did not require many words. The senate immediately appointed envoys—Gaius Claudius Cento, Lucius Hortensius, and Gaius Arunculeius—and sent them along with Athenaeus, instructing them to prevent Prusias from making war on Attalus.

Envoys from the Achaeans also arrived at Rome on behalf of the detained men, led by Xenon of Aegium and Telecles of Aegira.

After these men had spoken in the senate and the motion had been put before the house, the senators came very close to releasing the accused men.

The man responsible for preventing the release from being carried was Aulus Postumius, who was serving as praetor and presiding over the decision.

For there were three proposals: one urged that the men be released, another urged the opposite, and a third favored granting release but delaying it for the present. Although those who supported release were the most numerous,

Postumius set one proposal aside and put the other two in broad terms, asking who thought the detained men should be released and who thought the opposite.

As a result, those who had favored postponement for the present joined those who said the men should not be released, and they became more numerous than the supporters of release. That was the outcome in this matter.

## Book 33 Chapter 3: Release of Achaeans Refused

When the ambassadors returned from Rome to Achaia and made it clear that matters had come very close to ending in the return of all the detained men,

most people became hopeful and elated, and once again sent Telecles and Anaxidamus of Megalopolis from among themselves. That was the state of affairs in the Peloponnese.

## Book 33 Chapter 4: Character of Aristocrates

Affairs of Rhodes.

Aristocrates, the Rhodian general, had a commanding and impressive appearance.

Because of this, the Rhodians supposed, judging from appearances alone, that they had in him a thoroughly reliable leader and protector for the war.

But they were disappointed in their hopes. For when he came to practical action, like counterfeit coin put into the fire, he proved to be something quite different. And this became clear in his actual deeds.

## Book 33 Chapter 5: Suicide of Archias

They were to offer him five hundred talents if he would surrender Cyprus, and to make it clear that other profitable and honorable rewards would also follow from Demetrius if he rendered this service.

When Ptolemy received notice that Archias was present—

Archias, wishing to betray Cyprus to Demetrius, was detected and brought up for trial; but he hanged himself with one of the ropes from the palace awnings.

For indeed, as the proverb says, empty men make empty calculations under the sway of their desires.

So it was with him: imagining that he would gain five hundred talents, he lost both the money he already had and his life as well.

## Book 33 Chapter 6: The Prienians Refuse to Give Orophernes' Funds to Ariarathes

About this same time the people of Priene also fell into an unexpected disaster.

When Orophernes had seized the throne, they had received from him a deposit of four hundred talents. In the years that followed, Ariarathes, after recovering the kingdom, demanded the money from them.

In my opinion the Prienians took the right stand, saying that while Orophernes was still alive they would hand over the money to no one except the man who had deposited it.

Ariarathes, however, seemed to many people to be going beyond what was proper in claiming a deposit that belonged to someone else.

Even so, up to that point one might perhaps excuse him for making the attempt, on the assumption that the money belonged to the kingdom he had recovered. But to push his anger and ambition still further seemed in no way reasonable.

At the time now in question he sent forces that plundered the territory of the Prienians, with Attalus assisting and provoking him because of his own private quarrel with them.

Many people and many herds were lost, and there were even killings near the city itself. Since the Prienians were unable to defend themselves, they first sent envoys to the Rhodians and afterward appealed to the Romans.

But the Romans paid no attention to what they said. So the Prienians, who had placed great hopes in the size of the sum, found themselves at the mercy of their enemies.

For they returned the deposit to Orophernes, yet because of that same deposit they suffered serious unjust injuries at the hands of King Ariarathes.

## Book 33 Chapter 7: Marseilles Complains about the Ligurians

When Hortensius and Arunculeius had returned from Pergamum and explained both Prusias' contempt for the Senate's instructions

and the further fact that, in violation of his sworn agreements, after shutting them up in Pergamum along with Attalus and his party, he had shown every kind of violence and lawlessness,

the Senate, angered and deeply disturbed by what had happened, appointed from its own body ten envoys, headed by Lucius Anicius, Gaius Fannius, and Quintus Fabius Maximus,

and sent them out at once, instructing them to put an end to the war and compel Prusias to make restitution to Attalus for the wrongs he had committed in the war.

## Book 33 Chapter 8: The Ligurians, Ptolemies, And Prusias

At the same time envoys also arrived from Massalia.

They had long been suffering at the hands of the Ligurians, and at that time they were completely blockaded; in addition, the cities of Antipolis and Nicaea were under siege. So they sent envoys to Rome to explain what was happening and to ask for help.

When these envoys appeared before the Senate, the council decided to send legates who would both see the situation for themselves and also try by negotiation to correct the ignorance of the barbarians.

## Book 33 Chapter 9: The Senate Appoints Commissioners to Stop the War

When the people of Massalia sent envoys to Rome to report that they were being mistreated by the Ligurians, the Romans immediately appointed Flaminius, Popilius Laenas, and Lucius Popius as commissioners.

Sailing with the Massaliots, they put in at the territory of the Oxybii, at the town of Aegitna.

But the Ligurians had heard beforehand that they had come to order them to raise the siege, so they attacked while the rest were still bringing their ships to shore and prevented them from landing.

When they found Flaminius already ashore and his baggage unloaded, they first ordered him to leave their country; when he refused to obey, they began plundering his equipment.

When his attendants and freedmen tried to defend the property and stop them, the Ligurians used force and laid hands on them.

At that moment Flaminius too came to help his own people, and they wounded him, killed two of his servants, and drove the rest back to the ship, so that Flaminius barely escaped danger by cutting loose the stern-ropes and anchors.

He was taken to Massalia and treated there with every care.

When the senate learned what had happened, it immediately sent one of the consuls, Quintus Opimius, with a force to make war on the Oxybii and the Deciatae.

## Book 33 Chapter 10: The Ligurians Resist Roman Intervention

Quintus gathered his forces at the city of Placentia and, marching through the Apennines, came into the territory of the Oxybii.

After pitching camp beside the river Apros, he waited for the enemy, having learned that they were assembling and eager to fight.

Then he brought his army up to Aegitna, where the envoys had suffered this outrage, took the town by storm, reduced the inhabitants to slavery, and sent the leaders responsible for the violence to Rome in chains.

After accomplishing this, he advanced to meet the enemy.

The Oxybii, thinking that their crime against the envoys was beyond pardon, were seized by a kind of irrational fury and reckless impulse; before the Deciatae could join them, they gathered about four thousand men and charged the Romans.

Quintus, seeing their rush and the boldness of the barbarians, was struck by their recklessness; but since he saw that the enemy were acting without judgment, he was confident, being both experienced in war and exceptionally sharp by nature.

So he drew out his army, spoke the words suited to the moment, and advanced at a steady walk against the enemy.

Launching a vigorous attack, he quickly defeated those drawn up against him, killed many of them, and forced the rest into headlong flight.

Meanwhile the Deciatae had assembled and arrived, intending to share the same dangers as the Oxybii.

But because they came too late for the battle, they took in the fugitives and shortly afterward attacked the Romans with great dash and eagerness.

After they too were defeated in battle, they all at once surrendered themselves and their city to Roman authority.

Having thus gained control of these tribes, Quintus immediately assigned as much of their land as he could to the Massaliots, and for the future he compelled the Ligurians to give hostages to the Massaliots at fixed intervals.

He then disarmed those who had opposed him and, distributing his army among their towns, established winter quarters there.

So this war had both a swift beginning and a swift end.

## Book 33 Chapter 11: Ligurian War

At the very time when the senate sent Opimius against the war with the Oxybii, Ptolemy the Younger arrived in Rome.

He appeared before the senate and accused his brother, charging him with responsibility for the plot against his life.

At the same time he displayed the scars of his wounds before their eyes, and by adding the rest of his tale in a manner to match them, he appealed to the pity of his audience.

Envoys also arrived from the elder brother, headed by Neolaidas and Andromachus, to answer the accusations made by the younger Ptolemy.

But the senate, already prejudiced by the slanders of the younger brother, was not even willing to listen to their defense; instead, it ordered them to leave Rome at once.

For the younger Ptolemy it appointed five envoys, including Gnaeus Merula and Lucius Thermus, and gave each envoy a quinquereme; these men were instructed to restore Ptolemy to Cyprus.

And to the allies in Greece and Asia they wrote that they were permitted to assist Ptolemy in his return.

## Book 33 Chapter 12: Roman Commissioners Visit Attalus and Prusias

II. Affairs in Pergamum.

In Asia, Attalus was still, throughout the winter, gathering large forces, since Ariarathes and Mithridates had sent him cavalry and infantry in accordance with their alliance, under the command of Demetrius son of Ariarathes. While he was engaged in these preparations,

the ten envoys arrived from Rome, met him near Cadi, discussed the situation with him, and then set out for Prusias.

When they met Prusias, they conveyed the senate's instructions to him in very severe terms.

Prusias accepted some of the orders, but objected to most of them.

So the Romans took offense at him, renounced both friendship and alliance with him, and all left him and went back again to Attalus.

Prusias then changed his mind and followed after them for some distance, pleading earnestly; but when he accomplished nothing, he withdrew in helpless uncertainty.

The Romans instructed Attalus to take up position on the frontier of his own territory with his forces, not to begin the war himself, but to secure the safety of his cities and villages.

They then divided themselves into groups: some sailed off in haste to report Prusias' disobedience to the senate, some departed for Ionia, and others for the Hellespont and the regions around Byzantium, all acting with one and the same purpose,

namely, to detach people from friendship and alliance with Prusias, and to win them over to Attalus and support him as far as possible.

## Book 33 Chapter 13: Roman Envoys Make Peace Between Prusias and Attalus

At the same time Athenaeus sailed in with eighty decked warships.

Five of these were Rhodian quadriremes that had been sent for the Cretan war, twenty belonged to Cyzicus, twenty-seven were with Attalus, and the rest came from the other allies.

He sailed to the Hellespont and, after making contact with the cities aligned with Prusias, repeatedly put troops ashore and devastated the countryside.

When the senate had heard the envoys who had returned from Prusias, it sent out three more commissioners: Appius Claudius, Lucius Oppius, and Aulus Postumius.

When these men reached Asia, they brought the war to an end by inducing both kings to agree to the following terms:

Prusias was to hand over twenty decked ships to Attalus at once and to pay five hundred talents over twenty years.

Both kings were to keep the territory each had held before entering the war.

Prusias was also to make good the damage done in the territory of the Methymnaeans, Aegaeans, Cymaeans, and Heracleots, by paying one hundred talents to the communities just named.

After the treaty had been written out on these terms, the forces under Attalus, both fleet and army, withdrew home.

Such was the course taken by events in the dispute between Attalus and Prusias.

## Book 33 Chapter 14: Another Embassy from Achaia

C. Olympiad 156, year 3. Affairs in Italy.

At this time in Rome, when envoys from Achaia entered the senate on behalf of the detained men, the senate decided to remain with its existing policy.

## Book 33 Chapter 15: War Between Rhodes and Crete

Heracleides arrived in Rome while summer was still at its height, bringing Laodice and Alexander.

During his stay he lingered there, using a mixture of theatrical display and intrigue while he worked to influence the senate.

Astymedes of Rhodes, who had been appointed both envoy and admiral, then appeared before the senate and spoke about the war against the Cretans.

The senate listened attentively and immediately sent envoys under Quintus to settle the war.

## Book 33 Chapter 16: Crete and Rhodes Ask the Achaeans for Help

II. War between Rhodes and the Cretans.

At this time the Cretans sent envoys to the Achaeans to ask for assistance, headed by Antiphatas son of Telemnastus, a man of Gortyn; and in the same way the Rhodians sent envoys headed by Theophanes.

When the Achaean assembly met at Corinth and the envoys from both sides spoke in support of receiving aid, most people leaned more toward the Rhodians,

out of respect both for the standing of their city and for the general character of its constitution and leading men.

Seeing this, Antiphatas wanted to come forward again; and when the general gave permission, he spoke with more weight and seriousness than one would expect from a Cretan.

For the young man was not at all a typical Cretan, but had avoided the shameless habits associated with Crete.

As a result, the Achaeans welcomed his frank speech, and even more because his father Telemnastus had come with five hundred Cretans and had fought gallantly on their side in the war against Nabis.

Even so, after hearing him, the majority were still inclined to help the Rhodians, until Callicrates of Leontium stood up and said that they ought neither to go to war with anyone without Roman approval nor to send help to anyone against anyone else.

Because of this, the policy of leaving matters as they stood carried the day.

## Book 33 Chapter 17: Unreasonable Actions of the Rhodians

Because the Rhodians were distressed by the course of events, they fell into irrational impulses and schemes, coming into much the same frame of mind as people afflicted with chronic illness.

For such people often, when they follow every proper rule of treatment and obey the doctors yet still cannot make any progress toward recovery, grow despondent at what is happening to them. Some turn to sacrificers and seers; others try every kind of charm and amulet.

The same thing was happening among the Rhodians. Since everything kept turning out contrary to expectation, they were forced to listen to every proposal and to treat every hope as something real and worth waiting for.

And this was not unnatural. When nothing that accords with reason succeeds, yet one must keep acting without pause, one is compelled to test even courses that lie outside reason.

So the Rhodians, once they had fallen into such a state, did some of the things people do in that condition: among other irrational acts, they chose again as magistrate the very man whom they had previously rejected.

## Book 33 Chapter 18: Alexander Balas

D. Olympiad 156, year 4. I. Affairs in Italy.

When various embassies had arrived in Rome, the senate first called in Attalus, son of King Eumenes.

For he had come to Rome at this time while still a boy, both to be introduced to the senate and to renew his father's friendships and ties of hospitality.

He was kindly received both by the senate and by his father's friends, obtained the answers he wanted and honors suited to his age, and after a few days returned home,

being welcomed warmly and generously by all the Greek cities through which he passed.

Demetrius too arrived at the same time, but after receiving only a moderate welcome, appropriate enough for a boy, he again departed for home.

Heracleides, however, who had spent a long time in Rome, appeared before the senate, bringing Laodice and Alexander with him.

The young man first made a brief and modest speech and asked the Romans to remember their friendship and alliance with his father Antiochus, and above all to help him secure the kingship.

If not, then at least to permit his return and not hinder those who wished to help him recover his ancestral rule.

Then Heracleides took up the speech, spoke at length in praise of Antiochus and in accusation of Demetrius, and came to this point: that in justice they ought to permit the return of the young man and Laodice, since they were the legitimate offspring of King Antiochus.

None of this pleased the more sober-minded men; they saw the whole thing as a staged performance and openly loathed Heracleides.

But the majority, won over by Heracleides' trickery, were carried into drafting a decree like this:

"Alexander and Laodice, children of a king who was our friend and ally, appeared before the senate and addressed it.

The senate granted them permission to proceed to their ancestral kingdom, and it was resolved to assist them, as they requested."

Heracleides, taking advantage of this opening, at once began hiring mercenaries and summoning prominent men to join him; and when he arrived at Ephesus, he occupied himself with preparations for the enterprise he had in view.

## Book 33 Chapter 19: Demetrius's Intemperance

II. Affairs in Syria.

He also says that Demetrius—the man who escaped from his hostage life at Rome and became king of Syria in his thirty-third year—was a heavy drinker and spent most of the day drunk.

## Book 33 Chapter 20: The Multitude Will Act on its Feelings

E. Fragments of Uncertain Position.

Once the masses are seized by an extreme impulse to love or hate certain people, any pretext is enough to help them carry out their purpose.

## Book 33 Chapter 21

But I am afraid that I may stumble into the familiar proverb without noticing it: which is the greater fool, the man milking a he-goat or the man holding out a sieve? For by arguing so precisely against something everyone already admits is false, and by adding still more discussion, I too seem to be doing much the same thing.

So it is completely pointless to speak about such matters, unless someone wants to write down dreams and study dreams while fully awake.
