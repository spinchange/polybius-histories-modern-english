# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXXI

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 31 Chapter 1: War in Crete

A. Olympiad 154, year 1. Affairs in Italy.

Tiberius made the Cammani subjects of Rome, partly by force and partly by outwitting them.

When several other embassies had also arrived in Rome, the Senate gave audience to Attalus and Athenaeus and their party.

For Prusias had not only been actively engaged in spreading slanders against Eumenes and Antiochus himself, but had also stirred up the Galatians, the people of Selge, and many others in Asia to support the same cause.

For this reason King Eumenes had sent his brothers, to answer the accusations brought against him.

When they appeared before the Senate, they were judged to have made a satisfactory defense against all their accusers; and in the end they not only cleared away the charges brought against them, but were even honored before returning to Asia.

Even so, the Senate did not let go of its suspicion against Eumenes and Antiochus, but appointed Gaius Sulpicius and Manius Sergius as envoys and sent them out,

to inspect conditions among the Greeks, and also to settle for the Megalopolitans and the Lacedaemonians the dispute about the contested territory,

but above all to investigate the affairs of Antiochus and Eumenes, lest any preparations and any common enterprise against Rome should be developing between them.

## Book 31 Chapter 2: Gauls in Asia Granted Autonomy

Demetrius son of Seleucus, who had already long been kept in Rome as a hostage, had for some time been thought to be detained contrary to justice.

For Seleucus his father had given him on his own account as a pledge of good faith; but after Antiochus had succeeded to the throne, Demetrius ought not to remain a hostage on behalf of Antiochus' children.

Yet in the earlier period he kept quiet, chiefly because he was powerless, for he was still only a boy.

But now, being in the full prime of life, he came into the Senate and spoke, demanding and entreating them to restore him to his kingdom; for the sovereignty, he said, belonged more properly to him than to the sons of Antiochus.

After speaking at length on this claim, he pressed his case most strongly by saying that Rome was at once his fatherland and the city that had reared him, that he regarded all the sons of the senators as brothers and the senators themselves as fathers, since he had come there while still an infant and was now twenty-three years old.

All who heard him felt the appeal inwardly; yet the Senate as a body resolved to keep Demetrius in detention and to help secure the throne for the boy who had been left behind. It did this,

as it seems to me, because it took note of Demetrius in his full vigor, or rather because it judged that the youth and weakness of the boy who had inherited the kingdom better served Roman interests.

This was made clear by what happened afterward.

They at once appointed as envoys Gnaeus Octavius, Spurius Lucretius, and Lucius Aurelius and sent them out to regulate the affairs of the kingdom,

in the way the Senate itself had decided, because no one would stand in the way of what they ordered, since the king was a child and those who managed affairs were glad that the government had not been handed over to Demetrius; for this was what they most wanted.

So Gnaeus and his colleagues set out with instructions first to burn the decked warships, then to hamstring the elephants, and in general to cripple the royal power.

They were also instructed to examine the situation in Macedonia, for the Macedonians, being unaccustomed to a democratic and deliberative constitution, were in civil conflict with one another.

Gnaeus and his party were also to inspect the affairs of the Galatians and the condition of the kingdom of Ariarathes.

And after some time a letter was sent on to them from the Senate instructing them, if possible, to reconcile the kings at Alexandria as well.

## Book 31 Chapter 3: Grand Festival At Daphne

At the same time envoys arrived from Ariarathes, who had only recently succeeded to the throne of Cappadocia, to renew the friendship and alliance already existing,

and in general to urge the Senate to welcome the king's goodwill and eagerness, which he showed both publicly and privately toward all Romans.

After hearing their speech, the Senate renewed both the friendship and the alliance, and, approving the king's general policy, treated him with kindness.

This happened chiefly because Tiberius and his colleagues, when they had been sent out to inspect the affairs of the kings, came back and spoke favorably both of the king's father and of the kingdom as a whole.

Trusting their report, the senators gave the envoys a gracious reception and likewise approved the king's overall course.

## Book 31 Chapter 4: The King's Behavior at the Festival

II. Affairs in Greece.

The Rhodians, having recovered from the distress that had overtaken them, sent envoys to Rome headed by Cleagoras,

to ask that Calynda should be granted to them,

and also, concerning those who possessed property in Lycia and Caria, to request from the Senate that they might be allowed to hold it, as they had before.

They also voted to set up in the sanctuary of Athena a colossal statue of the Roman people, thirty cubits high.

## Book 31 Chapter 5: Roman Envoys Come to Antioch

When the people of Calynda revolted from the Caunians, and the Caunians afterward undertook to besiege them, the Calyndians at first appealed to the Cnidians.

When the Cnidians came to their aid, they held out against their enemies for a time,

but, anxious about what would happen next, they sent an embassy asking the Rhodians to take them and their city into their protection.

The Rhodians sent support both by land and by sea, raised the siege, and took over the city.

It so happened that the Senate also confirmed their possession of Calynda.

## Book 31 Chapter 6: Complaints Against Eumenes

III. Affairs in Asia.

Gaius Gallus, apart from the irrational conduct just mentioned, when he arrived in Asia posted notices in the most prominent cities,

ordering anyone who wished to accuse King Eumenes to appear at Sardis at an appointed time.

Then he himself came to Sardis, took his seat in the gymnasium, and for about ten days listened to the accusers,

accepting every kind of foul and abusive language against the king, and in general dragging in every possible affair and accusation,

like a man unbalanced in mind and eager to win distinction in his quarrel with Eumenes.

And the more harshly the Romans seemed to deal with Eumenes, the more the Greeks were drawn toward him, since human nature always gives its sympathy to the man under pressure.

## Book 31 Chapter 7: The Achaean Prisoners Detained

King Ariarathes of Cappadocia received the envoys who had been sent to Rome, and, judging from the answers they brought back that his throne now stood on firm ground because he had gained the goodwill of the Romans, he offered thank-offerings to the gods for what had happened and entertained the leading men.

After this he sent envoys to Lysias and his party at Antioch, eager to recover the bones of his sister and mother.

He decided not to bring any charge of impiety over what had happened, because he did not want to provoke Lysias and his party and so fail in his purpose, though he took the matter deeply to heart; instead, he gave the envoys instructions suited to their rank and sent them off.

When Lysias and his party consented, and the bones were brought back to him, he received them with great honor on their arrival and buried them beside his father's tomb with proper care.

## Book 31 Chapter 8: Envoys from Achaia in the Senate

At this time envoys arrived from Rome, first those with Marcus Junius, to clarify the king's disputes with the Galatians.

For when the Trocmi had been unable by their own efforts to cut off any part of Cappadocia for themselves, they brazenly manufactured a case and, by appealing to the Romans, tried to slander Ariarathes.

That is why the envoys with Junius were sent. The king addressed them as was fitting and otherwise treated them with kindness, so that he sent them away speaking well of him.

After this, when envoys arrived with Gnaeus Octavius and Spurius Lucretius and again discussed with the king his disputes with the Galatians,

he spoke only briefly and in general terms about those matters, saying that he was ready to accept whatever judgment was made, and then devoted the greater part of his speech to affairs in Syria, since he knew that Octavius and his party were proceeding there,

pointing out to them the instability of the kingdom there and the rashness of the men in charge of it, and also promising that he would follow with a force and support the situation until they returned safely from Syria.

The envoys with Gnaeus, approving in every respect the king's goodwill and eagerness,

said that for the present they had no need of an escort, but that in the future, if any need arose, they would let him know without hesitation; for they judged him to be one of Rome's true friends.

## Book 31 Chapter 9: The Senate Suspicious of Eumenes and Antiochus

In Syria King Antiochus, wanting to raise money, resolved to march against the temple of Artemis in Elymais.

But when he reached the place, his hopes were disappointed, because the barbarians living around the sanctuary would not permit this act of sacrilege.

So he withdrew, and on his retreat he died at Tabae in Persia.

Some say that he went mad, because certain signs of divine anger appeared in response to the lawless outrage he had attempted against that sanctuary.

## Book 31 Chapter 10: Sulpicius Gallus Investigates Eumenes

B. Olympiad 154, year 2. Affairs in Italy.

After the kingdom had been divided between the Ptolemies, the younger Ptolemy came to Rome, wishing to set aside the partition that had been made between himself and his brother.

He said that he had not agreed to it willingly, but had done what was demanded only because circumstances had forced him.

He begged the Senate to assign Cyprus to him, arguing that even if this were done his share would still be far inferior to his brother's.

But the supporters of Canuleius and Quintus testified against Menyllus and the envoys who had come from the elder brother, saying that the younger brother owed not only Cyrene but even his life to them, since the hostility and estrangement of the crowds toward him had become so severe.

That was why, when the settlement concerning Cyrene had been granted to him unexpectedly and beyond hope, he had gladly accepted it, had offered sacrifices, and had both received and sworn the oaths with his brother.

Ptolemy, however, disputed all this. The Senate, seeing on the one hand that the division had been completely settled, but on the other wishing in practical terms to break up the kingdom, now that the brothers themselves had supplied the pretext for division, agreed to the younger brother's requests for its own advantage.

For this has long been a Roman practice in policy: by taking advantage of the ignorance of neighboring peoples, they strengthen and extend their own power in practical ways, while at the same time gratifying those who make mistakes and seeming to bestow favors on them.

So, seeing the scale of Egyptian power and fearing that if it should ever obtain a capable protector it might grow more ambitious than was proper,

they appointed Titus Torquatus and Gnaeus Merula as envoys to escort Ptolemy to Cyprus and at the same time carry out both his design and their own.

They sent them off at once, with instructions to reconcile the brothers and secure Cyprus for the younger without war.

## Book 31 Chapter 11: Death of Antiochus Epiphanes

At this same time, when the disaster involving Gnaeus had taken place,

that is, when he had been killed, and when envoys sent by King Antiochus, men dispatched by Lysias, had arrived and spoken at length to show that the king's friends had had no part in the deed,

the Senate dismissed the envoys, not wishing to give any decision on the matter or to reveal its opinion at all.

Demetrius, alarmed by what had been reported, summoned Polybius and asked him in perplexity whether he ought to approach the Senate again about his own affairs.

Polybius urged him not to stumble twice against the same stone, but to place his hopes in himself and dare something worthy of a kingdom, since the present moment, he said, offered many openings.

Demetrius took the meaning of this, and for the moment said nothing; but a little later he shared the matter with one of his close companions, Apollonius.

Apollonius, who was innocent by nature and very young, advised him to make one more attempt with the Senate. He was convinced that, although they had unreasonably deprived him of the kingdom, they would at least release him from his hostage status.

For it was completely absurd, he said, that now that Antiochus' son had succeeded to the kingdom in Syria, Demetrius should remain a hostage on his behalf.

Persuaded by these arguments, Demetrius once more entered the Senate and begged them at least to free him from the obligation of being a hostage, since they had decided to secure the kingdom for Antiochus.

But although he spoke at still greater length on this point, the Senate remained with the same decision, as was only natural.

For even before, it had not resolved to preserve the rule for the boy because Demetrius had failed to make a just claim, but because that course served its own interests.

And since the whole situation remained in the same condition, it was natural that the Senate's judgment should also remain fixed on the same policy.

## Book 31 Chapter 12: Demetrius son of Seleucus

Yet Demetrius, after having wasted his breath in that final appeal and recognizing that Polybius had advised him well not to stumble twice against the same stone,

repented of what had happened; and being high-minded by nature and possessing courage equal to the decision he had to make, he immediately called for Diodorus, who had recently arrived from Syria, and confided his situation to him.

Diodorus had once been Demetrius' tutor, and, being a shrewd man who had closely observed conditions in Syria, he pointed out to him

that since affairs there were in confusion because of the murder of Gnaeus, and since the masses distrusted Lysias' circle while Lysias' circle distrusted the masses, and since the Senate was convinced that the outrage committed against its envoys had been the work of the king's friends, this was the best possible time for him to appear on the scene.

For the people there would quickly transfer the kingship to him, even if he arrived with only a single attendant, and the Senate would no longer dare to support or reinforce Lysias and his party after what they had done.

The only thing left, he said, was for them to get away from Rome unnoticed, so that no one would suspect his plan.

When this seemed good, Demetrius sent for Polybius, told him what had been decided, and asked him to help take charge of the attempt and think through how his escape should be managed.

Now at this time Menyllus of Alabanda happened to be in Rome as an envoy from the elder King Ptolemy, sent to support his cause and justify him against the younger Ptolemy. Polybius already had a strong friendship and bond of trust with Menyllus.

Thinking therefore that Menyllus would be useful in the present need, Polybius introduced him to Demetrius with great eagerness and zeal.

Menyllus, after joining the enterprise, undertook to prepare a ship and complete the other arrangements for the voyage.

He found at the mouth of the Tiber a Carthaginian sacred ship lying at anchor, and hired it.

These ships are specially selected from Carthage, and the Carthaginians send on them their ancestral firstfruits to the gods at Tyre; but he openly hired this one for his own return passage.

So he made the monthly provisioning without arousing suspicion, and openly gave orders and spoke with the sailors.

## Book 31 Chapter 13: Ariarathes of Cappadocia

Once everything was ready on the shipmaster's side, and Demetrius only had to complete his own arrangements, he sent his foster-father ahead to Syria to gather intelligence and observe what was happening there among the populace.

His companion Apollonius had shared in the plan from the beginning; and, since there were two brothers, Meleager and Menestheus, he disclosed the undertaking to them, but to no one else among his followers, though they were more numerous.

These men were Apollonius' own sons. Their father had enjoyed great influence with Seleucus, but when Antiochus took over the kingdom he had withdrawn to Miletus.

As the day agreed upon with the sailors drew near, there had to be a farewell entertainment at the house of one of his friends before the departure.

For it was impossible to hold the dinner at his own quarters, since he was accustomed to invite everyone in his circle with great care.

Those who knew of the plan were to dine at home and then come to the ship, each bringing one servant-boy with him; the rest they had sent on to Anagnia, intending to follow themselves on the next day.

At this time Polybius happened to be ill and confined to bed, but he knew everything that was being done, because Menyllus kept continually informing him of each new development.

So Polybius, anxious that habit might get the better of Demetrius — since he was naturally fond of drinking parties and still altogether young — and that drunkenness might create some difficulty at the moment of departure,

wrote a short note, sealed it, and at dusk sent one of his own servants with instructions to call out Demetrius' cupbearer, hand him the note without saying who he was or who had sent it, and tell him to give it to Demetrius at once for immediate reading.

Everything was done as arranged, and Demetrius took the note and read it.

The note contained these thoughts:

The man who acts with the future in mind goes forward prepared.

Night gives equal cover to all, but to the daring it gives more.

Dare something,

risk it, act, fail,
or succeed — anything rather than betray yourself.

Stay sober, and remember to mistrust: these are the sinews of good sense.

## Book 31 Chapter 14: Ambassadors from Ariarathes to Rome

After reading this and understanding the situation, and who the men were and who had sent them, Demetrius at once pretended that he was going aboard for the voyage and slipped away, his friends escorting him part of the way.

When he reached his lodging, he sent off the unnecessary servants to Anagnia, ordering them to take the nets and the dogs and meet him at Circeii.

For that was where he regularly liked to hunt wild boar with special zeal; indeed, this had been the original beginning of his friendship with Polybius.

After this he explained the plan to Nicanor and his companions and urged them to share the same hopes.

Since they all accepted the proposal eagerly, he ordered them to go back at once to their lodgings and instruct their servants to go on ahead before dawn to Anagnia and, together with the hunters, meet them at Circeii,

while they themselves were to take traveling clothes and return to him, telling their servants that on the next day they would join Demetrius at the place already mentioned.

When everything had been done as described, they set out by night for Ostia, to the mouth of the Tiber.

Menyllus went on ahead and spoke with the sailors, saying that a message had come to him from the king, and that for this reason he himself had to remain in Rome for the present, but should send to the king the most trustworthy of the young men, from whom he would learn everything concerning his brother.

So he said that he himself would not embark, but that the young men who were to sail would arrive about midnight.

The shipmasters were indifferent, because the freight agreed on had been paid to them from the beginning and they had long had everything ready for the voyage.

The party with Demetrius arrived as the third watch was ending: there were eight of them, with five servant-boys and three younger attendants.

Menyllus then talked matters over with them, showed them the monthly provisions, and further introduced them fully to the shipmaster and the passengers; after that they went aboard.

At first light the captain weighed anchor and put to sea, having absolutely no suspicion of what was really going on, but thinking that he was simply carrying some soldiers from Menyllus to Ptolemy.

## Book 31 Chapter 15: Rhodes Asks Rome for Calynda

On the next day in Rome no one was likely to go looking for Demetrius or for the companions who had left with him.

Those who stayed behind with him thought that he had gone off to Circeii, while those at Anagnia were coming to meet him at the same place, expecting that he would be there.

So the escape remained completely unnoticed until one of the servant-boys, after being flogged at Anagnia, ran off to Circeii, intending to join Demetrius there.

Not finding him there either, he ran back to Rome, expecting to meet him on the road.

When he failed to encounter him anywhere, he revealed the matter to Demetrius' friends in Rome and to those who had been left behind at the house.

So when Demetrius was looked for on the fourth day after his departure, what had happened was suspected.

On the fifth day the Senate at once met to consider the matter; by then Demetrius was already beyond the strait near Sicily.

They therefore gave up the idea of pursuit, both because they thought he had already gained too great a lead on the voyage — for he had a strong following wind — and because they foresaw that even if they wished to stop him, they would be unable to do so.

After some days they appointed as envoys Tiberius Gracchus, Lucius Lentulus, and Servilius Glaucia,

who were first to inspect affairs among the Greeks, then to cross over into Asia and keep watch on the situation concerning Demetrius, examine the intentions of the other kings, and clear up the disputed issues involving the Galatians that were mentioned earlier.

This was why they appointed Tiberius, because he had been an eyewitness of the whole situation.

And this was the state of affairs in Italy.

Demetrius, meanwhile, was intent on the arrival of the man who was to be sent to him.

## Book 31 Chapter 16: Rhodes Assists Calynda

II. Affairs in Asia.

Artaxias wanted to recover [text missing]; but at Ariarathes' urging he did not do so, and instead treated the man with greater honor than before. So great is the power that belongs to justice by its very nature,

and so great too are the judgments and counsels of good men, that they often save not only friends but even enemies and change their characters for the better.

The finest quality of any letter is its power to establish goodwill.

## Book 31 Chapter 17: The Two Ptolemies

III. Affairs in Africa.

After this, the younger Ptolemy arrived in Greece with the envoys and began gathering a substantial force of mercenaries. Among those he enlisted was the Macedonian Damasippus,

who had butchered the members of the council at Phacus and then fled from Macedonia with his wife and children.

When Ptolemy reached the Rhodian Peraea and was received as a guest by the people there, he intended to sail on to Cyprus.

But Torquatus and his colleagues, seeing that he had assembled a formidable foreign force, reminded him of their instructions: the enterprise was to be carried through without war.

At last they persuaded him to lead the mercenaries only as far as Side, then disband them, give up the attempt on Cyprus, and join them instead on the frontier of Cyrene.

They themselves said they would sail to Alexandria, induce the king to comply with their requests, and meet him on the frontier, bringing that king with them.

Convinced by these arguments, the younger Ptolemy gave up the Cyprus project and disbanded his mercenary force.

He himself first sailed to Crete, taking with him Damasippus and one of the envoys, Gnaeus Merula. After recruiting about a thousand soldiers in Crete, he put to sea, crossed over to Libya, and landed at Apis.

## Book 31 Chapter 18: The Murder of Octavius

The party with Torquatus, after being conveyed to Alexandria, tried to persuade the elder Ptolemy to come to terms with his brother and yield Cyprus to him.

But Ptolemy partly made promises and partly ignored them, and by this method simply dragged out the time.

Meanwhile the younger Ptolemy was encamped with the Cretans in Libya near Apis, in accordance with the arrangement, and was thoroughly irritated that nothing had been made clear. At first he sent Gnaeus to Alexandria, hoping that through him he might at least get matters moving with Torquatus and his party.

But when he too was treated just like the others, and time kept being stretched out, and forty days passed without any result, Ptolemy fell into uncertainty about the whole situation.

For the elder king, by offering every kind of courtesy, had won over the envoys one by one and kept most of them there, not of their own choice but against their will.

At this same time news reached the younger Ptolemy that the Cyrenaeans had revolted, that the cities were siding with them, and that a certain Ptolemy called Sympetesis had also joined in the revolt.

He was an Egyptian by birth and had been entrusted by the king with general supervision of affairs at the time when Ptolemy sailed to Rome.

When these reports reached the king, and shortly afterward came word that the Cyrenaeans were encamped in the open country, he feared that in trying to seize Cyprus he might lose Cyrene as well. So, putting every other concern aside, he broke camp and marched for Cyrene.

When he reached the place called the Great Catabathmus, he found the Libyans together with the Cyrenaeans holding the difficult ground.

Pressed by the situation, Ptolemy put half his soldiers aboard the ships and ordered them to sail around the rough terrain and show themselves in the enemy's rear, while he himself, with the other half, forced a frontal ascent.

The Libyans were alarmed by the attack from both sides and abandoned the position; so Ptolemy at once gained control both of the ascent and of the district below called Tetrapyrgia, where there was an abundant supply of water.

Setting out from there, he completed a seven-day march through the desert.

While his ships also coasted along past Mochyrinus, it turned out that the Cyrenaeans were encamped with about eight thousand infantry and some five hundred cavalry.

For the Cyrenaeans, having learned Ptolemy's character from what had happened at Alexandria, and seeing that his rule and his whole policy were not royal but tyrannical,

could not bring themselves to submit voluntarily. Instead, they dared to endure anything, holding fast to the hope of freedom.

So when he approached, they drew up to meet him in battle; and in the end Ptolemy was defeated.

## Book 31 Chapter 19: Demetrius Appeals Again to the Senate

At this time Gnaeus Merula also arrived from Alexandria and informed the king that his brother had accepted none of the demands, but said that they must stand by the terms originally agreed upon.

When the king heard this, he immediately selected the brothers Comanus and Ptolemy and sent them as envoys to Rome with Gnaeus, to explain to the Senate his brother's greed and contemptuous behavior.

At the same time the elder Ptolemy dismissed Titus and his companions without accomplishing anything.

That, then, was the situation at Alexandria and Cyrene.

## Book 31 Chapter 20: Demetrius Plans To Leave Rome

C. Olympiad 154, year 3. Affairs in Italy.

At the period under discussion envoys arrived from the younger Ptolemy, headed by Comanus, and likewise from the elder brother, headed by Menyllus of Alabanda.

When they entered the Senate, many speeches were delivered, and hostile accusations were exchanged face to face. The men attached to Titus and Gnaeus testified against the elder king and strongly supported the younger.

The Senate therefore decided that Menyllus and his party must leave Rome within five days, that the alliance with the elder Ptolemy should be revoked, and that envoys should be sent to the younger brother to report the Senate's decision.

Accordingly Publius Apustius and Gaius Lentulus were appointed. They sailed at once to Cyrene and delivered the Senate's decree to Ptolemy with all speed.

Ptolemy was immediately elated; he began hiring mercenaries at once and devoted himself wholly to plans for an attack on Cyprus.

And this was the state of affairs in Italy.

## Book 31 Chapter 21: Demetrius Plans His Escape

In Libya, Masinissa saw the large number of cities founded around the Lesser Syrtis, the beauty of the district called Emporia, and the great revenues long produced there; and for a long time he had had his eye on these advantages.

So, not many years before the present time, he resolved to put the Carthaginians to the test.

He quickly made himself master of the countryside, since he controlled the open country and the Carthaginians had always been strangers to work on the land; and at that time they had been completely softened by a long peace.

But he was unable to gain control of the cities, because the Carthaginians kept careful watch over them.

Since both sides kept referring the disputed claims to the Senate, and envoys had often come from each party on this business,

it always happened that the Carthaginians got the worse of it with the Romans, not because justice was on the other side, but because the judges were convinced that such a decision suited Roman interests.

And yet, not many years earlier, Masinissa himself, while pursuing the rebel Aphther with an army, had asked the Carthaginians for passage through this territory, and they had refused, on the ground that it did not belong to him at all.

But in the end the Carthaginians were brought, by the decisions now being given, to such a pass that they lost not only the cities and the territory, but also had to add five hundred talents for the revenue of the intervening years, from the time when the dispute first arose.

## Book 31 Chapter 22: Demetrius Escapes

The greatest and finest proof of Lucius Aemilius's character became plain to everyone after his death.

For the impression formed of his way of life while he was alive was found to be true even after he had passed away; and that, one might say, is the strongest evidence of virtue.

Though he had brought more gold from Iberia to Rome than any of his contemporaries, had become master of immense treasures in Macedonia, and had possessed the fullest authority in all these matters, he nevertheless left his private fortune in such a state that

his family could not repay his wife's entire dowry out of the household property unless they sold off some of the landed estate as well. I have spoken of this in detail earlier.

From this one may say that the reputation of the Greek statesmen admired on this score is eclipsed.

For if it is admirable to refuse money that is offered in the giver's own interest, as is said of Aristides of Athens and Epaminondas of Thebes,

how much more admirable is it, after becoming master of an entire kingdom and receiving full power to use it however one wished, to have desired none of it at all?

If what I say seems unbelievable to some, they should keep this in mind: the writer knew perfectly well that Romans above all would take these books into their hands, because they contain the most notable and the greater part of Roman achievements.

Among such readers these facts could neither remain unknown nor would a liar likely receive pardon.

Therefore no one would willingly expose himself to open disbelief and contempt.

Let this be remembered throughout the whole work whenever I seem to say something surprising about the Romans.

## Book 31 Chapter 23: No One Notices Demetrius's Absence

Now that the course of the narrative and the sequence of events have brought me to this household, I want, for the benefit of readers who enjoy such matters, to make good the promise I left unfulfilled in the previous book.

For I had promised to explain why and how Scipio's reputation in Rome advanced so far and shone out more quickly than might have been expected,

and at the same time how Polybius's friendship and intimacy with him grew so great that the report of them spread not only through Italy and Greece, but even farther away their principles and their way of life became well known.

I have already explained that the beginning of their association came from the use of books and from conversations about them.

As their friendship progressed, and the detainees who had been summoned were being sent back to their own cities, the two young men, Fabius and Scipio, sons of Lucius, earnestly pressed that Polybius should remain in Rome.

Once this had happened, and their companionship had advanced a great deal, there occurred an incident of the following kind.

One day, when they were all leaving Fabius's house together, Fabius happened to turn aside toward the forum, while Polybius went another way with Scipio.

As they walked on, Publius, speaking quietly and gently, and blushing as he spoke, said,

"What is this, Polybius? We are two brothers, yet you are always talking with him, putting all your questions to him and giving all your answers to him, while you pass me over.

Or is it clear that you too hold the same opinion of me that, as I hear, the other citizens hold?

For I seem to everyone, so I am told, to be quiet and sluggish, and far removed from the Roman character and manner of action, because I do not choose to plead cases in court.

And they say that the family I come from wants not such a protector, but the very opposite; and that is what pains me most."

## Book 31 Chapter 24: Decadence at Rome

Polybius was surprised at the way the young man had begun the conversation, for he was then not yet more than eighteen years old.

"For heaven's sake, Scipio," he said, "do not say such things, and do not entertain them in your mind at all.

It is not because I think less of you or pass you over that I act this way—far from it. It is simply because your brother is older that in conversation I begin with him and end with him again, and in judgments and advice I lean on him, assuming that you share the same opinions as he does.

But I admire you now when I hear that you think it painful to be milder than is fitting for one sprung from this house; for by these words you plainly show that your ambitions are high.

And I myself would gladly devote my efforts to you and become your helper, so that you may both speak and act in a way worthy of your ancestors.

As for the studies in which I now see you and your brother taking such pains and competing for distinction, you will not lack men ready to help you, both you and him.

For at present I see a whole crowd of such people pouring in from Greece.

But in the matter that troubles you now most of all, as you say, I think you would find no one among us better suited to stand beside you and help you."

While Polybius was still speaking, Scipio took his right hand in both of his own and pressed it warmly. "If only I could see that day," he said, "when you, putting everything else second, would devote your attention to me and live with me as my companion.

For from that moment I would think myself at once worthy of my house and of my ancestors."

Polybius was pleased as he watched the young man's zeal and frankness, but he was also at a loss when he reflected on the greatness of the family and the standing of the men around it.

Even so, from the time of this exchange onward, the young man never separated himself from Polybius, and everything else became secondary to Polybius's company.

## Book 31 Chapter 25: The Rhodians Lapse in Dignity

From this time onward, by continually proving themselves to one another in practical matters, they came to feel toward each other the affection of father and son, and of close kinsmen.

Scipio's first impulse and zeal for noble conduct was to recover a reputation for self-control and in this respect to surpass the rest of those of his own age.

And though this prize was great and hard to win, at that time in Rome it was easy to seize, because most men were rushing in the opposite direction.

Some of the young men were giving themselves over to boys, others to courtesans, and many to entertainments, drinking parties, and the extravagance connected with them, having quickly adopted during the war with Perseus the Greek looseness in such things.

So great a lack of self-mastery had fallen upon the youth in matters of this kind that many had bought a favorite for a talent, and many a jar of Pontic salt fish for three hundred drachmas.

Indignant at this, Marcus once said to the people that they could see most clearly from such things how far the constitution had declined, when handsome boys fetched a higher price than farms, and jars of preserved fish than teamsters.

This ambition first came into prominence at the time we are speaking of, first because, now that the Macedonian monarchy had been overthrown, the Romans thought their supremacy over the whole world beyond dispute,

and then because a great display of prosperity had arisen both in private life and in public affairs, from the wealth brought from Macedonia to Rome.

But Scipio, setting himself toward the opposite manner of life, resisting every desire, and making his conduct throughout consistent and harmonious, within perhaps his first five years made his reputation for discipline and self-control known to everyone.

After this he continually aimed to surpass the rest in greatness of spirit and integrity in money matters.

For this side of his character he had a fine foundation in his life with his natural father; he himself also had by nature good impulses toward what was right; and much besides came of itself to help forward this purpose.

## Book 31 Chapter 26: The Two Ptolemies

The first event was the death of the mother of his adoptive father, who was the sister of his natural father Lucius and the wife of his adoptive grandfather Scipio, the one called the Great.

When she left a large estate, and he became her heir, he was first to give proof in this matter of the principles on which he had chosen to live.

This woman, whose name was Aemilia, had maintained a magnificent establishment for women's public appearances, as was natural for one who had shared the life and fortune of Scipio.

For apart from the adornment of her own person and carriage, the baskets, cups, and other objects used in sacrifice, sometimes of silver and sometimes of gold, all accompanied her on her more splendid outings.

And along with these went the large number of maidservants and household attendants who followed in her train.

This entire establishment, immediately after Aemilia's funeral, Scipio gave to his mother, who had long since been separated from Lucius and whose means of support were less ample than her noble birth would suggest.

So, whereas before that time she had kept away from distinguished public appearances, when by chance there came a splendid and well-attended sacrifice, she went out with Aemilia's full equipment and provision, and with the same muleteers, team, and carriage besides.

As a result, the women who witnessed it were astonished at Scipio's kindness and generosity, and all stretched out their hands in prayer, calling down many blessings on him.

This would naturally seem admirable anywhere, but at Rome it seemed positively astonishing, since no one there simply gives any part of his private property to anyone else of his own free will.

This, then, was the first beginning of his reputation for nobility of character, and it advanced him greatly, since the female sex is both talkative and lavish in spreading whatever captures its interest.

## Book 31 Chapter 27: Ptolemy Physcon Invades Cyrene

After this, he was obliged to pay the remaining half of the dowries owed to the daughters of Scipio the Great, who were the sisters of his adoptive father.

For his father had agreed to give each of the two daughters fifty talents,

and of this amount their mother had immediately paid one half to their husbands, while at her death she left the other half still owing.

So Scipio had to discharge this debt to his father's sisters.

Now according to Roman law the money still owing as dowry had to be paid to the women in three yearly installments, after the furniture had first been sold within ten months, according to their custom.

But Scipio at once instructed his banker to pay each woman her twenty-five talents within those ten months.

When Tiberius and Scipio Nasica — for these were the husbands of the women just mentioned — came to the banker as soon as the ten months had expired and asked whether Scipio had given him any instructions about the money, and he told them to collect it and wrote out drafts to each of them for twenty-five talents, they said he must be mistaken.

For, they said, according to the law they should receive not the whole amount now, but only the third part at present.

But when he insisted that Scipio had given exactly those instructions, they believed him and went on to the young man, supposing that he did not understand the matter. Nor was this unreasonable.

For no one in Rome would pay fifty talents before the three years were up — indeed, not even a single talent before the appointed day.

So universal and so great among them all is their exactness in matters of legal claim, and at the same time their regard for the advantage of delay.

Nevertheless, when they approached him and asked why he had instructed the banker in this way, and he replied that the whole sum was to be paid to his sisters, they said that he did not understand, while at the same time showing a guardian's concern.

For under the laws, they said, he was still entitled for a considerable time to make use of the sums at issue.

But Scipio said that he was perfectly well aware of all this, yet that toward outsiders he observed the strictness of the law, whereas toward relatives and friends he acted simply and generously, as far as he could.

Therefore he told them to take the whole amount from the banker.

When Tiberius and the others heard this, they went away in silence, overwhelmed by Scipio's greatness of spirit and condemning their own pettiness, though they were second to none among the Romans.

## Book 31 Chapter 28: Gnaeus Merula Comes to Rome

Two years later his natural father Lucius died, leaving him and his brother Fabius as heirs to the estate; on this occasion Scipio did something noble and worthy of remembrance.

For Lucius, being left without children in his own house — since some had been given in adoption into other families, and the rest of the sons whom he had been rearing as successors to the family had all died — left his estate to these two.

Scipio, seeing that his brother was less well supplied with means, gave up his own share of all the property, whose total value was more than sixty talents, so that in this way Fabius might possess wealth equal to his own.

When this became widely talked about, he added to it another and even clearer proof of the principles by which he lived.

His brother wanted to give gladiatorial games in honor of their father, but was unable to bear the expense because of the amount of money required, and Scipio contributed half the cost from his own fortune.

And the total expense is not less than thirty talents, if a man puts on the show magnificently.

[text missing] while the report of his character was spreading abroad, his mother died.

He was so far from reclaiming anything that he had previously given her, about which I have just spoken, that he handed over both those gifts and all the rest of his mother's property to his sisters, although by law none of it belonged to them.

So when the sisters once again took over Aemilia's display and retinue for their public appearances, the generosity and family affection of Scipio's character were once more brought freshly into notice.

Thus, prepared from early youth in this way, Publius Scipio advanced toward winning distinction for self-control and nobility of character.

By spending perhaps sixty talents — for he had given up that much of his private means — he obtained an acknowledged reputation for generosity, accomplishing his purpose not so much by the amount of money as by the timing of the gift and the gracious way in which he bestowed the favor.

His self-control, however, he acquired at no cost at all; by abstaining from many and various pleasures he gained bodily health and vigor,

and this stayed with him throughout his whole life, repaying him with many pleasures and fine rewards in return for the easy pleasures from which he had earlier abstained.

## Book 31 Chapter 29

There remained the part concerned with courage, which is almost the most important element in every constitution, and especially at Rome; so this too required the most serious training.

Here again fortune provided him with a fine aid toward the goal he had set himself.

The Macedonian kings had always taken great pains over hunting, and the Macedonians had set apart the places most suitable for keeping a supply of wild animals.

So it came about that these districts were carefully preserved throughout the whole war, just as they had been before, but because of the distractions of those four years they were never hunted at all. As a result, they were full of every kind of game.

When the war had been decided, Lucius, thinking hunting both the best training and the best recreation for young men, introduced Scipio to the royal huntsmen and handed over to him complete authority over the hunts.

Once he had taken this up, and fancying himself almost a king, he devoted all his time to it for as long as the army remained in Macedonia after the battle.

And because great enthusiasm for this pursuit sprang up in him—partly because he was in the full vigor of youth, and partly because his nature was so well suited to it, like that of a well-bred hound—his passion for hunting became enduring.

So when he returned to Rome and also took on Polybius' enthusiasm for this pursuit, while the rest of the young men were eager for court cases and morning calls, spending their time in the forum and trying by such means to recommend themselves to the crowd,

Scipio, by occupying himself with hunting and always doing something splendid and memorable, won a finer reputation than the others.

For they could not gain praise unless they injured one of their fellow citizens; that is the usual effect of litigation.

But he, without causing pain to anyone at all, won a reputation for courage among the whole people, proving in deeds what others only claimed in words.

Accordingly, in a short time he outstripped his contemporaries to a degree no Roman is remembered ever to have done, even though in his pursuit of distinction he had taken a path opposite to that of all the others, so far as Roman custom and practice were concerned.

## Book 31 Chapter 30

I have spoken at greater length about the course Scipio chose in his youth because I believe such an account is pleasant for older readers and useful for the young,

and especially because I want to win belief for what will be said about him in the following books, so that those who hear it may neither be perplexed when some of the things that happened to him later seem extraordinary,

nor, by ignoring the causes from which each event came about, attribute to fortune achievements that were really the result of judgment and character—except for the very few things that ought to be credited only to fortune and chance.

Having carried this digression far enough, I now return again to the main thread of the narrative.

## Book 31 Chapter 31

II. Greek Affairs

In other respects the Rhodians preserved the dignity of their constitution, but at this time they slipped a little, in my judgment. For they accepted from Eumenes two hundred and eighty thousand measures of grain, so that the money realized from it might be lent out, and the interest used to pay the salaries of the instructors and teachers of their sons.

If a private household had fallen into real difficulty, one might perhaps accept such help from friends, rather than see one's children grow up without education because of poverty. But if one were well provided for, one would endure anything sooner than go collecting from friends the money used to pay teachers.

And in proportion as a state ought to think on a larger scale than a private person, what is fitting ought to be guarded even more carefully in public matters than in private ones—especially by the Rhodians, given both the abundance of their public resources and their claim to dignity.

## Book 31 Chapter 32

III. Asian Affairs

In Asia, Prusias sent envoys to Rome together with Galatians to bring accusations against Eumenes.

Eumenes in turn sent his brother Attalus to answer the charges.

Ariarathes also sent to Rome a golden crown worth ten thousand gold pieces, together with envoys who were to explain to the senate the answer he had given to Tiberius and, more generally, to urge them to state plainly whatever they required, since he was ready to do whatever the Romans commanded.

## Book 31 Chapter 33

When Menochares arrived at Antioch to see Demetrius and gave the king a full account of the meeting he had had with Tiberius and his party in Cappadocia,

Demetrius decided that, of all the business before him, the most necessary was to cultivate those men as far as he could.

So he treated everything else as secondary and kept sending messages to them, first to Pamphylia and then again to Rhodes, undertaking to do whatever the Romans required, until he succeeded in being acknowledged by them as king.

For Tiberius was especially well disposed toward him, and so he contributed greatly to Demetrius's success in establishing himself and securing the royal power.

And Demetrius, seizing the opportunity just mentioned, at once sent envoys to Rome, bringing a crown, the man who had with his own hand killed Gnaeus, and along with them Isocrates the literary critic.
