# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXIX

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 29 Chapter 1: Speech of L. Aemilius Paullus

Olympiad 152, year 4. Affairs in Italy.

For he said that they had only one occupation, both at dinner parties and in their conversations while walking about: seated in Rome, they managed the war in Macedonia, now criticizing what the generals were doing, now going through what they had left undone.

From this, he said, no benefit ever came to public affairs, but much harm had often resulted in many cases; and at times the commanders themselves were seriously damaged by these ill-timed displays of clever talk.

For slander always has something sharp and stirring about it, and when the mass of people has been prejudiced by constant chatter, the generals become easy for their enemies to despise.

## Book 29 Chapter 2: Gaius Popilius Laenas Sent to Alexandria

Because the senate learned that Antiochus had become master of Egypt and had come close to taking Alexandria as well,

and because it judged that the growth of this king's power touched its own interests, it appointed as envoys Gaius Popilius and his colleagues,

to put an end to the war and, more generally, to inspect the state of affairs.

Such, then, was the situation in Italy.

## Book 29 Chapter 3: Genthius Forms an Alliance with Perseus

II. War against Perseus.

When Hippias and his companions, whom Perseus had sent as envoys to Gentius about the alliance, arrived before winter,

and explained that the king was ready to undertake war against the Romans if he were given three hundred talents and the proper guarantees for the whole agreement,

Perseus, when he heard this and judged Gentius' cooperation necessary, chose Pantauchus, one of his chief friends,

and sent him off with instructions first to settle the matter of the money and to exchange sworn pledges for the alliance, then to take whatever hostages from Gentius Pantauchus thought fit and send them on, and to receive from Perseus whatever hostages Gentius should specify in the written terms, and besides this to arrange the transport of the three hundred talents.

Pantauchus at once set out and, arriving at Meteon in the land of the Labeatae, met Gentius there and quickly brought the young king round to sharing Perseus' hopes.

When the oaths for the alliance had been sworn and put into writing, Gentius immediately sent the hostages whom Pantauchus had specified, and with them Olympion to receive from Perseus the oaths and the hostages, while others were to take charge of the money.

Along with this, Pantauchus persuaded Gentius also to send envoys of his own, who, together with those being sent by Perseus, would go to Rhodes to negotiate a common alliance.

For if that were accomplished, and the Rhodians too entered the war, he declared that the Romans would be altogether easy to defeat.

Gentius, persuaded by these appeals, appointed Parmenion and Marcus and sent them out with instructions that, once they had received the oaths and hostages from Perseus and agreement had been reached about the money, they were to go on embassy to Rhodes.

## Book 29 Chapter 4: Genthius Joins Perseus

So all these men proceeded to Macedonia. But Pantauchus stayed at Gentius' side and kept reminding and pressing the young king not to delay his preparations, but, while ready, to seize positions, cities, and allies in advance; above all he urged him to prepare for a struggle at sea.

For since the Romans were completely unprepared in this sphere, both on the coasts of Epirus and in Illyria, everything proposed would be accomplished without a fight by Gentius himself and the forces he sent out.

So Gentius, persuaded by these arguments, busied himself with preparations both by land and by sea.

Perseus, meanwhile, when the envoys and hostages from Gentius arrived in Macedonia, set out from the camp by the river Elpeius with all his cavalry and went to meet them at Dion,

and when he met them he first gave the oaths for the alliance in the presence of all the cavalry. For he greatly wished the Macedonians to know clearly of Gentius' cooperation, hoping that with this additional advantage they would be more confident.

Next he received the hostages and handed over his own to Olympion and his companions. The most distinguished among them were Limnaeus son of Polemocrates and Balacrus son of Pantauchus.

After this he sent those who had come for the money on to Pella to receive it there, while he dispatched the envoys bound for Rhodes to Thessalonica, to Metrodorus, instructing them to hold themselves ready for the voyage. That embassy also succeeded in persuading the Rhodians to join the war.

After arranging these matters, he sent Herophon as envoy to Eumenes, a man whom he had employed on such missions before, and Telemnastus the Cretan to Antiochus,

telling him not to overlook the opportunity and not to suppose that Roman arrogance and harshness concerned Perseus alone,

but to understand clearly that unless he too now took a hand, preferably by ending the war, but if not by helping Perseus, he would soon experience the same fortune himself.

## Book 29 Chapter 5: Difficulty of Explaining the Intrigues of Perseus and Eumenes

In dealing with this matter I was at a loss what I ought to do. For to write in detail and with exact precision about such negotiations as the kings carried on secretly with one another seemed open to criticism and altogether unsafe,

while on the other hand to pass over completely what appeared to have been the most decisive element in this war, and the thing by which many of the later difficulties found their causes made clear, seemed to me a sign of sheer idleness and total lack of courage.

And so I was led to write briefly what seemed likely to be true, and the probabilities and indications by which I came to this judgment, since I was present at the time and was more struck than most people by each of the events that took place.

## Book 29 Chapter 6: Reasons to Suspect Intrigue between Eumenes and Perseus

I have already stated that Cydas the Cretan, while serving in Eumenes' army and being highly honored by him, first met with Cheimarus, one of the Cretans serving with Perseus, near Amphipolis, and then, when he came close to the wall at Demetrias, spoke first with Menecrates and then with Antimachus.

And the fact that Herophon twice went as an envoy from Perseus to Eumenes, and that on that account most of the Romans came to hold a not unreasonable suspicion about King Eumenes, is clear from what happened in the case of Attalus:

for they allowed him to come to Rome from Brundisium and to conduct whatever business he wished, and in the end they gave him a friendly reply and sent him away,

although he had done them nothing noteworthy, either before or during the war with Perseus;

whereas Eumenes, who had provided them with the greatest services and cooperated with them most of all in the wars against Antiochus and Perseus, they not only prevented from going to Rome, but also ordered him to leave Italy within a fixed number of days, though it was midwinter.

From this it is plain that some sort of intrigue had taken place between Perseus and Eumenes, and that because of it the Romans had become so alienated from Eumenes.

What this intrigue was, and how far it went, is what we must now examine.

## Book 29 Chapter 7: Eumenes Intrigues With Perseus

It is easy to see that Eumenes would not have wished Perseus to prevail in the war and become master of everything.

Quite apart from the inherited enmity and ill will they had toward each other, the similarity of their positions was enough in itself to create distrust, jealousy, and, in general, the deepest hostility between them.

What remained was for them to deceive and outscheme each other through secret dealings; and that is what both of them did.

For when Eumenes saw Perseus in difficulty, hemmed in on every side and willing to accept any terms at all in order to end the war, and when he saw him sending embassies year after year to the Roman generals about this business,

and when he saw the Romans themselves dissatisfied with the whole course of events—partly because the war had made no progress until Paulus took command, and partly because affairs in Aetolia were in an unsettled state—

he thought it quite possible that the Romans might consent to some settlement and bring the war to an end.

And he believed that he himself was especially well suited to act as mediator and bring this about.

With these ideas in mind, he began the year before to test Perseus through Cydas the Cretan, asking how much he would be willing to pay for such a chance.

## Book 29 Chapter 8: Struggle between Eumenes and Perseus

This, I think, was the beginning of their connection.

And when one compares the two men, one the most unscrupulous and the other the most money-loving, their contest naturally became ridiculous.

For Eumenes kept holding out every hope and every kind of bait, convinced that he would catch Perseus with promises;

while Perseus rushed eagerly toward what was held out to him, yet could never bring himself to swallow it far enough to part with any money of his own.

The bargain they haggled over was this: Eumenes asked for five hundred talents as the price of standing idle in the fourth year and not cooperating with the Romans, either on land or at sea; and for ending the war altogether he demanded fifteen hundred, promising at once to give hostages and guarantees.

Perseus accepted the proposal about hostages, naming how many were to be sent, when they were to be sent, and how they were to be kept safe at Cnosus.

But as for the money, he said it would be shameful both for the giver and even more for the receiver to appear to remain neutral for pay; however, he said he would send the fifteen hundred talents by Polemocrates and his companions to Samothrace, where they could be deposited.

Since he himself was master of Samothrace, while Eumenes, like a bad doctor, preferred a retainer to payment after the treatment, he at last gave up the attempt when he found that his own cleverness was no match for Perseus' meanness.

So they parted on even terms, like good wrestlers leaving the contest of greed a draw.

Some of these details became known immediately, others later reached Perseus' close friends; and from them we were enabled to learn that avarice is, so to speak, the fastening-pin of every vice.

## Book 29 Chapter 9: The Avarice of Perseus

I will add one further point of my own: avarice not only does harm, it also makes men foolish.

For who would not remark the ignorance of both kings? How on earth did Eumenes imagine that, in spite of the deep hostility between them, he would be trusted and would receive such a large sum of money, when he could give Perseus no sufficient guarantee that it would be returned if he failed to carry out his promises?

And how could he suppose that, after taking so large a sum, he would escape the notice of the Romans?

Even if he had managed to hide it at the time, he would certainly not have escaped discovery later.

At the very least, he would necessarily have had to exchange his quarrel with Rome for the money he received; and because of that he was about to lose both the money itself and his kingdom, and very likely his life as well, once he appeared as an enemy of the Romans.

For even as things stood—when he had done nothing, but had merely entertained the scheme—he was brought into the gravest danger; what, then, ought one suppose would have happened to him if the plan had been carried through to completion?

And again, who would not marvel at Perseus for thinking anything more profitable or more urgent than to give the money and let Eumenes swallow the bait?

For if Eumenes had carried out even part of his promises and ended the war, the payment would have been well spent.

But if that hope had failed, Perseus would at least have thrown him into open hostility with Rome; for Perseus himself had complete control over making the whole transaction public.

And how valuable that would have been to Perseus, whether he succeeded in the war or failed, is easy to see:

he would have regarded Eumenes as the guilty cause of all his misfortunes, and there was no more effective way he could have avenged himself on him than by making him an enemy of Rome.

So what was the cause of such obvious folly? Avarice. What else could one say?

One man, in order to get what did not belong to him, overlooked everything else and was ready to do anything;

while the other, in order not to give anything away, was willing to endure anything and to ignore everything else.

Accordingly, Perseus' dealings with the Gauls and with Genthius were of a piece with this—

## Book 29 Chapter 10: Rhodes Decides to Seek Peace

When the vote was put before the Rhodians, the proposal to send envoys to negotiate a settlement won the day.

This was how the council settled the Rhodian opposition, as I have explained in my discussion of public speaking.

In that division, the men who sided with Perseus proved stronger than those who were trying to save their country and its laws.

The prytaneis immediately appointed envoys to bring the war to an end: Agepolis, Diocles, and Cleombrotus were sent to Rome; Damon, Nicostratus, Agesilochus, and Telephus to the general and to Perseus.

But they did not stop there. They went on in the same course and added to it, making their offence beyond excuse:

for they at once sent envoys to Crete, to renew their former friendly relations with all the Cretans and to urge them to look at the times and the danger, to stand with the people of Rhodes, and to choose the same enemies and friends as they did;

and they planned to speak in the same way in private to the individual cities.

## Book 29 Chapter 11: Turbulent Assembly at Rhodes

When the envoys from Genthius, together with those led by Parmenio and Morcus and accompanied by Metrodorus, arrived at Rhodes, and the council had been summoned,

the assembly was in a complete uproar. The men around Deinus and Polyaratus were now openly daring to speak on Perseus' behalf, while the party around Theaedetus was stunned by what was happening.

For the arrival of the galleys, the number of cavalry that had been lost, and Genthius' defection crushed them.

So the outcome of the assembly followed naturally from what has just been said:

the Rhodians voted to give both kings a friendly answer, to tell them that they had decided to put an end to the war, and to urge each of them not to make difficulties over peace terms.

They also received Genthius' envoys at the common hearth with great friendliness.

## Book 29 Chapter 12: On Proportion in History

Again, other writers have gone on at length about the Syrian war. The reason for this is what I have already explained more than once.

For when men take a simple and straightforward subject and yet want not the facts but the bulk of their books to make them seem like historians, and want that sort of display to create the impression of importance, it becomes necessary for them to make small things big,

and to dress up matters that were briefly stated, and in some cases to turn incidental happenings into full-scale actions and affairs—staging contests and announcing battle lines in which sometimes ten infantrymen fell, sometimes only a little more, and cavalry even fewer than that.

As for sieges, geography, and similar topics, no one could say their treatment is adequate, so far as they develop them at all, because they have no material to work with.

But the opposite is true for those who write universal history:

and so one should not blame us as if we were dragging out events when we sometimes pass over things that others have given great attention and embellishment, and sometimes mention them only briefly; rather, one should trust that we give each subject the treatment it deserves.

For when those writers describe, somewhere in the course of their whole work, the siege of Phanoteia or Coroneia or Haliartus, they are forced to set out every contrivance, every bold move, and every detail of the siege,

and beyond that to dwell on the capture of Tarentum, Corinth, Sardis, Gaza, Bactra, and, above all, Carthage, adding details of their own; yet they are not at all satisfied if we give a plain and truthful account of such matters.

The same judgment should apply to our treatment of battles and speeches, and likewise to the other parts of history:

in all these matters we would fairly deserve much forgiveness, and the same is true of what I am about to narrate, even if I should seem inconsistent in my subject matter, in my handling of events, or in my style of expression;

and also if I should sometimes slip in the names of mountains, rivers, or local places. The size of the undertaking itself is excuse enough in all these matters,

unless I am caught deliberately making false statements for gain; for that I ask no pardon, as I have repeatedly and expressly said in this work.

## Book 29 Chapter 13: Character of Genthius

In the twenty-ninth book, the same writer says that Genthius, king of the Illyrians, committed many shameful acts in his life because of his heavy drinking, being drunk night and day alike:

and after killing his brother Plator, who was about to marry the daughter of Monunius, he himself married the girl and treated his subjects with savage cruelty.

## Book 29 Chapter 14: Scipio Nasica and Fabius Maximus Volunteer to Outflank the Macedonians

The first man to volunteer to lead the outflanking movement was Nasica, also called Scipio, the son-in-law of Scipio Africanus; later he became a man of great influence in the Senate.

The second was Fabius Maximus, the oldest of Aemilius' sons, still only a youth, who stood up eagerly.

Aemilius was pleased and gave them not as many men as Polybius says, but as many as Nasica himself says he received, in a letter he wrote to one of the kings, about these events.

The Romans were holding their own stoutly against the force of the parma shields and the Ligurian shields.

## Book 29 Chapter 15: A Cretan Deserter Brings Intelligence to Perseus

Meanwhile Perseus, seeing that Aemilius was staying still and not reckoning with what was happening, when a Cretan deserter slipped away from the road and came to him with news of the Roman outflanking move,

was thrown into confusion. He did not move his camp, but handed over ten thousand mercenary foreigners and two thousand Macedonians to Milo and sent them off, urging them to hurry and seize the mountain passes.

Polybius says that the Romans fell on them while they were still asleep, but Nasica says there was a sharp fight and real danger on the heights.

## Book 29 Chapter 16: Battle of Pydna

Polybius says that when the moon was eclipsed, the rumor spread among many of Perseus' Macedonians that it meant the king himself was being eclipsed.

This made the Romans bolder and brought the Macedonians low in spirit.

So true is the saying that war is full of empty alarms.

## Book 29 Chapter 17: Perseus Loses His Resolve

Lucius, though he had never seen a phalanx at all until he saw one in Perseus' army, used to tell people in Rome afterward that he had never seen anything more frightening and formidable than a Macedonian phalanx, even though he had seen and taken part in many battles, like anyone else.

Many schemes seem plausible and possible in theory, but when they come to practical use, like counterfeit coins thrown into the fire, they no longer live up to their first design.

Perseus, with only one thought in mind — either to win or to die — did not hold steady in his spirit at that moment, but lost heart, like cavalry scouts who break and run.

As time went on and the strain grew heavier, Perseus' spirit gave way, like athletes in poor condition; for when danger drew near and everything had to be decided, he could not keep his courage firm.

## Book 29 Chapter 18: Ambitious Youth

The Macedonian king, as Polybius says, lost his nerve just as the battle was getting under way and rode off to the city, pretending that he was sacrificing to Heracles — fitting cowardly rites for a cowardly man, and prayers that the god would not accept.

## Book 29 Chapter 19: The Senate Makes an Example of the Rhodian Ambassadors

At the time when Perseus, after his defeat, was making his escape, the senate decided to summon the Rhodian ambassadors, who had come to ask for an end to the war.

Fortune seemed, as if by design, to put the Rhodians’ ignorance on display before the stage — if one should call it ignorance of the Rhodians, and not rather of the men who were then dominant at Rhodes.

When the party led by Hegesipolis entered, they said that they had come to end the war. For the Rhodian people, seeing that the war had dragged on for a long time, and seeing that it was harmful to all the Greeks and also to the Romans themselves because of the enormous expense, had come to that view.

But now that the war had ended according to the Rhodians’ wishes, they had come simply to congratulate the Romans. Such, briefly, was what the men around Hegesipolis said.

The senate, taking advantage of the moment and wanting to make an example of the Rhodians, delivered a reply whose main point was this:

They did not think that this embassy had been sent by the Rhodians for the sake either of the Greeks or of themselves, but for Perseus.

For if they had been sent on behalf of the Greeks, the more fitting time would have been when Perseus was ravaging Greek territory and cities, while encamped in Thessaly for almost two years.

But to let that time pass by, and now to come eagerly seeking an end to the war, when our armies had entered Macedonia and Perseus was shut in with only the faintest hope of survival, made it plain to anyone who judged matters rightly that they had sent their embassy not to end the war, but to rescue Perseus and save him as far as they could.

For that reason, they said, the Romans were under no obligation at present either to show them favor or to answer them kindly.

That was the senate’s decision for the Rhodian ambassadors.

## Book 29 Chapter 20: Perseus an Example of the Impermanence of Fortune

Then the man who took up the Roman language addressed those in the senate, telling them to look at what was before their eyes and, with Perseus standing there in view, not to boast excessively about successes, not to plot anything arrogant or ruinous against anyone, and in general never to trust present good fortune.

Rather, he said, it was precisely when a man was enjoying the greatest success, whether in private life or in public affairs, that he should be most mindful of the possibility of a reversal.

For only in that way could a person show moderation even amid prosperity.

He said that this was what distinguished foolish men from sensible ones: the one class learn only from their own misfortunes, the other from the misfortunes of others.

## Book 29 Chapter 21: Uncertainties of Fortune

That is why one is often reminded of the words of Demetrius of Phalerum.

In his treatise on Fortune, wishing to show people clearly how changeable Fortune is, he began with the age of Alexander, when Alexander brought down the Persian empire, and said this:

"If you were to keep in mind not an endless time or many generations, but only the fifty years immediately before our own day, you would recognize the harshness of Fortune here.

For would you have believed, fifty years earlier, if any god had foretold it, that by this time not even the name of the Persians would remain at all — those who had been masters of almost the whole inhabited world — while the Macedonians, whose name was scarcely known before, would become masters of it all?

And yet Fortune, whose action in our lives is unpredictable, who is always making new things out of what lies beyond our reckoning and showing her power through the unexpected, is even now, in my view, making this plain to all men:

she has brought the Macedonians into the prosperity once enjoyed by the Persians, because she has used these blessings with them too, until she decides something else about them.

"And that is what has now happened in the case of Perseus. So Demetrius, as though inspired by some divine mouth, has spoken prophetically about the future.

As for myself, when I came in my narrative to the time at which the kingdom of Macedon was destroyed, I did not think it right to pass the moment by without comment, since I had myself witnessed the event; rather, I wanted both to speak fittingly on the matter and to recall Demetrius’s words.

For it seems to me that he made a statement more divine than merely human, since he had foretold what would happen almost a hundred and fifty years beforehand."

## Book 29 Chapter 22: Further Problems for Eumenes

After the battle between Perseus and the Romans had been brought to an end, King Eumenes fell into what seemed, to most people, a strange and unexpected situation; though in truth, if one looks at the course human affairs usually take, it was nothing out of the ordinary.

For Fortune is quite able to upset reasoned plans, and even when she has helped a man and added her own weight to his side, she can turn around again, as if repenting, and immediately spoil what has been achieved.

That is exactly what happened then in Eumenes' case.

For just when he believed that his own rule was now secure and that the coming years would be easy, since Perseus and the whole Macedonian kingdom had been utterly overthrown, he suddenly ran into the gravest danger, because the Galatians in Asia unexpectedly took advantage of the moment and rose up.

## Book 29 Chapter 23: The Ptolemies Ask Help From Achaia

In the Peloponnese, during the winter, ambassadors arrived from both kings — Ptolemy and Ptolemy — asking for help, and this led to repeated and highly spirited debates.

The party around Callicrates, Diophanes, and Hyperbatus did not like the idea of granting aid.

But Archon, Lycortas, and Polybius thought it right to give the kings help in accordance with the existing alliance.

For by then it had happened that the younger Ptolemy had been proclaimed king by the crowd because of the crisis, while the elder had come back from Memphis and was ruling jointly with his brother.

Since they were asking for every kind of support, they sent Eumenes and Dionysodorus to the Achaeans, requesting one thousand foot soldiers and two hundred cavalry, with Lycortas to command the infantry and Polybius the cavalry.

They also sent word to Theodoridas of Sicyon, asking him to raise a body of one thousand mercenaries.

This happened because the kings had previously formed a close acquaintance with the men we have mentioned, as a result of the events already described.

When the envoys arrived, the Achaean congress was meeting at Corinth. They accordingly came forward and, after recalling the many proofs of goodwill the Achaeans had shown toward the Egyptian kingdom and setting out the danger the kings were then in, they asked them for help.

The majority of the Achaeans were ready to go to the kings' assistance — both because the kings at that time wore the diadem and exercised royal authority — and not merely with a detachment, but with their full levy; for the party around Callicrates objected,

saying that in general they should not meddle in such matters, and certainly not at that time, but should keep themselves entirely available for Roman needs.

For at that very moment there was especially expected to be a crisis over the whole situation, since Quintus Philippus was wintering in Macedonia.

## Book 29 Chapter 24: The Achaeans Agree to Help the Ptolemies

When many of the people became uncertain and feared that they might seem to fall short of Roman expectations, Lycortas and Polybius took up the argument again and taught them,

among other things, that in the previous year the Achaeans had voted to march with their full levy alongside the Romans and had sent Polybius as envoy, but Quintus, after accepting their goodwill, had declined the help, saying that he had no need of it because he had already secured the invasion route into Macedonia.

From this it was clear that their supposed concern for Roman needs was only a pretext for preventing aid from being sent.

So they urged the Achaeans not to overlook the present moment, seeing how great the danger then surrounding the kingdom was, but to keep faith with the agreements and benefactions, and above all with the oaths, by carrying out the treaty.

But when the majority again became inclined toward sending help, the party around Callicrates blocked the motion, intimidating the magistrates by claiming that under the laws it was not lawful to debate aid in the public assembly.

After some time, however, when the congress had been summoned to the city of the Sicyonians, where not only the council but all men over thirty were present, and after several speeches had been made,

Polybius in particular argued first that the Romans did not need help — and he seemed to be speaking not without good reason, since he had spent the previous summer in Macedonia in the camp of Philippus —

and second, that even if the Romans did need the alliance, the Achaeans would still not be unable to provide help because of the two hundred cavalry and one thousand infantry who were to be sent to Alexandria; for if they were acting wisely, they could field thirty or forty thousand fighting men.

The majority were won over by what they heard and leaned toward sending the allied force.

So on the second of the two days, on which by law those who wished to do so were required to present motions, Lycortas proposed that the aid should be sent, while Callicrates proposed that ambassadors should be dispatched to reconcile the two kings with Antiochus.

When these motions were put forward again, a lively contest followed, but the men around Lycortas clearly had the advantage.

For the comparison between the two kingdoms was very striking.

It was hard to find any act of friendship at all between Syria and the Greeks in earlier times, although the present king's generosity was well known among them;

whereas from Egypt there had been so many and such great acts of kindness toward the Achaeans in earlier times that no one could ask for more.

By presenting this contrast, Lycortas made a strong impression, since the difference between the two kingdoms on this point was so great.

For just as it was not easy to count the benefactions of the Alexandrian kings, so it was impossible to find any act of goodwill done by the dynasty of Antiochus for the Achaeans in the course of affairs.

## Book 29 Chapter 25: Antiochus Forced To Leave Egypt

For a time Andronidas and Callicrates kept using arguments in favor of making peace, but since no one was listening to them, they resorted to a trick.

A letter-carrier happened to come from the road into the theater, bringing a letter from Quintus Marcius, in which he urged the Achaeans, in line with Roman policy, to try to reconcile the kings.

This was also in fact what the Senate had done, sending envoys with Numisius to bring about a settlement between the kings.

But this actually told against their case: for the men with Titus had failed to achieve reconciliation and had returned to Rome completely unsuccessful.

Still, Polybius and his faction did not want to argue against the letter, out of consideration for Marcius, and withdrew from the debate. Thus the proposal to help the kings came to nothing.

The Achaeans then decided to send ambassadors to make the reconciliation, and Archon of Aegira, Arcesilaus, and Ariston of Megalopolis were appointed.

The envoys from Ptolemy, having failed to secure the alliance, then handed over to the magistrates the letter from the kings, which asked the Achaeans to send Lycortas and Polybius to take part in the present war.

## Book 29 Chapter 26: Antiochus Renews the War

Forgetting what he himself had written and said, Antiochus resumed the war against Ptolemy.

So true are Simonides' words: "It is hard to be good."

For it is easy enough to have impulses toward what is noble, and even to hold to them for a while; but to remain steady through every trial, and never to put anything above honor and justice, is difficult indeed.

## Book 29 Chapter 27: Popilius Makes Antiochus Stop the War

When Antiochus had come up with the intention of seizing Pelusium from Ptolemy,

the Roman commander Popilius met him. The king greeted him from a distance by voice and extended his right hand; but Popilius, having the tablet ready at hand on which the Senate's decree was written, held it out to him and ordered Antiochus to read it first,

as I think, because he did not consider it right to exchange the sign of friendship before he knew the attitude of the man receiving him — whether he was to be a friend or an enemy.

When the king had read it and said that he wished to consult his friends about the matter, Popilius did something that seemed harsh and entirely overbearing:

with the vine-rod he happened to have in his hand, he drew a circle around Antiochus and ordered him to give his answer about the contents of the letter before stepping outside that ring.

The king, startled by what had happened and by the display of authority, hesitated briefly and then said that he would do everything the Romans asked. Then Popilius and his companions took his hand and all greeted him warmly.

The order contained in the letter was that the war with Ptolemy should be ended at once.

So a fixed number of days was granted him, and within that time he withdrew his forces into Syria, in pain and anger, though at present submitting to the situation.

Popilius and his companions then set matters in order at Alexandria and urged the kings to live in peace with one another; at the same time they ordered them to send Polyaratus back to Rome.

Then they sailed off to Cyprus, intending also to drive the forces stationed there out of the island without delay.

When they arrived and found that Ptolemy's generals had been defeated in battle and that the situation in Cyprus in general was in confusion, they quickly had the army withdraw from the country and remained there until the forces had sailed off to Syria.

In this way the Romans preserved Ptolemy's kingdom, which was all but ruined.

Thus Fortune so shaped the affairs of Perseus and the Macedonians that the outcome in Alexandria and in the whole of Egypt was decided by it: that is, by the earlier decision of what should be done with Perseus.

For if that had not taken place and been settled, I do not think Antiochus would have obeyed the orders given him.
