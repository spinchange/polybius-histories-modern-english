# Polybius — The Histories, Book XIV

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 14 Chapter 1: Scipio Plans To Attack the Punic Camp

II. Scipio’s Actions in Africa

While the consuls were occupied with these matters,

Publius in Libya, during the winter quarters, learned that the Carthaginians were equipping a fleet. He therefore gave attention both to that preparation and no less to the siege of Utica.

Yet he had not completely abandoned hope in regard to Syphax. Since their forces were not far apart, he kept sending messages, convinced that he would draw him away from the alliance with the Carthaginians.

For he did not despair that the king had already begun to feel satiety with the girl for whose sake he had chosen the Carthaginian side, and in general with his friendship for the Phoenicians, because of the natural fickleness of the Numidians and their disregard alike for gods and men.

But since his mind was occupied with many concerns and he entertained shifting hopes about the future, while dreading the danger before him because the enemy were many times more numerous, he seized on the following opportunity.

Some of the men sent to Syphax reported to him that the Carthaginians in their winter camp had made their huts from all kinds of timber and leafy brush, without earth,

while the Numidians made theirs from reeds, and the reinforcements now gathering from the towns were for the present building shelters from foliage alone, some inside the ditch and stockade, but most of them outside.

Publius therefore concluded that an attack by fire would be, for the enemy, the most unexpected course possible, and for his own side the most practical. So he turned his attention to preparing this plan.

In his exchanges with Publius, Syphax always came back to the same proposal: that the Carthaginians should withdraw from Italy, the Romans likewise from Libya, and that each side should keep whatever lay between those regions as they then held it.

Previously Publius would not tolerate even hearing of this. But now, through the men sent to him, he gave the Numidian a slight indication that the proposal he was making was not impossible.

As a result, Syphax, greatly relieved, became much bolder in pressing the matter.

So the number of messengers passing back and forth increased, and they came more frequently; sometimes they even stayed several days with one another without exciting suspicion.

On those occasions Publius always sent along with the envoys certain practical and military men, rough-looking and lowly, dressed in slave clothing, so that they might safely reconnoiter and inspect the approaches and entrances to each camp.

For there were two encampments: one, held by Hasdrubal, with thirty thousand infantry and three thousand cavalry; the other, about ten stades away, was the Numidian camp, with ten thousand horse and about fifty thousand foot.

This latter camp was easier to approach and its huts were especially fit to be burned, because the Numidians, as I said just now, used neither timber nor earth in building them, but simply canes and reeds.

## Book 14 Chapter 1a: Preface

I. From the Preface

Polybius says of himself and of the plan of his books: perhaps, then, the summaries prefixed to each Olympiad bring readers to understanding both because of the number and because of the greatness of the events, since deeds from the whole inhabited world are being brought under one general view.

Yet I think the events of this Olympiad especially will make an impression on readers, first because in this period the wars in Italy and Libya reached their conclusion. And who would not wish to learn what sort of outcome and ending they had?

For by nature all people, even if they gladly follow each separate action and speech in detail, nevertheless long to know the end of each story.

Besides this, it happened that at the same time the intentions of the kings became especially plain. For what had previously been said about them as rumor was then clearly recognized by everyone, even by those who had no desire at all to pry into public affairs.

Therefore, wishing to make the exposition proportionate to the importance of the events, I have not arranged the actions of those two years into a single book, as I did in the earlier parts of the work.

## Book 14 Chapter 2: Scipio Prepares to Attack Utica by Sea

When the season of spring was already beginning to show itself, and Scipio had fully investigated everything concerning the enemy for the attack just mentioned,

he launched his ships and fitted them with engines as though he intended to besiege Utica from the sea.

With about two thousand infantry he once more occupied the hill above the city and strongly fortified it with elaborate trenchworks,

making the enemy think he was doing this for the sake of the siege, though in truth he wanted to place a reserve there for the crucial moment of the operation, so that when the camps were separated from the Roman position, the soldiers watching Utica might not dare to sally out from the city, attack the nearby palisade, and besiege those guarding it.

While he was making these preparations, he also sent repeatedly to Syphax, asking whether, if he accepted the proposals being made, the Carthaginians too would be content with them, or whether they would once again say that they must deliberate about the concessions.

At the same time he instructed the envoys not to return to him before they had received an answer on these points.

When they arrived and delivered the message, the Numidian was persuaded that Scipio really meant to bring the negotiations to completion, both because the envoys said they would not depart before receiving his reply, and because Scipio showed concern about the Carthaginians’ assent.

Accordingly Syphax at once sent to Hasdrubal, explaining what had happened and urging him to accept peace; and he himself grew careless, allowing the Numidians who were joining him to camp outside his entrenchment.

Publius, for appearances, behaved in much the same way; in reality, however, he was pressing his preparations with the utmost energy.

When the Carthaginians informed Syphax that they agreed to conclude matters according to the terms, and the Numidian, delighted, communicated this to the envoys, the envoys immediately departed for their own camp to report to Publius what the king had done.

On hearing this, the Roman commander at once sent another embassy to Syphax to tell him that Publius himself approved and was eager for peace, but that the members of his council disagreed and insisted on standing by the existing position.

When they arrived, they conveyed this message to the Numidian.

Scipio made this embassy in order not to seem to be violating the truce, if, while the heralded negotiations for peace were still continuing between the two sides, he should undertake some act of war.

Once this declaration had been made, he believed that whatever he went on to do would be beyond reproach.

## Book 14 Chapter 3: Syphax and Hasdrubal Are Deluded

Syphax heard this with irritation, because he had already formed strong hopes about the peace; but he went to confer with Hasdrubal and explained the message brought him from the Romans.

After much perplexed discussion, they considered how they ought to handle what came next, being very far in their thoughts and plans from what was actually about to happen.

For they had no suspicion at all that they needed to take precautions or that some terrible blow was hanging over them; rather, they were full of eagerness to do something themselves and to draw the enemy down onto level ground.

At this time Publius, by his preparations and his orders, led the mass of the army to suppose that he had some operation in hand against Utica,

but, summoning at midday the ablest and most trustworthy tribunes, he disclosed the plan to them and ordered them, after taking supper at the proper hour, to lead the forces outside the palisade as soon as all the trumpeters gave the usual signal together.

For among the Romans it is the custom at supper time for all the buglers and trumpeters to sound near the general’s tent, so that the night watches may then take up their posts in the usual places.

After this he called back the scouts whom he had been sending into the enemy camps and compared and examined their reports about the approaches and entrances to the encampments, using Massanissa, because of his knowledge of the country, as both judge of what they said and adviser.

## Book 14 Chapter 4: The Romans Burn the Enemy Camp

When everything needed for the present operation was ready, he left in the camp men sufficient and suitable for its defense, took up his forces, and advanced just as the first watch was ending, for the enemy were about sixty stades away.

When he drew near the enemy, around the close of the third watch, he assigned half the soldiers and all the Numidians to Gaius Laelius and Massanissa, ordering them to attack Syphax’s palisade,

and exhorting them to prove themselves brave men and do nothing rashly, knowing clearly that, in proportion as darkness hinders and blocks the use of sight, night attacks require that one make up the loss by intelligence and courage.

He himself took the rest of the army and advanced against Hasdrubal. His calculation was not to begin the attack until Laelius and his force had first thrown fire among the enemy.

So, with these intentions, he advanced at a measured pace. Meanwhile the force under Laelius divided itself into two parts and attacked the enemy at the same time.

Since the arrangement of the huts was, as I said earlier, almost as though purposely designed for burning, the moment the leading men cast in the fire and it spread among the first tents, the disaster at once became uncontrollable because the huts stood so close together and because of the mass of combustible material beneath them.

Laelius stayed behind in reserve formation; but Massanissa, knowing the places by which those fleeing the fire were likely to retreat, stationed his soldiers at those points.

None of the Numidians had the least suspicion of what was happening—not even Syphax himself—but they took it to be an accidental fire in the palisade.

So some sprang from sleep without any suspicion, while others were still drinking and half drunk as they rushed from the huts.

Many were trampled down by their own people at the camp exits; many, hemmed in by the blaze, were burned alive; and those who did escape the flames all fell into the hands of the enemy and were destroyed, knowing neither what they were suffering nor what they were doing.

## Book 14 Chapter 5: Dreadful Scene In the Burning Camps

At this same moment the Carthaginians, seeing the mass of the fire and the greatness of the flame leaping up, supposed that the Numidians’ palisade had caught fire by accident. Some of them therefore ran straight out to help,

while all the rest poured unarmed out of their camp and gathered before their own encampment, astonished at what was happening.

Scipio, since events were advancing exactly as he could have wished, fell upon those who had come out, killing some and, by chasing others back, at the same time throwing fire into the huts.

Once this happened, the Phoenicians suffered under the fire and the whole surrounding confusion much the same fate as I have just described in the case of the Numidians.

Hasdrubal and those with him at once gave up trying to fight the fire, recognizing from what was happening that the disaster among the Numidians too had not arisen accidentally, as they had thought, but from the enemy’s attack and daring.

So they turned to saving themselves, though even in this there remained only a slight hope.

For the fire spread rapidly and took hold everywhere, while the passages were clogged with horses, pack animals, and men—some half dead and ruined by the fire, others frenzied with terror and driven out of their senses.

Thus these things became obstacles even to those resolved to fight bravely, and amid the panic and confusion escape was hopeless.

The case of Syphax and the other commanders was much the same. Yet both of the principal leaders managed to save themselves with a few horsemen.

But the remaining myriads of men, horses, and pack animals perished miserably and pitifully in the flames.

Some of the men, fleeing the force of the fire, met an ugly and shameful end at the hands of the enemy, being slaughtered naked, deprived not only of their weapons but even of their clothes.

In general the whole place was full of lamentation, disorderly shouting, fear, and a bewildering crash of noises, along with active fire and overwhelming flame.

Any one of these things by itself would have been enough to strike terror into human nature, much more when all of them came together in so astounding a fashion.

Therefore no one alive could, even by exaggeration, adequately match what happened, so completely did this disaster surpass all the events mentioned before in sheer horror.

For that reason, although Scipio accomplished many great and noble deeds, this seems to me the finest and boldest of all his achievements.

## Book 14 Chapter 6: Anda Surrenders to Rome

Yet when day came, and the enemy were either destroyed or in headlong flight, he exhorted the tribunes and at once pressed on in pursuit.

At first the Carthaginian commander stood his ground, even after receiving news of Scipio’s advance, because he trusted in the strength of the city.

But afterward, seeing that the inhabitants were in revolt, and terrified by Scipio’s approach, he fled with those who had escaped—no fewer than five hundred cavalry and about two thousand infantry.

The native inhabitants, now of one mind, put themselves in Roman hands.

Publius spared them, but he allowed the two neighboring cities to be plundered by the troops; and when he had accomplished this, he returned again to his original camp.

The Carthaginians, since their hopes had turned out contrary to the plans with which they had begun, took what had happened very hard.

For they had expected to shut up the Romans on the headland near Utica, where they had spent the winter, and to besiege them there, using their land forces on the one side and their naval forces on the other; and for this purpose they had prepared everything.

But now they found that not only had they unexpectedly and absurdly yielded the open country to the enemy, they were also brought to expect an imminent danger for themselves and their fatherland; therefore they were utterly stricken and panic-filled in spirit.

Still, since circumstances compelled them to make some provision and take counsel for the future, the council chamber was filled with bewilderment and with varied, troubled schemes.

Some said that they must send for Hannibal and recall him from Italy, since their one remaining hope lay in that general and the forces under him.

Others urged that envoys should be sent to Publius to discuss an armistice and speak about peace terms and a treaty, while others said they must keep heart, gather their forces, and send to Syphax.

For, they said, he had withdrawn nearby to Abba and was gathering together those who had escaped the danger. In the end this last opinion prevailed.

So they set about collecting forces, sent out Hasdrubal, and dispatched messengers to Syphax, begging him to assist them and to hold to the original plan, since a general with forces would very soon join him ...

## Book 14 Chapter 7: Carthaginians Reinforced and Resolute

... and link up with him. The Roman commander meanwhile continued to work at the siege of Utica; but for the most part, hearing that Syphax was holding firm and that the Carthaginians were again gathering an army, he led out his forces and encamped in front of Utica.

At the same time, after distributing the spoils ...

... he sent off the merchants ...

... on profitable terms. For since the recent success held out a fine hope about the whole result, the soldiers made little account of their present gain and readily disposed of it to the merchants.

As for the king of the Numidians and his friends, at first they decided to continue their retreat straight home.

But when the Celtiberians met them near Abba—men who had been hired by the Carthaginians and numbered more than four thousand—they put confidence in this reinforcement, halted where they were, and took a little courage in spirit.

At the same time the girl—who was the daughter of the general Hasdrubal and the wife of Syphax, as I said above—begged and pleaded with him to remain and not abandon the Carthaginians at such a crisis. The Numidian was persuaded and agreed to what was asked.

The Celtiberians also gave the Carthaginians no small grounds for hope. Instead of four thousand, they reported them to be ten thousand, and said that in battle they were irresistible both in spirit and in equipment.

Lifted up by this report and by the coarse and public chatter that spread everywhere, the Carthaginians were twice as strongly encouraged to try once more for the open country.

Finally, within thirty days, they pitched a camp on the place called the Great Plains together with the Numidians and the Celtiberians, the whole force numbering no fewer than thirty thousand.

## Book 14 Chapter 8: Scipio Determines to Attack

When news of this reached the Roman camp, Publius at once set about marching out. After giving instructions to those besieging Utica and to the force operating by sea about what they ought to do, he advanced with the whole army in light marching order.

On the fifth day he arrived at the Great Plains and, after coming near the enemy, on the first day encamped on a certain hill about thirty stades away from them.

On the next day he descended into the plain and pitched camp seven stades away, posting the cavalry in front.

They remained there for the next two days, testing one another a little by skirmishing attacks; on the fourth day both sides deliberately led out and drew up their forces.

Publius arranged his line in the ordinary Roman fashion, placing first the standards of the hastati, behind them those of the principes, and lastly those of the triarii in the rear.

Of the cavalry he stationed the Italian horse on the right and the Numidians with Massanissa on the left.

Syphax and Hasdrubal placed the Celtiberians in the center opposite the Roman infantry formations, the Numidians on the left wing, and the Carthaginians on the right.

At the very first onset the Numidians gave way before the Italian cavalry, and the Carthaginians before the men under Massanissa, already utterly defeated in spirit by their repeated previous failures.

But the Celtiberians fought bravely when they came to close quarters with the Romans. For they had no hope of safety in flight, because they did not know the country, nor, if taken alive, any hope of being spared because of their faithlessness toward Publius.

For, though they had suffered no injury from him during the operations in Iberia, they seemed to have come unjustly and in violation of good faith to aid the Carthaginians against the Romans.

Nevertheless, as soon as the troops on the wings had broken, they were quickly surrounded by the principes and triarii and all cut down except for a very few.

Thus the Celtiberians perished, but not before rendering great service to the Carthaginians, not only during the battle itself but also during the retreat.

For if they had not hindered the Romans, and if the Romans had immediately pressed on after the fugitives, only a very few of the enemy would have escaped altogether.

But as it was, because the Romans were delayed by dealing with them, Syphax and his cavalry withdrew safely to their own country, while Hasdrubal escaped with those saved from the disaster to Carthage.

## Book 14 Chapter 9: Scipio's Victory On the Great Plains

When the Roman commander had made proper arrangements about the spoil and the prisoners, he summoned the council and deliberated about what ought to be done next.

It was resolved that the commander, Publius, should remain with part of the force and move through the cities, while Laelius and Massanissa, taking the Numidians and part of the Roman army, should pursue Syphax and give him no time to recover his wits or prepare.

Having reached this decision, they separated, the one party going against Syphax with the forces mentioned, while the commander himself went against the cities.

Some of these attached themselves voluntarily to the Romans out of fear; others he besieged and took by storm.

Indeed, the whole country was ripe for a change, since it had long been exposed without interruption to hardships and exactions, because the wars in Iberia had lasted so many years.

In Carthage, where there had already been great disorder, an even greater upheaval now followed, since they had suffered so severe a blow for the second time and had given up the hopes they had previously entertained.

Still, those among the advisers who seemed the most manly urged that they should now put to sea with the fleet against those besieging Utica, attempt to raise the siege, and fight a naval battle with the enemy, who were unprepared in that respect.

They also argued that men should be sent to Hannibal and that they should omit no extreme measure in testing even this hope, since both enterprises offered, on reasonable grounds, great opportunities for saving themselves.

Others said the times no longer allowed such measures, and that they must fortify the city and prepare for a siege; for chance, if only they remained united, would offer them many opportunities.

At the same time they advised deliberating about peace and treaty terms—on what conditions and by what means they might free themselves from their present troubles.

And after many more speeches on these matters, they ratified ...

## Book 14 Chapter 10: Scipio Fears a Carthaginian Attack on the Fleet

... all the proposals together. After these decisions were made, those who were to sail to Italy went straight from the council chamber down to the sea, and the admiral went to the ships; the rest took thought for the security of the city and kept continually deliberating about details.

Publius meanwhile, as his camp was already filling up with booty because no one resisted and everyone yielded to his attacks, decided to send the greater part of the plunder back to his original camp.

He himself took the army in light marching order and moved to seize the entrenchment at Tunes, so that he might encamp within sight of the Carthaginians; for he thought that in this way he would frighten and overawe them most of all.

A few days later the Carthaginians, with their crews and provisions ready aboard ship, were putting to sea to carry out their plan, when Publius came to Tunes and, the guards fleeing from his attack, occupied the place.

Tunes lies about one hundred and twenty stades from Carthage and is visible from nearly the whole city, besides being strong both by nature and by fortification, as I have said before.

Just as the Romans had pitched camp there, the Carthaginians set sail for Utica.

Publius, seeing the enemy sailing up and fearing that something might happen to his own fleet, was thrown into anxiety, since everyone there was caught completely off guard and wholly unprepared for what was coming.

So he immediately broke camp again and hurried back to assist his own side.

When he found that the decked ships were well and properly equipped for hauling and bringing up engines and in general for siege operations, but were least of all prepared for a sea battle, whereas the enemy fleet had spent the entire winter fitting itself out for precisely that purpose,

he gave up any thought of sailing out to meet them and fight a naval action. Instead he moored the decked ships together and set the transports around them in lines three and four deep.

Then he took down the masts and yards and lashed the ships forcibly together by means of these,

leaving small intervals, enough for light service craft to sail out and pass through.

## Book 14 Chapter 11: Women at Alexandria

III. Egyptian Affairs

Polybius says in his fourteenth book that Philo became the flatterer of Agathocles, son of Oenanthe and companion of King Philopator.

Polybius also says in the fourteenth book of the Histories that many statues of Cleino, who served Ptolemy Philadelphus as his wine-pourer, were set up throughout Alexandria, dressed in a single tunic and holding a drinking horn in their hands.

And are not the finest houses there, he says, called by the names of Myrtion, Mnesis, and Pothine?

Yet Mnesis was a flute-girl, and so was Pothine; while Myrtion was one of the officially recognized common prostitutes.

And was it not Agathocleia, the mistress of King Ptolemy Philopator, who held power and overturned the whole kingdom?

## Book 14 Chapter 12: Ptolemy Philopator, B.C. 222-205

Perhaps some people may wonder why, although I write the rest of my history year by year, pairing parallel events together, I have in the present case treated the affairs of Egypt over a longer span of time.

I have done this for reasons of the following sort.

King Ptolemy, the one now under discussion, Philopator, after the war over Coele Syria had been brought to an end, turned away from every noble pursuit and gave himself over to a life of dissipation such as I have just described.

Late and only under compulsion from events, he became involved in the war now mentioned, which, apart from the mutual cruelty and lawlessness shown by the two sides, had neither pitched battle, nor sea fight, nor siege, nor anything else worthy of record.

Therefore it seemed to me, the writer, that the narrative would be easier and also clearer for readers if I did not touch year by year on small matters unworthy of serious attention, but instead, by presenting the king’s disposition in one single body, should report on it once for all.
