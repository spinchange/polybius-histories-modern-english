# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXVII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 27 Chapter 1: Affairs In Boeotia: The War with Perseus

Olympiad 152, year 1. The war with Perseus.

At this time envoys arrived: from the Thespians, Lases and Callea with their party, and from Neon, Ismenias.

Lases and his colleagues were placing their own city in Roman hands, while Ismenias was offering all the cities in Boeotia together, as a single body, to the discretion of the commissioners.

This proposal was exactly contrary to the policy of Marcius and his associates; what best suited them was to divide the Boeotians city by city.

So they gladly received and warmly courted Lases and his party, as well as the men from Chaeronea, Lebadea, and all the others who had come from individual cities,

but they made a public example of Ismenias, brushing him off and treating him with contempt.

Indeed some of the exiles joined in attacking him and nearly stoned him to death, if he had not fled for refuge beneath the Roman doorway.

At the same time disturbances and civil conflict were taking place in Thebes.

One side said the city must be entrusted to the Romans, while the men of Coronea and Haliartus, who had gathered in Thebes, still claimed control of affairs and insisted that they must remain in alliance with Perseus.

For a while the two factions were evenly matched. But when Olympichus of Coronea was the first to change sides and declared that they must stand by the Romans, there came a complete swing and reversal in the temper of the crowd,

and first they compelled Dicetas to go as envoy to Marcius and his colleagues to make a defense of the alliance with Perseus.

After that they expelled Neon and Hippias, rushing to their houses and ordering them to defend themselves for the policy they had managed; for these were the men who had arranged the alliance.

Once these men had withdrawn, the Thebans immediately assembled, voted honors and gifts for the Romans, and then ordered the magistrates to put the alliance into effect.

Finally they appointed envoys to hand over the city to the Romans and to bring back their own exiles.

## Book 27 Chapter 2: Romans and Perseus Try to Secure Greece

While these things were being carried through at Thebes, the exiles at Chalcis put forward Pompides and brought charges against Ismenias, Neon, and Dicetas.

Since the mistaken policy of the men already mentioned was plain enough, and since the Romans were strengthening the hand of the exiles,

Hippias and his party were brought into such extreme unpopularity that they even ran the risk of their lives at that very moment from the rush of the crowd, until the Romans provided them with a little security by restraining the mob's attack.

When the Thebans arrived carrying the decrees and honors already mentioned, each matter was answered with rapid effect, since the cities lay very close to one another.

After receiving the Thebans, Marcius and his colleagues praised the city and advised them to restore the exiles to their homes.

They immediately instructed all the cities to send embassies to Rome, each one separately placing itself under Roman protection.

And since everything was turning out according to their intention—for their object was to dissolve the Boeotian nation and to destroy the goodwill of the masses toward the Macedonian royal house—

they sent for Servius from Argos and, leaving him at Chalcis, advanced into the Peloponnese; while Neon, after a few days, withdrew to Macedonia.

Ismenias and Dicetas were then led off to prison, and after some time they took their own lives.

Thus the Boeotian nation, which had preserved its common federal union for a long time and had surprisingly survived many varied crises, was then dissolved and broken up into separate cities, because it had rashly and irrationally chosen the side offered by Perseus, panicking in a childish and thoughtless way.

Aulus and Marcius, when they reached the city of the Argives, conferred with the magistrates of the Achaeans and asked Archon the general to send a thousand soldiers to Chalcis to guard the city until the Roman crossing.

Archon readily complied. So after accomplishing these measures among the Greeks during the winter, and after meeting with Publius, they sailed back to Rome.

## Book 27 Chapter 3: Rhodes Prepares to Assist Rome

At the same period Tiberius, Postumius, and Junius were making a tour of the islands and the cities in Asia and spent the greatest part of their time in Rhodes,

though in those days the Rhodians had no need of supervision.

For Agesilochus, who was then serving as prytanis, a man of high standing who later also went on embassy to Rome, had even earlier, as soon as it became clear that the Romans were about to make war on Perseus, urged the people in other respects as well to share the same hopes as Rome, and had advised the Rhodians to put forty ships in readiness,

so that, if some need should arise from circumstances, they would not still be making preparations at the moment they were called upon, but, already ready, could carry out the decision at once.

By laying these points before the Romans at that time and showing them the preparations before their eyes, he sent the envoys away with the city in high favor. Tiberius and his colleagues, after welcoming the Rhodians' goodwill, carried the report back to Rome.

## Book 27 Chapter 4: Perseus Sends a Statement to the Greeks

After the conference with the Romans and the Greek states, Perseus set down in a letter the whole of his legal case and the arguments spoken on either side,

partly because he supposed he would appear to have the stronger claim in justice, and partly because he wished to test the disposition of each state.

So to the other states he sent the letters by ordinary couriers, but to Rhodes he sent envoys along with them, Antenor and Philip.

When these men arrived, they delivered the written statement to the magistrates; and a few days later, appearing before the council, they urged the Rhodians for the present to remain quiet and watch the course of events.

But if the Romans, contrary to the treaties, should attempt to lay hands on Perseus and the Macedonians, then they were to try to bring about a settlement.

For this, they said, was advantageous to everyone and especially fitting for the Rhodians.

The more they reached after equal speech and frankness, and continually stood forward as champions not only of their own freedom but also of that of the rest of the Greeks, the more they ought to foresee and guard against the opposite policy to the best of their ability.

When the envoys had spoken these and similar words, what they said pleased everyone.

But because the Rhodians were already committed to goodwill toward Rome, and because the sounder judgment prevailed among them, though they received the envoys kindly in all other respects, they insisted in their reply that Perseus must not call on them for anything of such a kind as would make them appear to act against the will of the Romans.

Antenor and his colleagues did not accept that answer; but, satisfied with the rest of the Rhodians' courtesy, they sailed back to Macedonia.

## Book 27 Chapter 5: Perseus Sends Alexander to Boeotia

Perseus learned that some of the cities in Boeotia were still holding fast to their goodwill toward him, and he sent Antigonus, the son of Alexander, as envoy.

When he arrived in Boeotia, he passed over the other cities because he found no opening for closer dealings there,

but he entered Coronea, Thisbae, and also Haliartus, and urged the people to hold fast to their goodwill toward the Macedonians.

They gladly accepted what he said and voted to send envoys to Macedonia, so he sailed away and, on meeting the king, gave him a full report on the situation in Boeotia.

A little later the envoys also arrived and asked him to send help to the cities that had chosen the Macedonian side,

for the Thebans, they said, were pressing them hard and harassing them because they would not agree with them or choose the side of Rome.

When Perseus heard this, he said that because of the truce he could not send military help to anyone at all,

but he urged them generally to defend themselves against the Thebans as best they could, while not making war on the Romans, but remaining quiet.

## Book 27 Chapter 6: The Boeotians and Rhodians

When the Romans had heard the envoys who had come from Asia speak about Rhodes and the other cities, they summoned the envoys sent by Perseus.

Solon and Hippias and their party tried to say something about the whole situation and to appease the senate, but for the most part they defended themselves against the charge of the plot against Eumenes.

When they had finished their defense, the senate, having long since resolved on war, ordered them to leave Rome at once, and all other Macedonians who happened to be staying in Italy to depart within thirty days.

After this they called in the consuls and urged them to seize the moment and not delay.

## Book 27 Chapter 7: War With Perseus Begun

While Gaius was still lying at anchor off Cephallenia, he sent the Rhodians a letter about dispatching ships, entrusting the letter to a certain athletic trainer named Socrates.

When the letter reached Rhodes, Stratocles was serving as prytanis in the second half-year of his term.

When the matter was brought forward for debate, Agathagetes, Rhodophon, Astymedes, and many others thought the ships should be sent and that they should join the war at once from the beginning, without making any pretext.

But Deinon, Polyaratus, and their party, though already displeased by the friendly attitude that had been shown toward the Romans, now put forward King Eumenes as a pretext and began to corrupt the inclination of the masses.

For the Rhodians had long felt suspicion and hostility toward Eumenes, first dating from the war against Pharnaces, when Eumenes was blockading the mouth of the Hellespont to keep ships from sailing into the Black Sea, and the Rhodians intervened and stopped him,

and then, in more recent times, the quarrel had been stirred up again by the Lycian disputes over certain forts and a tract of land that lay on the border of the Rhodian Peraea and was continually being ravaged by troops under Eumenes.

Because of all this they were ready enough to listen to anything said against the king.

So Deinon's party seized on this opportunity and tried to discredit the letter, saying that it had come not from the Romans but from Eumenes, who wanted by every possible means to drag them into the war and saddle the people with needless expense and suffering.

As proof of what they said, they pointed to the fact that the man who had brought the letter was just such a person as this, whereas the Romans were not accustomed to act in that way, but sent communications of this kind with great seriousness and official protection.

They said this even though they knew perfectly well that the letter had in fact been written by Lucretius, because they wanted to teach the masses not to do anything readily for the Romans, but in every matter to be difficult and to provide occasions for suspicion and resentment.

For their purpose was to estrange the people from their goodwill toward Rome and, so far as they could, entangle them in friendship with Perseus.

These men were devoted to that course because Polyaratus, being more boastful and vain, had burdened his estate with debt, while Deinon, being greedy and reckless, had from the beginning been drawn to the profits that came from powerful lords and kings.

At this point Stratocles the prytanis rose and, after speaking at length against Perseus and much in favor of the Romans, urged the majority to ratify the decree for dispatching the ships.

They immediately made ready six quadriremes, sent five of them to Chalcis with Timagoras appointed to command them, and the remaining one to Tenedos under a commander named Timagoras.

When he reached Tenedos, he came upon Diophanes, who had been sent by Perseus to Antiochus; he did not get possession of Diophanes himself, but he did capture his crew.

Lucretius received all the allies who had come by sea with kindness and then released them from this service, saying that the situation did not require naval assistance.

## Book 27 Chapter 8: Perseus Summons a Council

After the Macedonian victory, when a council had been gathered around Perseus, some of his friends pointed out that the king ought to send an embassy to the Roman commander,

and even now declare himself willing to pay the Romans the tribute that his father, when defeated, had previously promised, and to give up the same places.

For if the Romans accepted the settlement, they said, the war would end honorably for the king after his success in the field, and in general the Romans would in future be more cautious, now that they had had experience of Macedonian valor, not to impose anything unjust or oppressive on the Macedonians.

But if they refused to accept out of angry pride over what had happened, then heaven would justly be offended with them, while he, because of his moderation, would have both gods and men fighting on his side.

This view was approved by most of his friends, and Perseus agreed, so envoys were sent at once: Pantauchus son of Balacrus and Midon of Beroea.

When they arrived before Licinius, the commander immediately called a council. After the envoys had explained the message they had been instructed to deliver, Pantauchus and his colleague were asked to withdraw, and the Romans deliberated on the situation before them.

They unanimously decided to give the harshest answer possible.

For among the Romans this is in every way an established ancestral custom: in times of setback they show themselves most headstrong and severe, but in times of success most moderate.

Everyone would agree that this is a noble trait; but whether it is always practicable in certain circumstances, one might reasonably question.

At any rate, on that occasion they gave this answer: Perseus must place himself and all that concerned him in their hands, and in general give the senate authority to decide, as it thought best, about Macedonian affairs.

When Pantauchus and his colleague heard this, they returned and reported it to Perseus and his friends.

Some of them were shocked by such arrogance, grew angry, and advised Perseus to send no more embassies or messages about anything whatever.

Perseus, however, was not that sort of man. Instead he kept adding to the amount of money offered and sent to Licinius again and again.

But he made no progress, and most of his friends reproached him, saying that though he was the victor he was acting like the defeated party, as if everything had gone wrong for him.

So in the end, abandoning hope in further embassies, he was forced to move his camp back again to Sicyrium.

## Book 27 Chapter 9: Moral Effect of Perseus's Successes

When news of the cavalry engagement and the Macedonian victory spread through Greece, the feelings of the masses for Perseus flared up like fire, though most of them had previously kept those feelings concealed.

Their state of mind, as it seems to me, was something like this: what happened was much the same as in athletic contests.

For there too, whenever a humble and far inferior challenger is matched against a famous athlete thought to be unbeatable, the crowd immediately gives its support to the weaker man, urges him to be bold, and throws itself into his efforts.

And if he even brushes the other's face and leaves some visible mark from the blow, the whole crowd is at once thrown into excitement.

Sometimes they even begin jeering at the other man, not because they hate him or think little of him, but because, in a surprising way, their sympathies are stirred and they naturally give their favor to the weaker side.

But if someone checks them at the right moment, they quickly change and immediately recognize their own mistake. Cleitomachus is said to have done just this.

He seemed irresistible as an athlete, and his fame had spread throughout the inhabited world. So King Ptolemy, eager to put an end to that reputation, is said to have trained the boxer Aristonicus with great care and sent him out, since he seemed to have exceptional natural gifts for that purpose.

When Aristonicus arrived in Greece and was matched against Cleitomachus at Olympia, the crowd, it seems, leaned toward Aristonicus from the very beginning and kept encouraging him, delighted that someone had at least dared to stand up to Cleitomachus.

And when, as the contest went on, he appeared to be holding his own and even landed a telling blow, there was loud applause, and the mass of spectators broke out together, shouting encouragement to Aristonicus.

At that point, they say, Cleitomachus stepped back and paused a little to catch his breath; then he turned to the crowd and asked them what they meant by cheering Aristonicus and supporting him as much as they could,

whether they knew that he was fighting unfairly, or whether they failed to understand that Cleitomachus was now competing for the honor of the Greeks, while Aristonicus was competing for that of King Ptolemy.

Did they really want an Egyptian to carry off the Olympic crown by defeating Greeks, or a Theban and Boeotian to be proclaimed victor in boxing over his rivals?

After Cleitomachus said this, they say that the crowd's feeling changed so completely that Aristonicus was defeated more by the sudden reversal of the spectators than by Cleitomachus himself.

## Book 27 Chapter 10: The Unthinking Multitude

What happened with Perseus and the crowds was very similar to this.

For if someone had stopped them and frankly asked whether they wished so great a supremacy to fall into the hands of one man and wanted to experience monarchical power that was answerable to no one in any respect, I think they would quickly have come to their senses, taken back what they were saying, and swung round to the opposite view.

And if someone had briefly reminded them of the hardships the Greeks had suffered from the Macedonian royal house, and of the advantages they had gained from Roman power, I think they too would immediately have repented and changed their minds.

But at that moment, in their first unthinking impulse, the approval of the masses for the news that was being announced was obvious enough: they were delighted, out of sheer surprise, if someone had appeared at all capable of standing up to the Romans.

I have gone into this matter to this extent so that no one may rashly reproach the Greeks for ingratitude in the attitude they showed at that time, through ignorance of the impulses that naturally belong to human beings.

## Book 27 Chapter 11: Invention of the Cestros

The cestros was a new invention in the war with Perseus.

The missile was made like this: it was two palms in length, with the socket and the projecting point equal in size.

Into this was fitted a wooden shaft, a span long and a finger's breadth in diameter.

Into the middle of this shaft three very small wooden fins were fixed.

Because the sling had two thongs of unequal length, the missile was loosely set between them in the middle.

Then, while the sling was being whirled and the thongs were taut, it stayed in place; but when one of the two thongs was released at the moment of discharge, it slipped free,

and flew from the sling like a lead bullet, striking with violent force and badly mangling anyone it happened to hit.

## Book 27 Chapter 12: Character of Cotys

Cotys was a man impressive in appearance and outstanding in military matters; and in character he was anything but a Thracian.

For he was temperate, and he showed a gentleness and depth of spirit worthy of a freeborn man.

## Book 27 Chapter 13: Indecisive Result of the First Campaign

II. Affairs in Egypt.

Ptolemy, the commander in Cyprus, was not Egyptian in his ways at all, but a sensible and capable man.

For when he took over the island, while the king was still an infant, he applied himself diligently to raising money, but he gave absolutely nothing to anyone, though the royal administrators often asked him for funds and bitterly criticized him for never releasing anything.

But when the king came of age, he gathered together a considerable sum of money and sent it off.

As a result, King Ptolemy himself and the courtiers approved of his former frugality and of his refusal to part with any money.

## Book 27 Chapter 14: Dispute at Rhodes

Olympiad 152, year 2. The war with Perseus.

At the time when Perseus broke off operations in the war against the Romans, Antenor arrived from Perseus to negotiate the ransom of the prisoners who had sailed with Diophanes, and a serious division arose among the Rhodian statesmen over what ought to be done.

The party of Philophron and Theaetetus was completely opposed to entering into such an entanglement, while the party of Deinon and Polyaratus favored it.

In the end they came to an arrangement with Perseus for the redemption of the prisoners.

## Book 27 Chapter 15: Charops of Epirus

Cephalus came from Epirus. He had previously had ties with the Macedonian royal house, but was then compelled by circumstances to choose the side of Perseus. The cause of this was as follows.

There was an Epirote named Charops, a man of fine character and a friend of the Romans, who, when Philip had occupied the passes into Epirus, was responsible for driving Philip out of Epirus and for enabling Titus to gain control of both Epirus and the Macedonians.

This man had a son, Machatas, whose own son was Charops.

When the father died while this boy was still under age, the elder Charops sent him to Rome with the protection appropriate to his station, so that he might learn the Roman language and letters.

After some time, this young man returned home, having become acquainted with many people there.

The elder Charops then died. The young man, unstable by nature and full of every kind of wickedness, became puffed up and attached himself to the prominent men.

At first no one paid him any attention, but the men who held the leading place both in age and in reputation, the party around Antinous, managed public affairs according to their own judgment. But when the war with Perseus broke out,

this young man at once began to slander the men just mentioned before the Romans, using as his pretext their earlier connection with the Macedonian royal house,

while in present circumstances he watched everything closely and interpreted every word and action of theirs in the worst possible way, suppressing some facts and adding others, and so made his accusations against them convincing.

Cephalus, however, was in every other respect a prudent and steady man, and at this crisis too he held to the soundest judgment.

At the outset he prayed to the gods that the war might not break out and that matters might not be decided by force.

But once the war was actually under way, he wished to fulfill Rome's claims under the alliance fairly, while beyond that he did not want to run to them in a servile way or render any service beyond what was proper.

When Charops actively employed his slanders against him and treated everything done contrary to Roman wishes as deliberate malice, the men already mentioned at first ignored him, since they were conscious of no plans hostile to Rome.

But when they saw the Aetolians Hippolochus, Nicander, and Lochagus being carried off to Rome without cause after the cavalry action, and when they saw that the accusations brought against them by the party of Lyciscus had been believed, men who in Aetolia represented the same political tendency that Charops did in Epirus, then, foreseeing what was coming, they began to take counsel for their own safety.

They therefore resolved to try every possible means to prevent themselves from being carried off to Rome without a hearing because of Charops' slanders.

Thus Cephalus and his friends were forced, contrary to their own preference, to choose the side of Perseus.

## Book 27 Chapter 16: Plot to Kidnap a Roman Consul

The party of Theodotus and Philostratus committed an act that was openly impious and a clear breach of faith.

For when they learned that the Roman consul Aulus Hostilius was coming, on his way to the camp in Thessaly, and thought that, if they delivered Aulus to Perseus, they would both give the strongest proof of loyalty and do the Romans the greatest harm at that moment, they kept writing to Perseus and urging him to hurry.

The king wanted to advance at once and join them, but since the Molossians had occupied the bridge over the river Aous, he was prevented from making the attempt and was first forced to fight them.

It happened that when Aulus arrived among the Phanoteis he lodged with Nestor of Cropia, and thus gave his enemies just the opportunity they needed against him; and if some turn of fortune had not decided matters for the better, I do not think he would have escaped.

But as it was, Nestor somehow, with almost divine foresight, perceived what was coming and forced him to move on from there to Gitana during the night.

So, abandoning the overland route through Epirus, he put to sea and sailed to Anticyra, and from there made his way into Thessaly.

## Book 27 Chapter 17: Pharnaces, King of Pontus

Pharnaces proved to be the most lawless of all the kings before him.

## Book 27 Chapter 18: Attalus Wants his Brother's Honours Restored

While Attalus was wintering at Elateia and clearly knew that his brother Eumenes was deeply distressed and offended because the most distinguished honours granted to him had been annulled in the Peloponnese by a common decree, though Eumenes concealed these feelings from everyone,

Attalus decided to send messages to certain men in Achaea, eager by his own efforts to have his brother's honours restored, not only the honorific monuments but the inscribed honours as well.

He did this because he was convinced that he could give his brother no greater favour, and above all that by this action he would show the Greeks the brotherly affection and nobility of his own character.

## Book 27 Chapter 19: Antiochus Protests Ptolemy's War Plans

When Antiochus saw that the authorities in Alexandria were now openly making preparations for the war over Coele-Syria, he sent envoys headed by Meleager to Rome,

instructing them to address the senate and formally protest that Ptolemy was the one making the first attack on him, in defiance of every principle of justice.

## Book 27 Chapter 20: The Need of Promptness and Persistence

Perhaps in every human undertaking one should measure each action by the right moment, for timing has the greatest power; but this is especially true in war.

For in such matters the balance can swing very sharply in either direction, and to miss the critical moment is one of the gravest errors.

Many people seem to desire what is noble, but few dare to attempt it, and of those who do attempt it, very few carry through to the end what duty requires in each case.
