Other, and, as many have thought, wiser teachers, will have it, that
there are altogether six forms of government, three of them utterly bad,
the other three good in themselves, but so readily corrupted that they
too are apt to become hurtful. ... For this is the circle revolving
within which all States are and have been governed ... Wise legislators
therefore, knowing these defects, and avoiding each of these forms in
its simplicity, have made choice of a form which shares in the qualities
of all the first three, and which they judge to be more stable and
lasting than any of these separately. For where we have a monarchy, an
aristocracy, and a democracy existing together in the same city, each of
the three serves as a check upon the other.

**Note:** Machiavelli never names his source; the six forms and their cycle come from "other, and, as many have thought, wiser teachers." The teacher is Polybius, and the chapter follows his Book 6 argument step by step: the herd choosing the strongest, the birth of justice from anger at ingratitude, each good form sliding into its corrupt twin, and the escape into a mixed constitution on the model of Lycurgus. Machiavelli even keeps Polybius' twist about Rome (6.10): what Sparta got from one man's foresight, Rome reached through struggle, or, as he puts it, "obtained by chance."
