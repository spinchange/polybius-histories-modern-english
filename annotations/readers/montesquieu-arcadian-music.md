That judicious writer, Polybius, informs us that music was necessary to
soften the manners of the Arcadians, who lived in a cold, gloomy
country; that the inhabitants of Cynete, who slighted music, were the
cruellest of all the Greeks, and that no other town was so immersed in
luxury and debauchery.

**Note:** Montesquieu opens his chapter on a "paradox of the ancients" with this digression: why did serious Greek lawgivers treat music as a matter of state? Polybius' answer, that the Arcadians made music compulsory up to the age of thirty to soften men hardened by a harsh land, and that Cynaetha, the one town that gave it up, became a byword for savagery, is exactly the kind of evidence Montesquieu wanted. His larger argument, that climate shapes character and laws must answer to both, has one of its ancient roots in these two chapters.
