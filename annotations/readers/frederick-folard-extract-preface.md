The work we present to you may be called the Spirit of M. de Folard.
Among the visions and extravagances of this illustrious soldier there
are treasures: he had buried diamonds in the middle of a dunghill, and we
have dug them out, and instead of six fat quarto volumes we give
enthusiasts a quarter of one of them. We have made short work of the
system of columns ... Among the great number of books that are written,
very few are pure gold; there are even few from which one could draw as
many good things as from the Commentary on Polybius. For the progress of
human knowledge it would be desirable that, rather than writing new
books, people applied themselves to making good extracts of those we
already have; one might then hope not to waste one's time in reading.
... The art of war, which surely deserves to be studied and mastered as
much as any of the other arts, still lacks classic books. We have few.
Caesar, in his Commentaries, teaches us hardly anything beyond what we
see in the war of the Pandours ... a general of our day could make use
of nothing but the disposition of his cavalry at the battle of
Pharsalus. ... Those who have had this abridgment printed have had no
aim but the greater glory of the service, in trying to ease for
officers the study of their art and of a profession that leads to
immortality.

**Note:** Polybius argues in these chapters that command is an art to be learned from study as well as from the camp. In 1753 Frederick the Great took him at his word, having Folard's six-volume commentary on Polybius cut down to a quarter of one volume and printed in just 110 copies for his officers, with Folard's pet theory of attack in deep columns thrown out and the "diamonds" kept. Like Constantine eight centuries earlier, a monarch decided that the way to make a long book useful was to cut it. From all of Caesar, the one thing he thinks a modern general can use is the placing of the cavalry at Pharsalus, the battle on whose eve Brutus sat making his own epitome of Polybius.
