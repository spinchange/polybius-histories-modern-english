All those of old, kings and private men alike, who did not let pleasure
sweep their minds away or grow soft, but kept the nobility of their
souls unstained through virtue: these above all persevered in labour and gave
themselves to letters ... But since, in the course of so many years,
events multiplied beyond measure and writings were woven without end,
the web of history spread out into something boundless and
unmanageable; and since in later times men's inclinations were bound to
lean toward the worse, to grow careless of what is good and idle about
taking up the record of earlier ages ... so that the discovery of history
was overshadowed by obscurity, partly through a scarcity of useful
books, partly because people shrank in dread from sprawling
verbosity: Constantine, born in the purple, the most orthodox and most
Christian of all who have ever reigned, keener-eyed in
discerning what is good and possessed of an active mind, judged it best,
of common benefit and profitable for life, first to gather by diligent
search books from every quarter of the inhabited world, pregnant with
knowledge of every sort; and then, since the sheer bulk of their
prolixity wears out the ear and seems, besides, to most people tiresome and
burdensome, he thought it necessary to cut this up into small portions,
and to set out ungrudgingly, for all to share, the benefit that springs
from them ... and to divide it into distinct subjects, fifty-three in
number, in which and under which every great deed of history is
enclosed. ... They are taken from the chronicles listed below: 1. Peter
the Patrician and Magister. 2. The Chronicle of George the Monk. 3. John
of Antioch. 4. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities. 5.
Polybius of Megalopolis.

**Note:** The emperor whose scissors saved this book. Around 950 Constantine VII had the Greek histories in his library cut up and filed under fifty-three subjects; most of the subjects are lost, but those that survive, above all "On Embassies," "On Virtues and Vices" and "On Sentences," are where much of what we have of Books 7 to 39 comes from. Much of what follows survives because of the editorial decision this preface defends. Note the fourth and fifth names on his list: Dionysius, who complained that no one could finish Polybius, filed beside the historian he disparaged.
