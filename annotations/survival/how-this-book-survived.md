**Note:** Polybius wrote forty books, and only the first five survive whole. Book 6 survives in long stretches. Books 7 to 39 reached us only in pieces, and the pieces came by two roads. Book 17 did not reach us at all, which is why its page holds only a note saying so.

The first road was accident. Later writers quoted Polybius when they had a reason to: Athenaeus for a luxurious city, Strabo for a coastline, Plutarch for a biography, the Suda and Stephanus of Byzantium for a word or a place-name. Each such fragment survives because someone, centuries on, wanted that one passage. This chapter is an example. Athenaeus kept Polybius' remark that the Campanians of Capua, spoiled by their rich land, "drifted into luxury and extravagance," and the lexicon of Suidas kept the closing sentence. Wherever the quoting author is known, this edition says so in an italic line under the chapter heading.

The second road was policy. Around 950 the emperor Constantine VII had the Greek historians in his library cut into excerpts and sorted under fifty-three subjects, and Polybius stands fifth on his list of the chronicles used (his own preface, quoted in the note beside this one, is translated for this edition). Of those collections only some survive, chiefly "On Embassies," "On Virtues and Vices" and "On Sentences," and many chapters of Books 7 to 39 come down through them. Chapters that come from these collections carry no italic line in the book. Their provenance is recorded in this layer instead.

Both roads filter. An excerptor interested in embassies, vices, virtues and quotable sayings keeps those, and a lexicographer keeps the odd word, so the shape of what we have follows the interests of those who copied it. What looks to us like Polybius' emphasis is in part theirs. Where a chapter blends two sources, or where the evidence for its source is thin, its note in this layer says so rather than guess.
