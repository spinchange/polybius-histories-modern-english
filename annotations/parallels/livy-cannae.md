Cneius Lentulus, a military tribune, seeing, as he rode by, the consul sitting upon a stone and covered with blood, said to him: "Lucius Aemilius! the only man whom the gods ought to regard as being guiltless of this day's disaster, take this horse, while you have any strength remaining, and I am with you to raise you up and protect you. Make not this battle more calamitous by the death of a consul. ... Go and tell the fathers publicly, to fortify the city of Rome ... Allow me to expire amid these heaps of my slaughtered troops, that I may not a second time be accused after my consulate ... they overwhelm the consul with their weapons, not knowing who he was: in the confusion his horse rescued Lentulus.

**Note:** Polybius gives Aemilius Paullus only a brief notice (3.116): after "violent blows in the thick of hand-to-hand fighting" he "ended his life," a man who served his country to the last hour. Livy, writing nearly two centuries after the battle, gives him a scene instead: the wounded consul on a stone, the tribune with a spare horse, a refusal, and a last message for the senate and for Fabius. The speeches, the stone and the death in the confusion are not in Polybius' account; whatever Livy's source, the battle report has become a tragedy.
