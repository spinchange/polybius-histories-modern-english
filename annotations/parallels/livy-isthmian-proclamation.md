a herald, preceded by a trumpeter, according to custom, advanced into the centre of the theatre ... Silence being commanded by sound of trumpet, he uttered aloud the following proclamation: THE SENATE AND PEOPLE OF ROME, AND TITUS QUINCTIUS, THEIR GENERAL, HAVING SUBDUED KING PHILIP AND THE MACEDONIANS, DO HEREBY ORDER, THAT THE FOLLOWING STATES BE FREE, INDEPENDENT, AND RULED BY THEIR OWN LAWS ... Scarcely could they believe that they had heard them; and they looked at each other, marvelling as at the empty illusion of a dream. ... As everyone desired not only to hear, but to see the messenger of liberty, the herald was called out again; and he again repeated the proclamation.

**Note:** Livy tells the scene in the same order as Polybius (18.46): the herald and trumpeter, the proclamation, the crowd's disbelief "as at a dream," the call for the herald to come out and say it again so that they might see as well as hear. He changes the setting from the stadium to a theatre, drops Polybius' "without garrison, without tribute" from the herald's words, and puts "the Senate and People of Rome" where Polybius has the Senate alone. Polybius' own aside, "as it seems to me," about why the crowd wanted to see the herald has become plain narration in Livy.
