# Polybius — The Histories, Book II

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 2 Chapter 1: Recapitulation of Book 1

In the book before this one I set out clearly when the Romans, having consolidated their position in Italy, began to take a hand in affairs beyond it; and after that, how they crossed over into Sicily, and for what reasons they undertook the war against the Carthaginians over that island.

After that I told when they first began to assemble naval forces, and what happened to each side over the course of the war down to its conclusion—the outcome of which was that the Carthaginians withdrew from the whole of Sicily, while the Romans gained control of the entire island except for the districts under Hiero's command.

Following on from this, I set about describing how the mercenaries, in revolt against the Carthaginians, kindled what is called the Libyan War; how far the atrocities committed during it went; and what course these extraordinary events ran until their end and the triumph of Carthage.

Now I shall try to make plain what came next, touching on each matter in summary fashion, in keeping with the plan I laid out from the start.

For as soon as the Carthaginians had settled affairs in Libya, they at once raised an army and dispatched Hamilcar to the regions of Iberia.

He took over the troops, together with his son Hannibal—then nine years old—and crossing by the Pillars of Heracles, set about recovering Carthaginian fortunes in Iberia.

He spent about nine years in those parts, and after subjecting many of the Iberians to Carthage, some by war and some by persuasion, he ended his life in a way worthy of his earlier achievements.

For while drawn up in battle against the most warlike peoples, those with the greatest power, and while exposing himself with daring and reckless courage at the critical moment of danger, he met a valiant death.

The Carthaginians handed over the command to Hasdrubal, who was his son-in-law and the commander of his fleet.

## Book 2 Chapter 2: Situation in Illyria

It was at this same period that the Romans set about making their first crossing, with an army, into Illyria and these parts of Europe. This is not to be looked at as a minor matter,

but must be studied with close attention by anyone who genuinely wishes to grasp both the purpose of my work and the growth and consolidation of Roman power.

They decided to make the crossing for reasons of the following kind.

Agron, king of the Illyrians, was the son of Pleuratus, and he possessed the most powerful land and naval forces of any king who had reigned in Illyria before him.

Bribed with money by Demetrius, the father of Philip, he had promised to help the Medionians, who were being besieged by the Aetolians.

For the Aetolians, being quite unable to persuade the Medionians to join their own league, had resolved to take them by force.

So they marched out with their whole army, encamped around the city, and laid siege to it without a break, bringing every kind of force and siege engine against it.

But when the time for the annual elections drew near, and a new general had to be chosen, and the besieged were already in a bad way and seemed likely to surrender any day, the general then in office made a proposal to the Aetolians. He argued

that, since it was he who had taken on the hardships and dangers of the siege, it was only fair that, once they captured the place, he should be allowed both the management of the spoils and the privilege of inscribing his name on the dedicated arms.

But some objected to what he said—above all those who were standing for the office—and urged the assembly not to settle the matter in advance, but to leave it open, so that fortune might bestow this crown on whomever it chose.

In the end the Aetolians decided that whoever was appointed general and captured the city should share with his predecessor both the management

## Book 2 Chapter 3: The Illyrians Relieve Medion

of the spoils and the inscribing of the arms. This had been resolved, and on the very next day the election and the transfer of office were due to take place, as is the custom among the Aetolians, when during the night a hundred boats sailed up to the territory of Medion, to the places nearest the city, carrying five thousand Illyrians.

They put in to shore, and when day came they made their landing swiftly and unobserved, and forming up in the order customary among them, advanced in companies against the Aetolian camp.

When the Aetolians realized what was happening, they were taken aback by the unexpectedness and the boldness of the Illyrians; yet, having long been full of self-importance and confident in their own strength, they remained reasonably steady.

So they drew up the bulk of their hoplites and cavalry on the level ground in front of their camp, while with a portion of their cavalry and their light troops they moved to seize in advance the high ground in front of the palisade, which was conveniently placed for defense.

But the Illyrians, charging the light troops at the first rush, drove them back by the sheer mass and weight of their formation, and forced the cavalry stationed with them to fall back on the heavy infantry.

Then, charging down from the high ground against the troops drawn up on the plain, they quickly routed them, especially as the Medionians joined the attack on the Aetolians by sallying out from the city at the same time.

They killed many of them and took even more prisoners, and they captured all their arms and baggage.

So the Illyrians, having carried out their king's orders and brought the baggage and the rest of the plunder down to their boats, at once put to sea, setting

## Book 2 Chapter 4: Death of Agron of Illyria

their course for home. The Medionians, having won an unexpected deliverance, gathered in assembly and deliberated on their affairs in general and, in particular, on the inscribing of the arms.

They resolved to make the inscription a joint one, naming both the man who had held the office of Aetolian general and those who were standing for it for the coming year, in accordance with the Aetolians' own decree—

as though Fortune were deliberately demonstrating her power to the rest of mankind through what had happened to these men.

For the very things the Medionians had expected to suffer at the hands of their enemies any moment now, she gave them the power to inflict on those same enemies, all in a very short space of time.

And the Aetolians, by the unexpected disaster they met with, taught everyone never to treat the future as though it had already happened, nor to count confidently on outcomes that may still turn out otherwise, but to allow a share to the unexpected—being human in every situation, but most of all in matters of war.

As for King Agron: when the boats had sailed back and he had heard from his officers the details of the engagement, he was overjoyed at the thought of having beaten the Aetolians, those men of the highest spirit. He gave himself over to bouts of drinking and other such revelry, and fell ill with pleurisy, from which within a few days he died.

His wife Teuta took over the kingdom and conducted the detailed administration of affairs through trusted friends.

But she relied on a woman's reasoning, looking only at the recent stroke of good fortune and taking no thought for anything beyond it. First, she gave permission to those sailing on their own account to plunder any ships they fell in with;

second, she gathered a fleet and a force no smaller than the previous one, and sent it out, declaring every coast fair game to its commanders.

## Book 2 Chapter 5: Queen Teuta's Pirates

Those who were sent out made their first attack on Elis and Messenia, for the Illyrians were always raiding these regions.

Because of the length of their coastline, and because the cities that held power in them lay inland, relief against the Illyrians' landings was always very slow and a long time in coming. As a result, the Illyrians overran and pillaged these regions whenever they liked, without fear.

On this occasion, however, when they reached Phoenice in Epirus, they put in for supplies.

There they fell in with some Gauls—about eight hundred in number—who were serving as mercenaries with the Epirotes and were stationed in Phoenice. They discussed the betrayal of the city with them, and when these Gauls agreed to the plan, they disembarked and at the first assault made themselves masters of the city and everyone in it, with the Gauls inside working with them.

When the Epirotes learned what had happened, they hurried to the rescue with a full levy. On reaching Phoenice, they encamped with the river that flows past the city in front of them, and for safety's sake tore up the planks of the bridge over it.

But when word reached them that Scerdilaidas was approaching by land with five thousand Illyrians through the pass near Antigoneia, they detached part of their force and sent it off to guard Antigoneia; meanwhile they passed the rest of their time carelessly, indulging without restraint in the produce of the countryside, and grew slack about their guard duty and outposts.

The Illyrians, perceiving both the division of their forces and their general carelessness, marched out by night, laid planks on the bridge, crossed the river safely, took up a strong position, and stayed there for the rest of the night.

When day came, and both sides drew up in front of the city, the Epirotes were beaten: many of them fell, still more were captured, and the rest escaped in the direction of the Atintanes.

## Book 2 Chapter 6: The Aetolians and Achaeans Support the Epirotes

The Epirotes, then, having met with such misfortunes and lost all the hopes they had placed in themselves, sent envoys to the Aetolians and to the Achaean League, begging with suppliant pleas for help.

These took pity on their plight and consented, and afterwards came to their aid, arriving at Helicranum.

The men who had seized Phoenice, having first joined up with Scerdilaidas and advanced to the place, encamped opposite the relief force, wishing to give battle.

But they found themselves hampered by the difficult terrain; and at the same time a letter arrived from Teuta, in which she said they ought to return home as quickly as possible, because some of the Illyrians had defected to the Dardani.

So, after ravaging Epirus, they made a truce with the Epirotes.

Under its terms they restored to them the free persons and the city for a ransom, but loaded the slaves and the rest of the spoils onto their boats; then some of them sailed away, while the troops under Scerdilaidas withdrew again by land through the pass near Antigoneia.

In doing so they instilled no small or ordinary alarm and fear in the Greeks who lived along the coasts.

For each of these peoples, seeing the most strongly fortified and most powerful city in Epirus so unexpectedly enslaved, no longer felt anxiety, as in earlier times, merely for their property in the countryside, but now for themselves and their cities.

The Epirotes, however, having been saved beyond all expectation, were so far from trying to take revenge on those who had wronged them, or to show gratitude to those who had helped them, that on the contrary they sent envoys to Teuta and made an alliance with the Illyrians, together with the Acarnanians,

under which they cooperated with the Illyrians in the period that followed, while working against the Achaeans and the Aetolians.

By these actions they showed themselves to have behaved senselessly toward their benefactors at that time, and to have planned foolishly from the start in regard to their own affairs.

## Book 2 Chapter 7: A Band of Gallic Mercenaries

For that men, being human, should fall unexpectedly into some calamity is the fault not of the sufferers but of Fortune and of those who do the wrong;

but that they should plunge themselves into the gravest disasters thoughtlessly and with their eyes open is, by common admission, the fault of the victims themselves.

That is why those who stumble through Fortune are met with pity, forgiveness, and assistance, whereas those who fail through their own bad judgment are followed by reproach and censure from sensible people.

And this is just what the Epirotes would rightly have met with from the Greeks at that time.

For, in the first place, who—mindful of the general reputation of the Gauls—would not have been wary of entrusting to them a prosperous city that offered so many openings for treachery?

And second, who would not have been on his guard against the known character of that particular band? For in the beginning they had been driven out of their own country, when their fellow countrymen banded together against them because they had betrayed their own friends and kinsmen;

then, having been taken in by the Carthaginians because the latter were hard pressed by war, they were brought into Agrigentum to garrison it—being at that time more than three thousand strong—and, when a dispute arose between the soldiers and their generals over pay, they at once set about plundering the city of the Agrigentines.

After that, when the Carthaginians once more brought them into Eryx for the same purpose, while the Romans were besieging the city, they attempted to betray both the city and those besieged in it along with them;

and when this attempt failed, they deserted to the enemy, by whom they were again trusted—and then plundered the temple of Aphrodite of Eryx.

For this reason the Romans, having clearly recognized their impiety, made it their first priority, as soon as they had ended the war with the Carthaginians, to disarm them, put them aboard ships, and banish them from the whole of Italy.

These were the men the Epirotes made guardians of their democracy and their laws, and into whose hands they entrusted their most prosperous city. How, then, could they not reasonably be seen as the authors of their own misfortunes?

So much, then, for the folly of the Epirotes, and for the principle that sensible people should never bring in a garrison stronger than themselves—especially one of barbarians.

## Book 2 Chapter 8: Queen Teuta and Rome

This much I judged worth recording. Now the Illyrians had, even in earlier times, persistently preyed upon ships sailing from Italy.

During the period when they were operating around Phoenice, a good number of them broke away from the main fleet and fell upon many of the Italian merchants: some they merely robbed, others they slaughtered, and not a few of those they captured they carried off alive.

The Romans had until then turned a deaf ear to those who lodged complaints against the Illyrians; but now, when still more people came before the Senate on the matter, they appointed envoys to go to Illyria and look into the grievances described—Gaius and Lucius Coruncanius.

When the galleys returned to Teuta from Epirus, she was struck by the sheer quantity and the beauty of the spoils they brought back—for Phoenice was at that time by far the most prosperous of the cities of Epirus—and so she was doubly emboldened to press on with her depredations against the Greeks.

For the moment, however, she held back because of disturbances at home; but once she had quickly settled affairs with the Illyrians who had revolted, she laid siege to Issa, since it alone still refused to submit to her.

It was at this very time that the Roman envoys arrived. Granted an audience, they set out the wrongs that had been done to their citizens.

Throughout the entire discussion Teuta listened to them in an arrogant and thoroughly overbearing manner.

When they had finished speaking, she replied that, as a matter of public policy, she would try to see to it that no wrong was done to the Romans by the Illyrians; but that, as a private matter, it was not lawful for the kings of Illyria to bar the Illyrians from the profits to be had at sea.

The younger of the envoys, offended at this answer, spoke out with a frankness that was fitting in itself but in no way suited to the moment. For he said:

"Romans, Teuta, have a most excellent custom of using the state to punish private wrongs and to come to the aid of those who have been wronged.

We shall therefore endeavor, God willing, to compel you—and that soon and emphatically—to reform the royal practices of Illyria."

Receiving this frankness with a woman's temper and without reflection, she was so enraged at what had been said that, disregarding the rights established among all mankind, she sent men after the envoys as they sailed away with orders to kill the one who had spoken so frankly.

When news of this reached Rome, the Romans were incensed at the woman's lawless act and at once set about their preparations, enrolling legions and assembling a fleet.

## Book 2 Chapter 9: Teuta Sends Out Another Fleet

When the sailing season came round, Teuta fitted out more galleys than before and sent them off once again toward the regions of Greece.

Some of them made their crossing by the open sea straight for Corcyra, while a portion put in at the harbor of Epidamnus—ostensibly to take on water and provisions, but in reality to make an attempt on the city by treachery.

The Epidamnians received them without suspicion and carelessly, and the Illyrians went in wearing nothing but their loincloths, as though to fetch water, but with daggers hidden in the water-jars. In this way they cut down the men guarding the gate and quickly seized the gatehouse.

When, according to plan, reinforcements arrived in force from the ships, they let these men in and easily held most of the walls.

The people of the city, though caught unprepared by the surprise, came to the rescue with spirit and fought it out, so that in the end the Illyrians, after holding their ground for a long while, were driven out of the city.

So on this occasion the Epidamnians, through their negligence, came near to losing their homeland, but through their courage came off unharmed and the wiser for the future.

The Illyrian commanders then put to sea in haste, joined the squadron that had sailed ahead, and made land at Corcyra. There they made a terrifying landing and set about besieging the city.

At this turn of events the Corcyraeans, hard-pressed and despairing of their whole situation, sent envoys to the Achaeans and the Aetolians—and along with them the Apollonians and Epidamnians did the same—begging them to come to their aid quickly and not to stand by while they were driven from their homes by the Illyrians.

The Achaeans and Aetolians, having heard the envoys and granted their plea, jointly manned the ten decked ships of the Achaeans, and within a few days had them fitted out and sailed for Corcyra,

## Book 2 Chapter 10: Illyrian Victories

hoping to break the siege. But the Illyrians, having taken on seven decked ships supplied by the Acarnanians under the terms of their alliance, put out to meet them and engaged the Achaean vessels off the islands called Paxi.

Now the Acarnanians, and the Achaean ships posted opposite them, kept the fight even and came through the clashes unscathed, apart from wounds among the men themselves.

The Illyrians, however, lashed their own galleys together in groups of four and so closed with the enemy. They paid no regard to their own vessels but threw them in broadside and so worked to assist the enemy's ramming charges.

And when the enemy ships, having rammed and become locked in by the impact, were for the moment in difficulty—with the lashed-together galleys hanging on around their rams—the Illyrians then leapt onto the decks of the Achaean ships and overpowered them by sheer weight of numbers.

In this way they captured four four-banked ships, and sank one five-banked ship with all hands aboard—the ship on which Margos of Caryneia was sailing, a man who had served the common government of the Achaeans with complete integrity right up to his death.

Those who were fighting the Acarnanians, seeing the advantage the Illyrians had gained and trusting in their own swiftness, ran before the wind and made their withdrawal home in safety.

The Illyrian host, emboldened by their success, now pressed the siege with ease and full confidence.

The Corcyraeans, despairing of their whole situation after what had happened, held out under the siege only a little while longer and then came to terms with the Illyrians, accepting a garrison and, with the garrison, Demetrius of Pharos.

Once this had been arranged, the Illyrian commanders immediately put to sea; and, putting in at Epidamnus, set about besieging that city once again.

## Book 2 Chapter 11: Corcyra Submits To the Romans

At this same period, of the men holding the consulships, Gnaeus Fulvius set sail from Rome with two hundred ships, while Aulus Postumius set out with the land forces.

Gnaeus's first intention was to sail to Corcyra, supposing that he would still find the outcome of the siege undecided.

Although he came too late for that, he nevertheless sailed on to the island, wishing both to learn clearly what had happened concerning the city and to test the proposals being conveyed by Demetrius.

For Demetrius, being under suspicion and in fear of Teuta, was sending messages to the Romans, offering to hand over the city and to surrender the other affairs of which he was in charge.

The Corcyraeans, delighted at the arrival of the Romans, surrendered the Illyrian garrison with Demetrius's approval, and of their own accord, on being invited, gave themselves over wholeheartedly into the protection of Rome—judging this to be their one safeguard for the future against the lawlessness of the Illyrians.

The Romans, having received the Corcyraeans into their friendship, sailed for Apollonia, taking Demetrius as their guide for the remaining operations.

At the same time Postumius was ferrying his land forces across from Brundisium—some twenty thousand foot and about two thousand horse.

As soon as both forces had converged together upon Apollonia, and the Apollonians too had welcomed them and given themselves into Roman trust, they at once put to sea again on hearing that Epidamnus was under siege.

The Illyrians, learning of the Romans' approach, broke up the siege in complete disorder and fled.

The Romans, taking the Epidamnians too under their protection, advanced into the interior of Illyria, subduing the Ardiaei as they went.

When many envoys came to meet them—among them those from the Parthini, offering an unconditional surrender—they received these into their friendship; and likewise those who came from the Atintanes. They then advanced toward Issa, since that city too was being besieged by the Illyrians.

On reaching it and breaking the siege, they received the Issaeans as well into their protection.

In the course of their voyage along the coast they also stormed certain Illyrian cities; and at one of these, Nutria, they lost not only many soldiers but also some of the military tribunes and the quaestor.

They likewise captured twenty galleys that were carrying off the plunder from the countryside.

Of those besieging Issa, the men from Pharos were left unharmed for Demetrius's sake, while all the rest scattered and fled to Arbo.

Teuta herself, with a very few companions, escaped to Rhizon, a little town well fortified for strength, set back from the sea and lying right on the river Rhizon.

Having accomplished this, and having placed most of the Illyrians under Demetrius and invested him with great power, the Romans withdrew to Epidamnus with their fleet and their land army.

## Book 2 Chapter 12: Teuta Agrees to Pay Tribute to Rome

Gnaeus Fulvius, then, sailed back to Rome with the greater part of both the naval and the land forces.

Postumius stayed behind with forty ships, raised a legion from the surrounding cities, and wintered there, keeping watch over the Ardiaei and the other peoples who had given themselves into Roman trust.

About the spring season Teuta sent envoys to the Romans and concluded a treaty, in which she agreed to pay the tribute that had been imposed, to withdraw from all Illyria except for a few places, and—the crucial point, which most concerned the Greeks—not to sail beyond Lissus with more than two galleys, and those unarmed.

Once these terms had been settled, Postumius afterward dispatched envoys to the Aetolians and to the Achaean nation. On their arrival, they first explained the grounds for the war and for the crossing, then went through in order what had been accomplished and read out the treaty they had made with the Illyrians.

Having met with a fitting courtesy from each of the two nations, they then sailed back to Corcyra, having freed the Greeks from a considerable fear by means of the aforesaid treaty.

For it so happened that the Illyrians were at that time the common enemies not of some peoples only, but of all alike.

Such, then, was the first crossing of the Romans with an army into Illyria and these parts of Europe, along with their first involvement, by way of an embassy, in the affairs of Greece—and these were the causes that brought it about.

From this beginning the Romans straightaway sent yet other envoys to the Corinthians and to the Athenians; and it was then that the Corinthians first admitted the Romans to take part in the Isthmian Games.

## Book 2 Chapter 13: Jealousy At Rome of Hasdrubal In Spain

During this same period—for it was at this point that we broke off our account of affairs in Iberia—Hasdrubal was administering his command with prudence and practical skill, and was making great headway overall. In particular, by founding the town called Carthage by some and New Town by others, he contributed greatly to the interests of the Carthaginians,

and this above all because of the favorable position of the site, both for affairs in Iberia and for those in Libya. Of this place I shall, when I find a more suitable occasion, point out its location and the use it can serve for each of the two regions I have mentioned.

When the Romans saw this power now growing greater and more formidable, they were spurred to interest themselves in affairs in Iberia.

Finding that in the preceding period they had let themselves doze off and had allowed the Carthaginians to build up a great force, they tried to make up the lost ground as best they could.

For the moment, however, they did not venture either to dictate terms to the Carthaginians or to go to war with them, because the fear of the Celts was hanging over their own affairs and they were all but daily expecting their attack.

So, having soothed and conciliated Hasdrubal, they then decided to take on the Celts and bring matters to a decision against them; for they reckoned that, with such men poised on their flank, they would never be able—not merely to hold sway over the rest of Italy—but even to live in safety in their own homeland.

Accordingly, as soon as they had sent envoys to Hasdrubal and made a treaty with him—in which they passed over the rest of Iberia in silence, but stipulated that the Carthaginians were not to cross the river called the Ebro for the purpose of war—they at once launched their war against the Celts in

## Book 2 Chapter 14: Cisalpine Gaul

Italy. Concerning these Celts, it seems to me useful to give an account in summary form, so as to keep intact the proper purpose of my introductory survey in accordance with my original design, and to go back in time to the beginning—to the point at which the people just mentioned first occupied the country.

For I consider the history of these matters not only worth knowing and remembering, but absolutely essential, in order to learn what men and what places Hannibal afterward relied upon when he set himself to break the power of Rome.

First I must speak about the country—what sort it is and how it lies in relation to the rest of Italy. For in this way it will be easier to grasp the distinctive features of the events as well, once the peculiarities of the localities and of the land have been sketched out.

Italy as a whole is triangular in shape. One of its sides, the one inclined toward the east, is bounded by the Ionian strait and, continuous with it, by the Adriatic gulf; the side facing south and west is bounded by the Sicilian and Tyrrhenian seas.

These two sides, meeting one another, form the apex of the triangle—the promontory of Italy that juts out toward the south, which is called Cocynthus and divides the Ionian strait from the Sicilian sea.

The remaining side, which stretches along the north and the interior, is bounded throughout by the foothills of the Alps. This range takes its beginning from Massalia and the region above the Sardinian sea, and runs on continuously as far as the head of the whole Adriatic, falling short only by a little where it stops before reaching it.

Along this foothill range—which we must think of as, so to speak, the base of the triangle—there lie, to the south of it, plains that are the most northerly of all Italy, the very ones now under discussion, surpassing in fertility and extent any in Europe that have come within the scope of my history.

The overall shape, and that of the line enclosing these plains, is likewise triangular. The apex of this figure is formed by the meeting of the mountains called the Apennines and the Alpine range, not far from the Sardinian sea and above Massalia.

As for its sides: along the northern one, as I said above, the Alps themselves extend for two thousand two hundred stades,

while along the southern one the Apennines extend for three thousand six hundred.

The seaboard of the Adriatic gulf serves as the base of the whole figure: the length of this base, from the city of Sena to the head of the gulf, is more than two thousand five hundred stades,

so that the entire perimeter of the plains just described comes to little short of …

## Book 2 Chapter 15: Grain Production in Cisalpine Gaul

...of the ten thousand stades. As for the fertility of the land, it is not even easy to put into words. Grain is so abundant in these regions that in our own day the Sicilian medimnus of wheat has often gone for four obols, of barley for two, and the metretes of wine for the same price as the barley.

There is, moreover, an utterly overwhelming abundance of panic-grass and millet among them. And as for the quantity of acorns produced by the oak woods scattered at intervals across the plains, one may best judge it from this:

although a very great number of pigs are slaughtered in Italy, both for private households and for the provisioning of the armies, by far the fullest supply of them comes from these plains.

As to the cheapness and plenty of the various articles of food in detail, one could grasp it most precisely in this way:

travellers passing through the country take their lodging at the inns, and do not haggle over the individual provisions, but simply ask how much the host charges per person.

Now for the most part the innkeepers take in their guests—on the understanding that everything needful is provided—for half an as, which is the fourth part of an obol; and only rarely do they exceed this.

As for the numbers of the men, the size and beauty of their bodies, and beyond that their boldness in war, all this will be plain to learn from the events of their history themselves.

On each of the two flanks of the Alps—the one inclining toward the river Rhone, the other toward the plains already mentioned—the hilly, fertile regions are inhabited: those facing the Rhone and the north by Gauls called Transalpine, and those facing the plains by the Taurisci and the Agones and several other barbarian peoples.

The Transalpine are so named not from a difference of race but from a difference of location: for "trans," translated, means "beyond"; and so they call those who live on the far side of the Alps the Transalpine.

The peaks, both because of their ruggedness and because of the great depth of the [snow]...

## Book 2 Chapter 16: Rivers and Mountains in Northern Italy

...of the snow that lies there always, [the peaks] are utterly uninhabitable. The Apennine, from its beginning above Massalia and its junction with the Alps, is inhabited by the Ligurians—both the flank of it inclining toward the Tyrrhenian Sea and the flank toward the plains—

along the sea as far as the city of Pisa, which lies first in Etruria toward the west, and inland as far as the territory of the Arretini.

Next come the Etruscans; and continuous with these, on both slopes of the mountains just mentioned, dwell the Umbrians.

After that the Apennine, keeping a distance of about five hundred stades from the Adriatic Sea, leaves the plains behind, bending off to the right, and running through the middle of the rest of Italy stretches down to the Sicilian Sea.

The remaining flat part of that flank runs down to the sea and the city of Sena.

The river Padus—celebrated by the poets as the Eridanus—has its sources in the Alps, toward the apex rather of the figure just described, and descends into the plains, making its course toward the south.

But when it reaches the level country, it turns aside with its stream toward the east and flows through the plains; it makes its outlet by two mouths into the region of the Adriatic, and the larger part of the plain country is cut off by it toward the Alps and the head of the Adriatic.

It carries a volume of water inferior to none of the rivers in Italy, because all the streams that flow down toward the plains from the Alps and the Apennine mountains fall into it from every side.

It runs with its greatest and most beautiful flow around the rising of the Dog Star, swollen by the mass of snows melting on the mountains just mentioned.

From the sea it is navigable upstream, by the mouth called Olana, for nearly two thousand stades.

For at first, from its sources, it has a single channel, but it divides into two parts at the place called Trigaboli; of these, the one mouth is named the Padoa, the other the Olana.

On the latter lies a harbour that offers anchorage as safe as any on the Adriatic to those who put in there. Among the local people the river is called the Bodencus.

As for the other tales told about this river among the Greeks—I mean the stories of Phaethon and his fall, and the tears of the poplars, and the black-clad people living along the river, who, they say, still to this day wear such garments out of mourning for Phaethon—

But when a fitting occasion comes, we shall give them the notice they deserve, especially in view of Timaeus's ignorance about the regions just described.

## Book 2 Chapter 17: Gallic Settlements in the Valley of the Po

...except that these plains were in ancient times occupied by the Etruscans, at the same period as the so-called Phlegraean plains around Capua and Nola—plains which, just because they lay in many people's way and so became known, have won a great reputation for fertility.

For this reason those who record the dominions of the Etruscans should not direct their account to the land now held by them, but to the plains just mentioned and to the resources they drew from these places.

The Celts, mingling with them because they lay alongside one another, and casting envious eyes on the beauty of the country, on a slight pretext unexpectedly attacked with a great army, drove the Etruscans out of the country around the Padus, and took possession of the plains themselves.

So the first districts, those lying toward the eastern part of the Padus, the Laevi and the Lebecii settled; after these the Insubres, who were the greatest of their peoples; and next to these, along the river, the Cenomani.

The districts already adjoining the Adriatic were held by another, very ancient people: they are called the Veneti, differing little from the Celts in customs and dress, but using a different language.

About them the tragic poets have made much talk and dealt out much marvel-mongering.

The districts beyond the Padus, around the Apennine, were settled first by the Anares, and after them by the Boii; next to these, toward the Adriatic, the Lingones; and the last, on the sea, the Senones.

These, then, were the most notable of the peoples that took possession of the regions just described.

They lived in unwalled villages, without any other furnishings of life.

For because they slept on beds of straw, ate meat, and practiced nothing besides war and farming, they led simple lives, with no other science or craft known among them at all.

Each man's property consisted of livestock and gold, since these alone could, whatever the circumstances, be readily carried about everywhere and moved according to their own wishes.

They set the greatest store by their bands of companions, because among them the most feared and most powerful man was the one who was reckoned to have the most attendants and followers in his train.

## Book 2 Chapter 18: Early Conflicts between Gauls and Romans

In the beginning, then, they not only mastered the territory, but also made many of the neighbouring peoples their subjects, having overawed them by their daring.

Some time later, having defeated the Romans and those drawn up in battle alongside them, and pursuing the fugitives, they occupied Rome itself, except for the Capitol, three days after the battle.

But when a diversion arose—the Veneti invading their country—they then made terms with the Romans, handed back the city, and returned home.

After this they were caught up in internal wars; and some of those who lived in the Alps kept making raids and often gathered against them, comparing their own situation with the prosperity that had come to the others.

During this period the Romans recovered their own strength and once more set in order their affairs with the Latins.

When the Celts came again to Alba with a great army, in the thirtieth year after the capture of the city, the Romans did not then dare to lead out their armies against them, because—the assault having come unexpectedly—they were caught off guard and had no time to assemble the mustered forces of their allies.

But again, on a further expedition twelve years later, when the Celts were advancing with a great army, the Romans, having learned of it beforehand and gathered their allies, went out to meet them with great eagerness, hastening to engage and to risk all in a decisive battle.

But the Gauls, dismayed at their approach and falling into discord among themselves, when night came on made their withdrawal homeward in something very like a flight.

## Book 2 Chapter 20: The Boii Attack the Romans and Lose

The Boii, seeing the Senones driven out, and fearing for themselves and their land that they might suffer the same fate, marched out in full force, having summoned the Etruscans.

Mustering near the lake called Vadimon, they drew up against the Romans.

In this battle most of the Etruscans were cut down, and only very few of the Boii escaped.

Nevertheless, in the very next year the same peoples came to an agreement once more, and, arming even those of their young men who had only just reached manhood, drew up against the Romans.

Utterly defeated in the battle, they barely brought themselves to yield, and by sending envoys to ask for a truce and a settlement they made a treaty with the Romans.

These events happened to fall in the third year before Pyrrhus's crossing into Italy, and the fifth before the destruction of the Gauls at Delphi.

For at this period fortune set, as it were, a kind of plague of war upon all the Gauls.

From the struggles just described two excellent advantages fell to the Romans: for, having grown accustomed to being cut down by the Gauls, they could neither see nor expect anything more dreadful than what had already been done to them;

and so, having become perfect athletes in the works of war, they faced Pyrrhus,

and, having crushed the daring of the Gauls at the right moment, thereafter fought without distraction—first against Pyrrhus for Italy, and after that against the Carthaginians for the supremacy over Sicily.

## Book 2 Chapter 21: War with Insubres and Boii and Gaesatae

After the defeats just described, the Gauls kept quiet for forty-five years, maintaining peace with the Romans.

But when those who had been eyewitnesses of those terrors had passed out of life with the passage of time, and a younger generation had come up—full of unreasoning passion, and with no experience or sight of any evil or any reverse—

they began once again to disturb the established order—as naturally tends to happen—being roused to anger against the Romans on trifling pretexts and calling in the Gauls from the Alps.

At first these designs were carried on apart from the common people, by the leaders themselves, in secret.

And so, when the Transalpine Gauls arrived as far as Ariminum with an armed force, the masses of the Boii grew suspicious and fell into discord both against their own leaders and against the newcomers; they put to death their own kings, Atis and Galatus, and cut one another to pieces in pitched battles.

It was then that the Romans too, alarmed at the invasion, went out with an army; but on learning of the self-inflicted destruction of the Gauls, they withdrew home again.

In the fifth year after this alarm, when Marcus Lepidus was consul, the Romans parcelled out among their citizens the territory in Gaul called Picene, from which they had defeated and expelled the Gauls called the Senones,

Gaius Flaminius having introduced this demagogic measure and policy—which indeed we must say became for the Romans, so to speak, the first cause of the people's turn for the worse, and also the cause of the war that afterward arose for them against the aforesaid Gauls.

For many of the Gauls slipped into that undertaking, and especially the Boii, because their lands bordered on Roman territory, having come to believe that the Romans were now making war on them no longer for leadership and dominion, but for their total uprooting and destruction.

## Book 2 Chapter 22: Several Gallic Tribes Join Forces

Accordingly the greatest of the tribes—those of the Insubres and the Boii—at once came to an agreement and sent messengers to the Gauls living about the Alps and along the river Rhône, who are called Gaesatae because they serve for pay; for this is what the word properly means.

To their kings, Concolitanus and Aneroestes, they offered on the spot a large sum of gold, and for the future they pointed out the greatness of Rome's prosperity and the abundance of goods that would be theirs if they prevailed; and in this way they urged them on and incited them to the campaign against the Romans.

They persuaded them easily, since along with the arguments already mentioned they gave pledges of their own alliance and reminded them of the exploit of their own ancestors—

in which those men, when they took the field, not only defeated the Romans in battle but, after the battle, seized Rome itself by direct assault;

and having become masters of all it contained and having held the city itself for seven months, they at last handed it back willingly and as an act of grace, returning home with their gains unbroken and unharmed.

When the leaders among them heard this, they were so roused to the expedition that never before had more men, or more renowned, or more warlike, gone out from these regions of Gaul.

During this same period the Romans, partly from what they heard and partly from guessing at what was to come, fell into continual fears and disturbances to such a degree

that at one time they were enrolling legions and laying in supplies of grain and provisions, and at another were even leading their forces out to the frontiers, as though the enemy were already in their land—though the Celts had not yet stirred from their own country.

This movement also did not least help the Carthaginians toward securing their affairs in Iberia.

For the Romans, as has been said by us before, judging these matters the more pressing because they lay right at their side, were forced to overlook affairs in Iberia, being eager first to put matters with the Celts on a good footing.

Therefore, having secured their position with the Carthaginians through the agreements with Hasdrubal, which we have just described, they set about with one accord at this time to deal with the enemy in Italy, considering it to their advantage to settle matters decisively with them.

## Book 2 Chapter 23: The Roman Forces

The Gaesatae Gauls, having assembled a costly and heavily armed force, came across the Alps to the river Padus in the eighth year after the distribution of the land.

Now the people of the Insubres and the Boii stood nobly by their original designs, but the Veneti and the Cenomani, after the Romans had sent envoys, chose to ally themselves with the Romans.

On this account the kings of the Celts were compelled to leave behind part of their force to guard their country against the threat from these tribes.

They themselves set out confidently with the whole army, making their march toward Etruria, with about fifty thousand foot and about twenty thousand horse and chariots.

As soon as the Romans heard that the Celts had crossed the Alps, they sent out the consul Lucius Aemilius with a force toward Ariminum to watch for the enemy's advance by that route, and one of the praetors into Etruria.

For the other of the consuls, Gaius Atilius, happened to have already gone out to Sardinia with his legions.

All those in Rome were terrified, supposing that a great and fearful danger was bearing down on them. And they felt this naturally, since the old fear of the Gauls still sat deep in their souls.

So, with this thought in mind, they partly gathered and partly enrolled legions, and ordered those of the allies who were ready to stand prepared.

In general they directed their subjects to submit registers of those of military age, eager to learn the total number of the force available to them.

And of grain, missiles, and the other equipment for war they made such great provision as no one could remember before.

Everything was supplied to them readily and from every quarter.

For the inhabitants of Italy, struck with terror at the Gauls' advance, no longer thought of themselves as allies of the Romans, nor that the war was about Roman supremacy, but each people reckoned that the danger was being brought against themselves and their own city and territory. They therefore obeyed the orders readily.

The allies with each of the two consuls were, together, thirty thousand foot and two thousand horse.

The Umbrians and Sarsinatae who inhabit the Apennines were assembled to the number of twenty thousand, and with these were twenty thousand Veneti and Cenomani. These they stationed on the frontiers of Gaul,

In Rome itself there remained, held ready against the chances of war and serving as a reserve, twenty thousand foot of the Romans themselves and with them one thousand five hundred horse, and of the allies thirty thousand foot and two thousand horse.

## Book 2 Chapter 25: Defeat of the Romans Near Faesulae

The Celts, having descended into Etruria, advanced through the country, plundering it fearlessly; and since no one opposed them, they finally pushed on against Rome itself.

When they were already near a city called Clusium, three days' march from Rome, news was brought that the Roman forces stationed in Etruria were following close on their rear and were catching up with them.

On hearing this, they turned back to meet them, eager to engage.

When the two armies had drawn near each other, already about sunset, both then encamped at a moderate distance apart and bivouacked.

When night came on, the Celts lit fires and left their cavalry behind, instructing them that at daybreak, once they had become visible to the enemy, they should withdraw along the same track.

They themselves made a secret withdrawal toward the city of Faesulae and took up position there, intending both to wait for their own cavalry and to harass the enemy's advance by surprise.

When day came, the Romans, seeing the cavalry alone and supposing the Celts had fled, eagerly pursued the cavalry along the line of their withdrawal.

As they drew near the enemy, the Celts rose up and fell upon them, and at the outset the struggle on both sides was fierce.

In the end, as the Celts had the upper hand in daring and in numbers, it came about that no fewer than six thousand of the Romans were killed and the rest fled; most of these withdrew to a certain strong position and stayed there.

The Celts at first set about besieging them; but being in a wretched state from the night's march and their hardship and exhaustion, they turned to rest and recovery, leaving a guard of their own cavalry around the hill,

intending on the following day to besiege those who had taken refuge there, unless they should surrender of their own accord.

## Book 2 Chapter 26: Aemilius Deters the Gauls

At this time Lucius Aemilius, who was posted in the regions along the Adriatic, having heard that the Celts had invaded by way of Etruria and were drawing near to Rome, came to the rescue in all haste, arriving fortunately at the critical moment.

When he had encamped close to the enemy, those who had taken refuge on the hill, seeing his fires and grasping what had happened, quickly recovered their courage and sent some of their men, unarmed, by night through the woods to report to the general what had occurred.

He, having heard them out and seeing that no other course of deliberation was left to him in the present circumstances, ordered the tribunes to lead out the infantry at daybreak, while he himself took the cavalry and led the way for the force, making his march toward the aforementioned hill.

The leaders of the Gauls, observing the fires during the night and reckoning that the enemy was at hand, held a council.

At it King Aneroestes put forward the view that, since they had become masters of so much booty—for the quantity of captives and cattle, and also of the baggage they had, was, it seems, beyond reckoning—

they ought therefore no longer to take risks nor stake everything, but should withdraw home in safety; and once they had disposed of all this and made themselves unencumbered, they should then, if it seemed good, attack the Romans' state again in full force.

Having resolved to deal with the present situation according to Aneroestes' opinion, they took this decision in the night, broke camp before daylight, and advanced along the coast through the country of the Etruscans.

Lucius, taking up from the hill the surviving part of the army together with his own forces, judged that it was in no way to his advantage to risk a pitched battle, but rather to watch for favorable times and places as he followed, in case he might somewhere be able to harm the enemy or strip away part of the booty.

## Book 2 Chapter 27: Atilius Meets the Gauls

At this same time the consul Gaius Atilius, having sailed down from Sardinia with his legions to Pisae, was advancing with his force toward Rome, making his march toward the enemy from the opposite direction.

When the Celts were already near Telamon in Etruria, their foragers fell in with the advance party from Gaius's force and were captured;

and on being questioned they made clear to the general what had taken place and reported the presence of both armies, indicating that the Celts were very close at hand and that behind them came the forces of Lucius.

He, partly surprised at this news and partly becoming hopeful at the thought that the Celts had been caught in the middle on the march, ordered the tribunes to draw up the legions and advance at a walk to the front, as far as the ground would allow a frontal advance.

He himself, having opportunely spotted a hill lying above the road, beneath which the Celts had to march, took his cavalry and pressed on, eager to seize the crest first and to be the first to open the action, convinced that in this way he would win for himself the greatest share of the credit for the outcome.

The Celts at first, unaware of the presence of Atilius's force and supposing from what was happening that Aemilius's cavalry had marched around during the night and were seizing the positions ahead of them, immediately sent out their own cavalry and some of the light troops to contest the ground about the hill.

But on quickly learning of Gaius's presence from one of the prisoners who had been brought in, they hastily drew up their infantry, forming them up to face both ways at once—both to the rear and to the front;

At first the action at the hill itself was the whole of the fighting, plainly visible to all, since so great a number of cavalry from each army had fallen in together in confused combat.

In this engagement it happened that the consul Gaius, fighting recklessly in hand-to-hand combat, lost his life, and his head was carried off to the kings of the Celts; while the Roman cavalry, after struggling stoutly, finally prevailed over both the position and the enemy.

After this, when the infantry forces were now close upon one another, the event that took place was remarkable and astonishing—not only to those present at that very moment, but also to those able afterward to bring it before their own eyes from the accounts of it.

## Book 2 Chapter 29: A Peculiar and Surprising Battle

This was the way the engagement unfolded. In the first place, since the battle was made up of three separate forces, it is clear that the spectacle, as well as the actual fighting of the troops drawn up in this fashion, must have looked strange and out of the ordinary.

In the second place, one might well be uncertain — both now and at the time, for anyone present at the very moment — whether the Celts held the most perilous position of all, with the enemy pressing in on them from both directions at once,

or, on the contrary, the most advantageous one: fighting against both forces simultaneously, yet securing their own safety from either side by means of the men at their backs, and, most important of all, having every avenue of retreat to the rear cut off, along with any hope of survival should they give way. For this is precisely the special quality of a formation that faces two ways.

Such, indeed, is the advantage of a double-fronted line of battle.

As for the Romans, on the one hand they were emboldened by having caught the enemy in the middle and surrounded them on every side; on the other hand they were unnerved by the splendor and the din of the Celtic army.

For the number of horn-blowers and trumpeters was beyond counting, and when, along with these, the whole army joined in raising its war cry together, the clamor that arose was so great and of such a character that not only the trumpets and the men, but even the surrounding country, seemed to echo back and to be giving off a voice of its own.

Terrifying too were the appearance and the gestures of the naked warriors who stood in the front line, men in the very prime of their strength and conspicuous for their fine physique.

And all those who held the foremost companies were adorned with gold torques and armlets.

At the sight of all this the Romans were on the one hand dismayed, but on the other, drawn on by the hope of profit, they were spurred on twice as keenly toward the

danger. But as soon as the javelin-throwers advanced in front of the Roman legions, in keeping with their custom, and let fly with vigorous and densely massed volleys of missiles, the Celts in the rear ranks found their cloaks and trousers of great use to them;

In this way the spirit of the Gaesatae was broken down by the javelin-throwers.

But the main body of the Insubres, Boii, and Taurisci — once the Romans had taken back their own javelin-throwers and the companies charged at them — clashed with the enemy and put up a stubborn hand-to-hand fight.

For though they were being cut down, they held firm in spirit on equal terms, inferior only in this one respect — both as a whole and man for man — namely in the construction of their weapons.

The Roman shields, that is, offered far greater protection, and their swords were far more effective in action, while the Gallic sword could only deliver a downward cut.

And when the Roman cavalry charged in from higher ground against the flank, riding down from the hill and pressing the attack vigorously, then the Celtic infantry were cut to pieces right where they stood in their line, while their cavalry took to flight.
## Book 2 Chapter 31: Aemilius Returns Victorious

About forty thousand of the Celts were killed, and no fewer than ten thousand taken prisoner, among them one of their kings, Concolitanus.

The other king, Aneroestes, fled with a few men to a certain place and there laid hands on himself and on his close companions.

The Roman general gathered the spoils and sent them to Rome, and restored the plunder to its rightful owners.

Then, taking up his legions and marching along the very edge of Liguria, he invaded the territory of the Boii. After satisfying the legions' appetite for plunder, within a few days he returned with his forces to Rome.

He adorned the Capitol with the standards and the torques — a torque being the gold collar that the Gauls wear around their necks —

while the rest of the spoils and the captives he used for his own entry into the city and for the decoration of his triumph.

So this, the most formidable Celtic onslaught, was destroyed in this manner — an invasion that had hung over all the Italians, and especially the Romans, as a great and fearful danger.

Emboldened by this success, the Romans came to hope that they would be able to drive the Celts out of the regions around the Padus altogether. They therefore sent out both of the consuls appointed for the following year, Quintus Fulvius and Titus Manlius, together with their forces and large-scale preparations, against the Celts.

These consuls, by terrifying the Boii in a sudden assault, forced them to put themselves under Rome's protection; but for the remainder of the campaign,

they accomplished nothing in the end. After these men, Publius Furius and Gaius Flaminius were appointed and once again invaded Celtic territory, marching through the country of the Anares, who happen to live not far from Massalia.

Having won this people over to friendship, they crossed into the land of the Insubres at the confluence of the rivers Adua and Padus.

After suffering some losses both during the crossing and around their camp, they stayed put for the moment; but afterward they made a truce and withdrew from the region by agreement.

Then, after marching around for several days and crossing the river Clusius, they came into the country of the Cenomani, and taking these people along as allies, they invaded once more — descending from the Alpine regions into the plains of the Insubres — and laid waste the land and plundered their settlements.

The chiefs of the Insubres, seeing that the Romans' purpose was unshakable, decided to put their fortune to the test and to risk a full, decisive battle against them.

So they gathered all their available forces in one place, took down the golden standards known as "the immovables" from the temple of Athena, made the rest of their preparations as needed, and then, with confidence and in formidable array, encamped opposite the enemy, numbering some fifty thousand men.

The Romans, on the one hand, seeing that they were far outnumbered by their opponents, wished to make use of the forces of the Celts allied with them;

but on the other hand, reflecting on Gallic faithlessness and on the fact that they were about to fight against men of the same race as these auxiliaries, they were wary of sharing such a moment and such an undertaking with men of that sort.

In the end, then, they themselves remained on their own side of the river, while the Celts who were with them they sent across to the far bank, and then tore up the bridges over the stream —

at once securing themselves against those allies, and at the same time leaving themselves only one hope of survival, namely in victory, since the aforementioned river lay impassable at their backs.

Having done this, they were ready to risk the battle.
## Book 2 Chapter 33: Tactics Against the Gauls

The Romans are thought to have handled this battle intelligently, for the military tribunes showed how the fighting ought to be conducted, both collectively and by each man individually.

For they had observed from earlier engagements that the whole Gallic race is most fearsome in its first charge, in its fury, so long as that fury is still fresh;

and that their swords, by their construction, as has been said before, have only one effective downward stroke — the first — but after that they immediately lose their edge, bending both lengthwise and crosswise so badly that, unless the men are given time to straighten them by setting them against the ground with the foot, their second blow is utterly useless.

So the tribunes distributed the spears of the triarii, who stood in reserve behind, to the men in the front companies, and ordered them to use their swords only after switching weapons; and they engaged the Celts in pitched battle, facing them head-on.

As soon as the Gauls made their swords useless by their first downward strokes against the spears, the Romans closed to hand-to-hand range and rendered the Celts helpless, robbing them of their slashing style of fighting — which is peculiar to Gallic warfare — since their sword has no point for thrusting at all.

The Romans, by contrast, used their swords not for cutting but for thrusting, holding them straight, since the point on them is effective; and striking at the chests and faces and landing blow upon blow, they killed the greater part of those drawn up against them, thanks to the foresight of the tribunes.

For the consul Flaminius is thought not to have made good use of the engagement described above. By forming his line right along the very brow of the river, he ruined what is distinctive about Roman tactics, leaving no room for the companies to fall back step by step.

For if the men had been pressed back even slightly from their ground during the battle, they would have had to throw themselves into the river, all because of their commander's blunder.

Nevertheless, by their own valor they won a decisive victory, as I have said, and after taking abundant plunder and no small amount of spoils, they returned to Rome.
## Book 2 Chapter 34: Capture of Mediolanum and End of the War

In the following year, when the Celts sent an embassy seeking peace and promising to do anything, the newly appointed consuls Marcus Claudius and Gnaeus Cornelius pressed hard that no peace be granted them.

The Celts, having failed in this and resolving to put their last hopes to the test, once more set about hiring about thirty thousand of the Gaesatae Gauls who live around the Rhone. Having taken these into service, they kept them at the ready and awaited the enemy's attack.

The Roman generals, when the season came, took up their forces and led them into the country of the Insubres.

Arriving there and encamping around the city of Acerrae, which lies between the Padus and the Alpine mountains, they laid siege to it.

The Insubres, unable to bring relief because the favorable positions had already been seized, but eager to break the siege of Acerrae, sent a portion of their force across the Padus into the country of the Anares and laid siege to the place called Clastidium. When news of what was happening reached the generals,

Marcus Claudius took the cavalry and some of the infantry and hurried off, eager to relieve the besieged.

The Celts, learning of the enemy's arrival, raised the siege, came out to meet them, and drew up for battle.

When the Romans, with the cavalry alone, boldly charged them in a sudden assault, they held out at first; but afterward, with the enemy surrounding them both at the rear and on the flank, they were hard-pressed in the fighting and were finally routed by the cavalry alone.

Many of them fell into the river and were destroyed by the current, while the greater number were cut down by the enemy.

The Romans also captured Acerrae, full of grain, after the Gauls withdrew to Mediolanum, which is the principal stronghold of the Insubres' territory.

Gnaeus followed close on their heels and made a sudden assault on Mediolanum; at first the Gauls kept quiet,

but when he started back again toward Acerrae, they sallied out, boldly fell upon his rearguard, killed many men, and even forced a part of his force to flee,

until Gnaeus called back the men from the vanguard and urged them to stand and engage the enemy.

The Romans, obeying their general, fought stoutly against those pressing on them.

The Celts, on account of their present success, held their ground confidently for a while, but before long they were routed and fled to the foothills. Gnaeus pursued them, ravaged the countryside, and
## Book 2 Chapter 35: Character of the Gauls

took Mediolanum by storm. When this happened, the chiefs of the Insubres, despairing of any hope of safety, surrendered everything they had to the Romans.

Such, then, was the outcome of the war against the Celts. In terms of the desperate fury and daring of the men who fought it, and further in terms of the battles and the number of those killed and engaged in them, it was inferior to none recorded in history;

but in terms of strategic planning and the lack of judgment in the conduct of its individual stages, it was utterly contemptible — because not the greater part, but absolutely everything the Gauls did was governed by passion rather than by reason.

Having observed for myself how these people were driven out of the plains around the Padus not long afterward — except for a few localities lying right at the foot of the Alps — I decided that I ought not to leave unrecorded either their original invasion, or their subsequent actions, or their final expulsion,

believing it proper to the work of history to bring such episodes of Fortune into memory and to hand them down to those who come after,

so that our descendants may not be utterly ignorant of these things and thus be terrified by the sudden and unexpected attacks of the barbarians, but rather, bearing in mind to some degree how short-lived and how easily broken the barbarian host is, may withstand their onslaught — those who face danger with judgment — and may exhaust all their own resources before yielding any of what is essential.

For I believe that those who recorded for us, and handed down to memory, the Persians' invasion of Greece and the Gauls' invasion of Delphi contributed in no small way — indeed greatly — to the struggles waged for the common freedom of the Greeks.

For no one, dismayed by an abundance of supplies or arms or men, would abandon his last hope — that of fighting to the end for his own land and homeland — if he kept before his eyes the astonishing nature of what happened then, and remembered how many myriads of men, what daring, and how vast the preparations were that the resolve and power of those who faced danger with judgment and calculation brought to nothing.

And since the dread of the Gauls has dismayed the Greeks repeatedly, not only in ancient times but even down to our own day,

I was all the more moved to give an account of these matters — summary in form, yet reaching back to the beginning.

## Book 2 Chapter 36: Hasdrubal Dies and Hannibal Succeeds Him

Now Hasdrubal, the Carthaginian general — for it was from his affairs that we digressed in our narrative — died after governing the affairs of Iberia for eight years. He was treacherously murdered at night in his own quarters by a certain man of Celtic stock, in revenge for private wrongs.

He had brought about no small but a great advance in the fortunes of Carthage, not so much through his military exploits as through his dealings with the native princes.

The Carthaginians then conferred the command in Iberia on Hannibal, despite his youth, on account of the shrewdness and daring that his conduct had already begun to display.

As soon as he took up the command, it was clear from his designs that he intended to make war on Rome — and this in fact he soon did, after waiting only a very short time.

From this period onward, then, relations between the Carthaginians and the Romans were marked by mutual suspicion and friction.

For the Carthaginians were plotting, eager to take their revenge for their losses around Sicily, while the Romans were distrustful, observing what the Carthaginians were planning.

From all this it was clear to anyone who judged the situation correctly that the two peoples would be at war with each other before long.

## Book 2 Chapter 37: Greece At This Time

At the same period the Achaeans and King Philip, together with their other allies, were embarking on the war against the Aetolians known as the Social War.

Now, having carried our account of the affairs of Sicily and Libya, and of what followed, continuously through the preliminary portion of the work down to the beginning of the Social War and of the second war between Rome and Carthage — the one most people call the Hannibalic War — and since from the outset it was my declared intention to make this period the starting point of my own composition,

it would be fitting to set those matters aside and turn to events in Greece, so that, having brought my preliminary survey from every quarter to the same level and the same point in time, I may then at last begin the demonstrative history proper.

For I have not undertaken, as my predecessors did, to record particular sets of events — Greek history, say, or Persian — but rather the events in all the known parts of the world at once, because the times we live in have contributed something distinctive toward this aspect of my plan, of which I shall give a clearer account elsewhere.

It would therefore be proper, even before the main construction of the work, to touch briefly on the most prominent and best-known nations and regions of the inhabited world.

Now as for the affairs of Asia and Egypt, it will be enough to begin our account of them from the period just mentioned, since the earlier history of those countries has been published by many writers and is familiar to everyone, and since in our own day nothing so unusual or unexpected has befallen them at the hands of fortune as to require any reference back to their past.

But as for the Achaean nation and the royal house of Macedonia, it will be appropriate to go back briefly in time — since the latter has now been utterly extinguished,

while in the case of the Achaeans, as I said above, there has occurred in our own time an extraordinary growth and a coming-together in unity.

For although many men in past times had set out to bring the Peloponnesians together for their common advantage, and not one had succeeded — because each was exerting himself not for the freedom of all but for his own personal power —

yet in our own day this enterprise has reached such progress and completion that there is among them not merely an alliance and friendly partnership in their affairs, but they also use the same laws, weights, measures, and currency, and in addition the same magistrates, councillors,

and judges. In general, the only respect in which the whole Peloponnese falls short of being a single city is that its inhabitants are not enclosed within one and the same wall; in all other respects, both collectively and city by city, their institutions are nearly identical.

## Book 2 Chapter 38: Origin of the Name "Achaean"

First, it is worth learning how and in what way the name of the Achaeans came to prevail over all the Peloponnesians.

For those who from the beginning bore this name as their ancestral title are not distinguished by any greater extent of territory and cities, nor by greater wealth, nor by the valor of their men.

The Arcadian people, and likewise the Laconian, far surpass them in numbers of men and in extent of land; and indeed neither of these would ever consent to yield the first place in courage to any of the Greeks.

How then, and for what reason, are these peoples and the rest of the Peloponnesians now content to share both the constitution and the name of the Achaeans?

It would clearly be quite inappropriate to call it the work of chance — that is a feeble answer; rather, one must seek a cause, for without a cause nothing, whether it seems to follow reason or to defy it, can be brought about. The cause, in my opinion, is something like this.

Nowhere could one find a system and a principle of equality before the law, of free speech, and in a word of genuine democracy, more pure than that which exists among the Achaeans.

This constitution found some of the Peloponnesians willing converts of their own accord, and won over many by persuasion and argument; and those whom it constrained at the right moment it presently brought, even so, to acquiesce in it.

For since it reserved no special advantage for the original members, but granted equal rights to all newcomers alike, it swiftly attained its purpose, using the two most powerful aids: equality and humane treatment.

This, then, must be regarded as the originating and contributing cause of the Peloponnesians' coming to share one purpose and establishing the prosperity they now enjoy.

This principle and this distinctive character of their constitution, as just described, had existed among the Achaeans in earlier times as well.

This could be shown by many other proofs, but for the present it will suffice, for the sake of confidence, to bring forward one or two pieces of evidence.

## Book 2 Chapter 39: The First Achaean League

At the time when, in the regions of Italy then called Magna Graecia, the meeting-houses of the Pythagoreans were burned down,

there followed, as was natural, a complete upheaval of the political order, since the leading men of each city had thus been destroyed in so unexpected a fashion;

and the Greek cities in those parts came to be filled with slaughter, factional strife, and confusion of every kind.

At that time, when embassies came from most parts of Greece to try to settle these disorders, the cities turned to the Achaeans and to their good faith for deliverance from the evils that beset them.

And it was not only on this occasion that they showed their approval of the Achaean way; some time later they set out wholeheartedly to become imitators of their constitution.

For the people of Croton, Sybaris, and Caulonia, after inviting one another and reaching a common understanding, first established a shared temple and precinct of Zeus Homarios, in which they held their meetings and deliberations, and then, adopting the customs and laws of the Achaeans, undertook to use them and to govern their state accordingly. But under the pressure of the despotic power of Dionysius of Syracuse,

and likewise of the domination of the neighboring barbarians, they were hindered, and abandoned these arrangements — not willingly, but under compulsion.

Later, when the Lacedaemonians had suffered their unexpected defeat at the battle of Leuctra, and the Thebans had unexpectedly laid claim to the leadership of Greece, there was great uncertainty among all the Greeks, and especially among the two peoples just named, since the one would not admit it had been beaten, and the other did not believe it had won.

Nevertheless, in their dispute the Thebans and the Lacedaemonians entrusted the points at issue to the Achaeans alone of all the Greeks — not with any regard to their power,

for at that time they had perhaps the smallest power of any people in Greece, but chiefly out of regard for their good faith and their general integrity. For that was, by common consent, the reputation everyone then held of them.

At that period, then, these qualities of principle existed among them only in bare form; but no achievement or notable action tending to the growth of their own power came about,

because they were unable to produce a leader worthy of their principles. Whenever someone gave promise of it, he was overshadowed and held back, at one time by the dominance of the Lacedaemonians, at another, still more, by

## Book 2 Chapter 40: Unification of the Peloponnese

that of the Macedonians. But when at last, in due time, the nation found leaders of sufficient worth, it quickly made its own power manifest by accomplishing that most glorious of works — the concord of the Peloponnesians.

As the originator and prime mover of the whole undertaking we must reckon Aratus of Sicyon; as the champion who carried the work through to completion, Philopoemen of Megalopolis; and as the man who secured its lasting permanence for a time, Lycortas and those who shared his policy.

What each of these men did, and how, and at what times, I shall try to make clear, always giving my attention to it in the manner appropriate to the narrative.

As for the measures carried out by Aratus, I shall mention them in summary both now and again later on, because he himself composed thoroughly truthful and lucid memoirs of his own deeds;

but for the others I shall give a more detailed and discriminating account. I suppose the easiest course for me, and the one that will make the subject easiest for my readers to follow, will be to take my starting point from the period when, the Achaean nation having been broken up city by city by the kings of Macedon, a new beginning was made and the cities once more drew together toward one another.

From that time the nation, growing continuously, advanced to the state of completion in which it stood in our own day, about which I have just spoken in some detail.

## Book 2 Chapter 41: The Second League

It was the 124th Olympiad when the people of Patrae and Dyme first began to act in concert,

the period in which Ptolemy son of Lagus, Lysimachus, and also Seleucus and Ptolemy Ceraunus departed this life — for all these died around the Olympiad just mentioned.

Now in the times before this, the situation of the aforesaid nation was roughly as follows.

They had been ruled by kings ever since Tisamenus, who was the son of Orestes and who, having been expelled from Sparta on the return of the Heraclidae, took possession of the regions around Achaea;

from him they were ruled by kings of the same line in unbroken succession down to Ogyges. After that, becoming dissatisfied with the sons of Ogyges because they ruled them not constitutionally but despotically, they changed their government into a democracy.

Thereafter, throughout the following times down to the supremacy of Alexander and Philip, their fortunes varied with circumstances, now one way, now another; but their common government, as we have said, they tried to maintain as a democracy.

This league consisted of twelve cities, which still survive today, except for Olenus and for Helice, which was swallowed up by the sea before the events at Leuctra.

These cities are: Patrae, Dyme, Pharae, Tritaea, Leontium, Aegium, Aegeira, Pellene, Bura, and Caryneia.

But in the period after the time of Alexander and before the Olympiad just mentioned, they fell into such discord and ill condition — chiefly through the agency of the kings of Macedon — that all the cities became separated from one another and pursued interests opposed to each other.

As a result, some of them came to have garrisons imposed on them by Demetrius and Cassander, and afterwards by Antigonus Gonatas, while others fell under the rule of tyrants; for this last king seems to have planted more absolute rulers among the Greeks than anyone else.

But around the 124th Olympiad, as I said above, they changed their minds and began once again to act in concert. This was at the time of Pyrrhus's crossing into Italy.

The first to form the union were the peoples of Dyme, Patrae, Tritaea, and Pharae; and for this reason there happens to be no monument recording the federation of these cities.

After this, in about the fifth year, the people of Aegium expelled their garrison and joined the federation; next to them the people of Bura, after killing their tyrant.

At the same time the people of Caryneia were restored to it. For Iseas, then tyrant of Caryneia, seeing that the garrison had been driven out of Aegium and that the despot in Bura had been destroyed by Margos and the Achaeans, and seeing himself on the point of being attacked from every side,

laid down his power and, after receiving guarantees from the Achaeans for his safety, attached his city to the Achaean confederation.

## Book 2 Chapter 42: Policy of the Achaean League

For what reason, then, did I go back to these times? First, so that it might become clear how, at what periods, and which of the original Achaeans were the first to undertake again the formation of the present league;

and second, so that their guiding policy might be confirmed not merely by my own assertion but by the facts themselves — namely, that the Achaeans always held to one and the same principle.

In accordance with this principle, holding out the equality before the law and the freedom of speech that prevailed among them, and continually making war on and crushing those who, whether on their own account or with the help of the kings, were enslaving their respective homelands, they accomplished this work in this manner and with this aim — partly by their own efforts, partly with the help of their allies.

For indeed whatever was achieved toward this end through those allies in later times must be credited to the deliberate policy of the Achaeans.

For though they took part in many enterprises with others, and in very many and very fine ones with the Romans, they never at any time sought from their successes any private advantage for themselves;

rather, in exchange for all the zeal they offered their allies, they bargained for nothing but the freedom of each people and the common concord of the Peloponnesians.

But it will be possible to grasp these things more clearly from the actual record of their deeds.

## Book 2 Chapter 43: Margos, the First Sole Strategus

For the first twenty-five years, the cities I have named governed themselves jointly, choosing in rotation a common secretary and two generals.

After this they decided instead to appoint a single man and to entrust him with authority over the whole confederacy. The first to receive this honor was Margos of Caryneia.

In the fourth year after this man held the office, Aratus of Sicyon—then only twenty years old, having freed his native city from tyranny through his own courage and daring—brought it into the Achaean union, for from the very start he had been an enthusiast for their political ideals.

Then, in the eighth year after that, having been elected general for the second time, he laid a secret plot against the Acrocorinth, which Antigonus held, and seized it. By gaining control of it he relieved the inhabitants of the Peloponnese of a great fear, and by liberating the Corinthians he brought them into the Achaean union.

During the same term of office he also won over the city of Megara and attached it to the Achaeans.

These events took place in the year before the defeat of the Carthaginians, the defeat that made them withdraw entirely from Sicily and submit, for the first time, to paying tribute to Rome.

Having advanced his design so greatly in so short a time, Aratus thereafter continued as leader of the Achaean nation, directing all his plans and actions toward a single goal:

namely, to expel the Macedonians from the Peloponnese, to abolish the autocracies, and to secure for each people the common freedom their ancestors had enjoyed.

So long as Antigonus Gonatas lived, then, Aratus persisted in opposing both that king's meddling and the rapacity of the Aetolians,

handling each matter with practical skill—even though the two of them had advanced so far in injustice and reckless daring that they made a formal compact with each other for

## Book 2 Chapter 44: The Death of Demetrius

the dismemberment of the Achaean nation. But when Antigonus died, the Achaeans came to terms and made an alliance with the Aetolians, who joined them honorably in the war against Demetrius; and so, for the time being, the estrangement and ill will were set aside, and a sense of partnership and friendship grew up between them.

When Demetrius, after a reign of only ten years, died at about the time of the Romans' first crossing into Illyria, events flowed favorably toward the Achaeans' original design.

For the autocrats in the Peloponnese fell into despair: Demetrius, who had been, as it were, their patron and paymaster, was dead; and Aratus was pressing hard upon them, insisting that they ought to lay down their tyrannies, holding out great gifts and honors to those who complied, while threatening those who resisted with still greater terrors and dangers at the hands of the Achaeans.

So they were driven to comply—to give up their tyrannies, to set their own native cities free, and to join the Achaean union.

Now Lydiades of Megalopolis, while Demetrius was still alive, had on his own initiative—shrewdly and prudently foreseeing what was to come—laid down his tyranny and joined the national confederacy.

And Aristomachus, tyrant of Argos, Xenon, tyrant of Hermione, and Cleonymus, tyrant of Phlius, now likewise gave up their autocracies and shared in the Achaean democracy.

## Book 2 Chapter 45: The Aetolians Envy the Achaeans

But as the nation grew and prospered all the more on this account, the Aetolians, prompted by their innate injustice and greed, grew envious—and, even more, hoped to carve up the cities among themselves, just as before they had divided the cities of Acarnania with Alexander and had schemed to divide those of the Achaeans with Antigonus Gonatas.

So now, buoyed up by similar hopes, they had the audacity to make common cause and join hands at once with both Antigonus—who at that time was head of the Macedonians, acting as guardian of the boy Philip—and Cleomenes, king of the Lacedaemonians.

For they saw that Antigonus held secure control of Macedonia, and that he was an acknowledged and open enemy of the Achaeans because of their plot that had captured the Acrocorinth.

They reckoned that if they brought in the Lacedaemonians as additional partners in the scheme and pushed them first into hostility against the nation, they could easily crush the Achaeans by attacking them at the right moment and surrounding them with war on every side.

And in all likelihood they would quickly have accomplished this, had they not overlooked the most crucial point in their plan: they failed to reckon that in their designs they would have Aratus as an opponent—a man capable of meeting every emergency.

And so, when they set out to meddle and to launch unjust attacks, far from achieving any of their aims, they did the opposite: they actually strengthened Aratus, then the leader, and the nation itself, since he skillfully drew them off and wrecked their plans.

How the whole affair was managed will become clear from what I am about to relate.

## Book 2 Chapter 46: The Intrigues of the Aetolians

For Aratus observed that the Aetolians were ashamed to take up war against the Achaeans openly, since the favors the Achaeans had done them in the Demetrian War were still very fresh,

but that they were conspiring with the Lacedaemonians and envied the Achaeans so deeply that, when Cleomenes had treacherously seized from them Tegea, Mantinea, and Orchomenus—cities that were not only Aetolian allies but at that time actually members of their own league—they were not the least indignant about it, but even confirmed his takeover;

and whereas in the past, out of greed, they had treated any pretext as sufficient grounds for going to war against people who had done them no wrong, now Aratus saw them willingly letting their treaties be violated and deliberately giving up their greatest cities for one purpose only—to see Cleomenes become a formidable adversary to the Achaeans.

Seeing all this, Aratus—and likewise all the leaders of the Achaean state—decided that they should not initiate war against anyone, but should resist the designs of the Lacedaemonians.

At first, then, they held to this view; but as time went on, when they saw Cleomenes boldly building the fort called the Athenaeum in the territory of Megalopolis and openly revealing himself as their bitter enemy,

then at last they convened the Achaeans and resolved, together with the council, to take up open hostility against the Lacedaemonians.

This, then, was how the war called the Cleomenic War began, and at about these

## Book 2 Chapter 47: The Cleomenic War

times. At first the Achaeans set out to face the Lacedaemonians with their own forces, partly because they thought it most honorable not to obtain their safety through others, but to preserve their cities and territory by themselves,

and partly because they wished to maintain their friendship with Ptolemy, on account of the benefits he had bestowed earlier, and not to appear to be reaching out to others for help.

But when the war had gone on for some time, and Cleomenes had abolished his country's ancestral constitution and converted its lawful kingship into a tyranny, and was waging the war with energy and reckless daring,

Aratus, foreseeing what was to come and fearing the desperate audacity of the Aetolians, decided that he must spoil their plan well in advance.

He saw that Antigonus was a man of action and intelligence who laid claim to a reputation for trustworthiness, while he knew perfectly well that kings by nature regard no one as either an ally or an enemy, but always measure their hatreds and friendships by the calculus of self-interest.

So he resolved to open communication with that king and to join hands with him, pointing out to him the outcome that his present circumstances would bring.

To do this openly, however, he judged inexpedient for several reasons. For one thing, it would prepare Cleomenes and the Aetolians as adversaries to the scheme;

for another, it would dishearten the mass of the Achaeans, since by taking refuge with their enemies he would seem to have utterly given up his hopes in them—which was the very last impression he wanted to create.

For this reason, holding to such a purpose, he planned to manage the matter in secret.

As a result, contrary to his own real intentions, he was often forced to say and do things in public through which, by presenting the opposite impression, he meant to conceal his actual management of affairs.

For this reason he did not even record some of these things in his memoirs.

## Book 2 Chapter 48: Aratus Involves Megalopolis

Aratus knew that the people of Megalopolis were suffering badly in the war, because, lying next to Sparta, they had to bear the brunt of the fighting ahead of the others, yet were not receiving the proper support from the Achaeans, since the Achaeans too were hard-pressed and crippled by their circumstances.

He also knew clearly that they were favorably disposed toward the Macedonian royal house because of the benefits they had received in the time of Philip, son of Amyntas.

So he understood that, once hard-pressed by Cleomenes, they would quickly take refuge with Antigonus and the hopes that Macedonia offered.

Accordingly, he confided his whole plan in secret to Nicophanes and Cercidas of Megalopolis, who were his family's hereditary guest-friends and well suited to the undertaking.

Through them he easily prompted the Megalopolitans to send an embassy to the Achaeans and urge them to appeal to Antigonus for help.

So the Megalopolitans appointed Nicophanes and Cercidas themselves as envoys to the Achaeans, and from there straight on to Antigonus, should the nation agree.

The Achaeans gave the Megalopolitans permission to send the embassy.

Nicophanes and his colleagues hastened to meet the king, and concerning their own city they spoke only of the essentials, briefly and in summary; but most of what they said, following Aratus's instructions, concerned

## Book 2 Chapter 49: The Message to Antigonus Doson from Aratus

the broader situation, in accordance with his directions. These were: to point out what the collaboration between the Aetolians and Cleomenes amounted to and where it was heading, and to make clear that the Achaeans themselves had primarily to be on their guard, but next—and even more so—Antigonus.

For one thing, it was plain to everyone that the Achaeans could not withstand a war from both sides at once; and for another, that if the Aetolians and Cleomenes prevailed over the Achaeans, they would not rest content nor stop at the present situation—and this was even easier for a sensible man to see than the first point.

For the greed of the Aetolians would never be content to be confined within the boundaries of the Peloponnese—nor even within those of Greece;

and the ambition of Cleomenes, his whole design, was for the present aimed at the rule of the Peloponnese, but once he gained that, he would at once reach for the leadership of all the Greeks.

And that leadership he could not attain without first overthrowing the Macedonian empire.

They therefore asked him to consider, looking ahead to the future, which course better served his own interests: to fight Cleomenes in the Peloponnese for the leadership of Greece alongside the Achaeans and Boeotians, or to abandon the greatest nation and stake the Macedonian empire on a battle in Thessaly against the Aetolians and Boeotians—and the Achaeans and Lacedaemonians as well.

If, then, the Aetolians—out of regard for the goodwill the Achaeans had shown them in the time of Demetrius—were to pretend to keep quiet, as they were doing even now, the Achaeans, they said, would wage war against Cleomenes on their own; and if fortune lent its support, they would have no need of anyone to help them. But if fortune turned against them,

and the Aetolians joined in the attack, they urged him to keep an eye on developments, so that he might not let the right moment slip, but might come to the aid of the Peloponnesians while they could still be saved.

As for good faith and the repayment of his kindness, they thought he need not trouble himself: once the service was rendered, they pledged that Aratus would himself find guarantees satisfactory to both parties.

Likewise, they said, he would himself indicate the right moment for the help to be given.

## Book 2 Chapter 50: Antigonus Doson Will Help the League

When Antigonus heard these arguments, he judged that Aratus was laying out the situation honestly and practically, and he attended carefully to the next steps. He also wrote to the people of Megalopolis,

promising to send help, provided that the Achaeans, too, were willing that this should happen.

When Nicophanes and Cercidas returned home, delivered the king's letters, and made clear the rest of his goodwill and his eagerness for the cause,

the Megalopolitans were elated and were keen to go to the assembly of the Achaeans and urge them to win Antigonus over and to place the conduct of affairs in his hands without delay.

Aratus, having heard privately from Nicophanes about the disposition the king held both toward the Achaeans and toward himself, was overjoyed that his scheme had not been undertaken in vain, and that he had not found Antigonus, as the Aetolians had hoped, utterly estranged from him.

He also considered it very much to his advantage that the Megalopolitans were eager to bring the matter to Antigonus through the agency of the Achaeans. For above all,

as I said earlier, his goal was to avoid needing the assistance at all; but if it should become unavoidable to fall back on it, he wanted the invitation to come not through himself alone, but still more from all the Achaeans together.

For he was anxious that, should the king come, defeat Cleomenes and the Lacedaemonians in the war, and then adopt some less favorable policy toward their common constitution, he himself might by general agreement bear the blame for whatever followed—

since Antigonus would be thought to be acting justly, on account of the wrong previously done by Aratus himself against the house of Macedon in the matter of the Acrocorinth.

Therefore, as soon as the Megalopolitans came before the common council, displayed the letters to the Achaeans, made clear the king's full goodwill, and on top of this asked them to secure Antigonus's alliance as quickly as possible—and as the assembly too was of the same inclination—

Aratus came forward, welcomed the king's eagerness and praised the disposition of the majority, but then in a lengthy speech urged them above all to try to preserve their cities and their territory by their own efforts: for nothing, he said, was more honorable or more advantageous than this;

and only if fortune turned against them in this effort, he said, should they—after first exhausting every hope that lay within themselves—then

## Book 2 Chapter 51: The Achaeans Must Appeal to Antigonus

But Ptolemy, despairing of the league, set about subsidizing Cleomenes instead—wishing to set him up against Antigonus, because he had greater hopes that the Lacedaemonians, rather than the Achaeans, could hold in check the designs of the Macedonian kings.

## Book 2 Chapter 55: Capture of Megalopolis

About this time Cleomenes observed that the forces had been disbanded, that Antigonus with his mercenaries was lingering at Aegium, three days' march distant from Megalopolis,

and that this city was hard to guard because of its size and the thinness of its population, and was at that moment being watched all the more carelessly because of Antigonus's presence in the country. Most important of all, he knew that the greater part of its men of military age had perished in the battle at Lycaeum and afterward in the battle at Ladocea.

So, getting the help of some Messenian exiles who happened to be staying in Megalopolis, he slipped in by night through their agency, undetected, inside the walls.

When day broke, he came within a little of not merely being driven out but of risking total disaster, on account of the courage of the Megalopolitans.

This was just what had happened to him three months earlier, when he had broken in by the part of the city called the Colaeum.

On this occasion, however, by the size of his force and by seizing the favorable positions in advance, he accomplished his design, and in the end, driving out the Megalopolitans, he took possession of the city.

Having made himself master of it, he destroyed it so bitterly and so malignantly that no one could even hope it might ever be resettled.

He did this, it seems to me, because among the Megalopolitans and the Stymphalians alone, throughout the vicissitudes of those times, he had never been able to procure either a partisan and accomplice in his own designs, or a traitor.

For as to the Clitorians, their love of freedom and their nobility were disgraced by the baseness of a single man, Thearces—whom the Clitorians, with good reason, deny was born among them, asserting that he was a changeling foisted in from Orchomenus, the son of one of the foreign garrison soldiers there.

## Book 2 Chapter 56: The Credibility of Phylarchus

Now, since among those who have written about the same period as Aratus, Phylarchus is, in some quarters, held worthy of acceptance—though on many points he holds opposing views and writes the contrary of Aratus—

it would be useful, or rather necessary for us, since we have chosen to follow Aratus concerning the Cleomenic affairs, not to leave this matter unexamined, lest we allow falsehood in written works to carry weight equal to the truth.

In general, then, this writer has throughout his whole work said much at random and as chance dictated.

But while it is perhaps not necessary to censure him on the other matters at present, or to scrutinize them closely, those of his statements that bear on the period I am now writing about—that is, the affairs of the Cleomenic war—on these it is necessary for us to give a precise account.

These will be quite sufficient to enable us to understand his whole purpose and ability in his work.

Wishing, then, to set forth the cruelty of Antigonus and the Macedonians, and along with theirs that of Aratus and the Achaeans, he says that the Mantineans, when they fell into their hands, met with great misfortunes, and that the most ancient and greatest city of Arcadia struggled with such great calamities as to move all the Greeks to amazement and tears.

And in his eagerness to call forth the pity of his readers and to make them feel for what he describes, he brings in women clinging together, hair flung loose, breasts bared, and besides these the tears and laments of men and women being led off into captivity together with their children and aged parents. And he does this throughout his whole history,

always trying in each instance to set the horrors before our eyes.

Now let the ignoble and womanish character of his approach be passed over; let us examine instead what is proper to history and at the same time useful.

A historian, then, must not try to astonish his readers by sensational writing, nor seek out the speeches that might have been delivered, nor reckon up the circumstances that might attend his subject—as the writers of tragedy do—but must record above all only what was actually done and said, even if it happens to be quite ordinary.

For the aim of history and of tragedy is not the same, but the opposite. In the latter the object is to astound and beguile the hearers for the moment by the most plausible words; in the former it is, through true deeds and words, to instruct and convince for all time those who love learning.

In the one, what governs is plausibility, even if it be false, because the object is the deception of the spectators; in the other it is the truth, because the object is the benefit of those who love learning.

Apart from this, he relates most of his catastrophes without setting out the cause and manner of what happened—without which it is impossible reasonably to feel pity or properly to feel anger at anything that occurs.

For who among men does not think it a dreadful thing that free men should be struck? Yet, nonetheless, if a man suffers this because he began with unjust violence, he is judged to have suffered justly; whereas if the same thing is done for correction and instruction, those who strike free men are thought worthy even of honor and gratitude.

And again, the killing of fellow citizens he treats as the gravest impiety, deserving the heaviest penalties—and yet plainly the man who kills a thief or an adulterer goes unpunished, while the man who kills a traitor or a tyrant receives honor and precedence among all.

So in every case the final judgment about such things rests not in the deeds themselves, but in the causes and purposes of those who act, and in the differences between them.

## Book 2 Chapter 57: The Treatment of Mantinea

The people of Mantinea, then, had at the outset abandoned their membership in the Achaean confederacy and of their own free will handed themselves and their homeland over to the Aetolians, and after that to Cleomenes.

Having committed themselves to this course and become participants in the Lacedaemonian polity, in the fourth year before the arrival of Antigonus they were taken by storm by the Achaeans, when Aratus carried off a stroke against their city.

On that occasion they were so far from suffering any harsh treatment for the offense just described that what happened actually became famous, because of the suddenness with which both sides reversed their attitude toward one another.

For the moment he secured the city, Aratus at once ordered the men under his command that no one was to touch anything belonging to another.

Next, gathering the Mantineans together, he urged them to take heart and to remain in possession of their own property; for their safety would be guaranteed if they continued as citizens within the Achaean confederacy.

To the Mantineans this prospect appeared so unsuspected and unexpected that they all instantly swung over to the opposite frame of mind.

The very men against whom they had been fighting a little while before—men through whom they had watched many of their kinsmen perish and not a few of their own people fall to grievous wounds—these they now brought into their own houses and made sharers of their hearths along with their families, leaving out nothing of mutual goodwill.

And they had good reason to act so. For I do not know whether any men ever encountered more reasonable enemies, or whether any ever came through what seemed the greatest of calamities with less harm than the Mantineans did, thanks to the humanity of Aratus and the Achaeans toward them.

## Book 2 Chapter 58: The Mantineans Turn Over their City to the Lacedaemonians

But afterward, foreseeing the factional strife within their own community and the schemes of the Aetolians and Lacedaemonians, they sent envoys to the Achaeans and requested that a protective garrison be provided for them.

The Achaeans agreed and selected by lot three hundred men from their own number. Those on whom the lot fell set out, leaving behind their own cities and livelihoods, and took up residence in Mantinea, guarding the freedom and at the same time the security of its people.

Along with these they also sent out two hundred mercenaries, who together with the Achaeans helped maintain the established order there.

Not long afterward, however, the Mantineans fell into civil strife, drew in the Lacedaemonians, handed over the city to them, and slaughtered the men stationed among them by the Achaeans—a graver and more dreadful breach of faith than one could easily describe.

For even granted that they had decided to renounce altogether their gratitude and friendship toward the confederacy, they surely ought at least to have spared the men just mentioned and allowed every one of them to depart under a truce.

This much, after all, it is customary to grant even to enemies, by the common laws of mankind.

But they, in order to give Cleomenes and the Lacedaemonians a sufficient pledge of their commitment to the present design, transgressed the common rights of mankind and deliberately carried out the most monstrous act of impiety.

For to become the murderers and avengers of men who had earlier taken them by storm yet released them unharmed, and who at that very moment were guarding their freedom and security—how great an indignation does this deserve?

What would these men have to suffer before they would be thought to have paid a fitting penalty? Perhaps one might say: to be sold into slavery along with their wives and children, once they had been overcome in war.

But this is a fate that, by the laws of war, awaits even those who have committed no act of impiety at all. So they deserved to meet with some more thorough and heavier punishment;

and therefore, even if they had suffered what Phylarchus says, it would not have been natural for pity toward them to follow from the Greeks. Rather, praise and approval would have been due to those who carried out the punishment of their impiety.

Yet, although nothing worse befell the Mantineans in their downfall than the plundering of their goods and the selling of the free into slavery, this writer, for the sake of sensationalism, not only introduced an outright falsehood,

but a falsehood that is implausible as well; and through the excess of his ignorance he could not even take account of what lay right beside it—namely, how these same men, who at the very same period took Tegea by storm, did nothing of the kind there.

And yet, if the cruelty of the perpetrators had been the cause, it would have been natural for the people of Tegea too to suffer the same fate as those who had fallen under their power at the same time.

But if the difference in treatment concerned the Mantineans alone, it is obvious that the cause of the anger directed at them must also have been something distinctive.

## Book 2 Chapter 59: Execution of Aristomachus

Again, Phylarchus says that Aristomachus of Argos—a man from a most distinguished family, who had himself been tyrant of the Argives and was descended from tyrants—after falling into the hands of Antigonus and the Achaeans, was led off to Cenchreae and tortured to death, having suffered the most unjust and dreadful treatment of any man alive.

In this episode too the writer keeps to his characteristic manner and invents certain cries supposedly heard through the night as the man was being racked, reaching the people living nearby; of whom some, he says, were appalled at the impiety, others incredulous, others outraged at what was happening, and all came running to the house.

As for this sort of sensationalism, let it pass; enough has been said about it.

For my own part, even if Aristomachus had committed no other crime against the Achaeans, I judge that, by reason of the way he chose to live and his lawless treatment of his own country, he deserved the heaviest punishment.

And although the writer, wishing to magnify the man's reputation and to dispose his audience to share more deeply in his indignation at what Aristomachus suffered, says that he was not only a tyrant himself but also descended from tyrants—

no one could easily bring a graver or more biting accusation than this. For the very word carries with it the most impious connotation and embraces every injustice and lawlessness found among men.

And if Aristomachus did endure the most terrible tortures, as this man claims, even so he did not pay an adequate penalty for a single day—

the day on which, after Aratus had broken into the city with the Achaeans and undergone great struggles and dangers for the freedom of the Argives, and had in the end been driven out because none of those inside who had arranged to support him stirred, out of fear of the tyrant—

Aristomachus, using this as a pretext and excuse, on the ground that certain men had been complicit in the Achaeans' entry, tortured and slaughtered the eighty foremost citizens, who had done no wrong, in front of their own relatives.

I pass over the crimes committed throughout the whole of his life and

## Book 2 Chapter 60: Crimes of Aristomachus

those of his ancestors, for that would be a long story. And so one should not regard it as a terrible thing if he met with some such fate himself; far more terrible would it have been if he had died scot-free without experiencing any of this.

Nor should any lawlessness be charged against Antigonus or Aratus because they took a tyrant captive in war, tortured him, and put him to death—a man for killing and punishing whom, even in time of peace, praise and honor would have followed from all who reason rightly.

And when, on top of the crimes already mentioned, he also betrayed the Achaeans, what did he deserve to suffer?

He had laid down his tyranny only a short time before, hemmed in by circumstances on account of the death of Demetrius, and had unexpectedly obtained his safety, protected by the mildness and generosity of the Achaeans;

for they not only made him exempt from punishment for the crimes committed during his tyranny, but even admitted him into their commonwealth and clothed him with the highest honor, appointing him their leader and general.

Yet he forgot the favors just described in an instant: as soon as his hopes for the future were a little raised by Cleomenes, he tore both his native city and his own allegiance away from the Achaeans at the most critical moment and attached them to the enemy.

Such a man, once he had been taken captive, ought not to have died being tortured at night in Cenchreae, as Phylarchus says, but rather to have been led around the Peloponnese and made an example of under punishment, and so to have departed from life.

And yet, for all that he was such a man, he met with no harsh treatment except being drowned in the sea by those stationed at Cenchreae.

## Book 2 Chapter 61: The Loyalty of the Megalopolitans

Besides this, he recounted the misfortunes of the Mantineans with exaggeration and dramatic effect, evidently supposing that it is the duty of historians to call attention to lawless deeds;

yet of the nobility of the Megalopolitans, which they displayed at the very same period, he made not even the slightest mention—

as though it were more proper to history to enumerate the offenses of the wicked than to mark out the fine and just things that men do, or as though readers of such records were less improved by serious and admirable deeds than by lawless and despicable ones.

How Cleomenes took the city, and how, having kept it intact, he at once sent letter-carriers to the Megalopolitans in Messene, inviting them to recover their homeland undamaged and to share in his own cause—this he did relate to us, wishing to display the magnanimity and moderation of Cleomenes toward his enemies.

And further, how the Megalopolitans, while the letter was being read aloud, would not let it be read to the end and nearly stoned the letter-carriers—this much he made clear. But the natural sequel, the very thing proper to history, he left out:

the praise and the honorable record of their admirable resolve.

And yet it lay ready at hand. For if we count as good men those who endure war on behalf of friends and allies merely by their words and votes, while to those who go so far as to take upon themselves the devastation of their land and a siege we award not only praise but also gratitude and the greatest rewards,

what estimate must we form of the Megalopolitans? Surely the most honorable and the highest?

First they surrendered their land to Cleomenes; then again they suffered utter ruin for their homeland because of their loyalty to the Achaeans;

and finally, when an unhoped-for and unexpected opportunity was given them to recover it undamaged, they chose instead to lose their land, the tombs of their ancestors, their temples, their homeland, their possessions—everything, in a word, that men hold most necessary—rather than betray their faith to their allies.

What nobler deed has ever been done, or could be done? To what could a historian better draw the attention of his audience? By what deed could he more strongly urge men toward keeping faith and toward genuine and lasting partnership? Yet of none of this did Phylarchus make any mention,

being blind, as it seems to me, to the finest things and those most fitting for a historian to treat.

## Book 2 Chapter 62: The Wealth of Megalopolis

Furthermore, in what follows he says that from the spoils of Megalopolis six thousand talents fell to the Lacedaemonians, of which two thousand were given to Cleomenes according to custom.

Here, in the first place, who would not be amazed at his inexperience and his ignorance of the common understanding of the resources and power of Greek affairs—a knowledge that historians above all ought to possess?

For I am not even speaking of those earlier times in which the resources of the Peloponnesians had been utterly ruined, both by the kings in Macedonia and, still more, by the unbroken succession of their wars against one another;

but in our own day, in which everyone, speaking with one and the same voice, is thought to be enjoying the greatest prosperity, nevertheless from the whole Peloponnese, from its movable property alone—leaving slaves out of account—it would be impossible to gather so great a sum of money.

That I make this claim now not at random but on solid reasoning is clear from the following.

For who has not read concerning the Athenians that at the time when, together with the Thebans, they entered upon the war against the Lacedaemonians and sent out ten thousand soldiers and manned a hundred triremes,

they then decided to base their war contributions on a property assessment and had the entire territory of Attica valued, along with the houses, and likewise the rest of their property—yet the whole valuation of their wealth fell short of six thousand talents by two hundred and fifty?

From this, what I have just said about the Peloponnesians will appear not unreasonable.

And at that period, from Megalopolis itself, not even the most extravagant estimate would dare to claim that more than three hundred talents were obtained,

seeing that it is agreed that most of the bodies, both free and slave, managed to escape to Messene. And the strongest proof of what has been said is this:

the Mantineans, who were second to none of the Arcadians in either power or wealth, as this very writer says, and who were captured through siege and surrender, so that no one could escape and nothing could easily be smuggled away,

nevertheless yielded as their entire spoil, captives included, only three hundred talents at this same period.

## Book 2 Chapter 63: Cleomenes Invades Argolis

But who would not marvel still more at what comes next? For in his account he states that about ten days before the battle the ambassador from Ptolemy came to Cleomenes announcing that Ptolemy refused to continue supplying funds and urged him to make terms with Antigonus.

And he says that, on hearing this, Cleomenes decided he must stake everything on a throw as quickly as possible, before his forces learned what had happened, since he had no hope in his own resources of being able to pay his troops.

But if he had at that very time become master of six thousand talents, he could have outdone Ptolemy himself in supplies;

and as for war against Antigonus, if he had been master of only three hundred talents, he would have been more than able to hold out safely and wear the war down.

To declare at once that all of Cleomenes' hopes rested on Ptolemy because of the supplies, and at the same time to assert that he had become master of so vast a sum at the very same period—how is this not a sign of the greatest absurdity and, moreover, of want of reflection?

Many other things of this kind have been set down by the writer, both for the present period and throughout his whole work; but concerning these I think that what has now been said is sufficient for my original purpose.

## Book 2 Chapter 64: Cleomenes Invades Argos

After the capture of Megalopolis, Antigonus wintered in the city of the Argives. As soon as the spring season set in, Cleomenes assembled his army, gave them the encouragement the moment called for, marched the troops out, and invaded the territory of the Argives.

To most observers this seemed a reckless and audacious move, given the strength of the fortified positions guarding the approaches; but to those who reasoned correctly it was both safe and shrewd.

For he could see that Antigonus had disbanded his forces, and so he knew clearly two things: first, that he would make his incursion without danger; and second, that as the countryside was ravaged right up to the walls, the Argives, watching what was happening, were bound to grow indignant and blame Antigonus.

If, then, it turned out that Antigonus could not endure the abuse of the crowds and came out to risk a battle with the forces he had on hand, it was plain from a reasonable calculation that Cleomenes would win easily.

But if Antigonus held to his strategy and stayed quiet, Cleomenes reckoned he could withdraw home safely, having struck fear into his opponents and instilled confidence in his own troops. And that is exactly what happened.

As the land was being plundered, the crowds gathered and railed at Antigonus. But he, acting very much like a true commander and king, set nothing above acting according to sound reason, and kept his calm.

And Cleomenes, in keeping with his original plan, having ravaged the country, struck fear into his opponents, and made his own forces confident in the face of the coming danger, returned home in safety.

## Book 2 Chapter 65: Battle of Sellasia

When summer set in, and the Macedonian and Achaean troops had reassembled from their winter quarters, Antigonus took up his army and advanced with his allies into Laconia.

He had ten thousand Macedonians for the phalanx, three thousand peltasts, three hundred cavalry, a thousand Agrianians along with these, an equal number of Gauls, and in all three thousand mercenary infantry and three hundred mercenary cavalry.

There were also picked Achaean troops—three thousand infantry and three hundred cavalry—and a thousand Megalopolitans equipped in the Macedonian fashion, commanded by Cercidas of Megalopolis; and among the allies, two thousand Boeotian infantry,

two hundred Boeotian cavalry, a thousand Epirote infantry and fifty cavalry, the same numbers from Acarnania, and sixteen hundred Illyrians, under the command of Demetrius of Pharos.

The entire force thus came to about twenty-eight thousand infantry and twelve hundred cavalry.

Cleomenes, anticipating the attack, had secured the other passes into the country with garrisons, trenches, and felled trees.

He himself, with his army, was encamped at the place called Sellasia, his whole force amounting to about twenty thousand men, having judged from reasonable calculation that the enemy would make their incursion by that route—which is just what came to pass.

Two hills lie right at the entrance of the pass, one called Evas and the other Olympus,

with the road between them, running alongside the river Oenus, leading to Sparta. Cleomenes threw a trench and palisade across the front of both hills. On Evas he posted the perioeci and the allies, placing his brother Eucleidas in command of them, while he himself held Olympus with the Lacedaemonians and the mercenaries.

On the level ground beside the river, on either side of the road, he stationed the cavalry together with a portion of the mercenaries.

When Antigonus arrived and surveyed both the strength of the ground and the way Cleomenes had so skillfully occupied the advantageous points with every appropriate part of his army—so that the whole layout of the encampment resembled the defensive front of good armored fighters—

for nothing had been left undone for either attack or defense; rather, it was at once an effective battle line and a position hard to approach—

he therefore gave up any attempt to test it and engage at the first onset.

## Book 2 Chapter 66: The Two Armies Line Up

He gave up the idea of a rash attack, and instead pitched camp a short distance away, covering his front with the river called Gorgylus. There he remained for some days, studying both the peculiarities of the ground and the differences between the two armies,

while at the same time making some show of intended maneuvers to draw out the enemy's plans.

But since he could never catch any point off guard or unarmed—because Cleomenes was always ready to counter every move—

he abandoned that approach, and at last both sides agreed to settle the matter by battle: for Fortune had brought together in these two men commanders highly gifted and well matched.

Against the troops on Evas, then, the king drew up the bronze-shielded Macedonians and the Illyrians, arranged in alternating companies, placing Alexander, son of Acmetus, and Demetrius of Pharos in command.

Behind these he set the Acarnanians and the Epirotes, and behind them stood two thousand Achaeans, taking up position as a reserve.

His cavalry, around the river Oenus, he stationed opposite the enemy's horse, giving them Alexander as commander and flanking them with a thousand Achaean infantry and an equal number of Megalopolitans.

He himself, with the mercenaries and the Macedonians, resolved to make his attack along Olympus against the troops with Cleomenes.

So he placed the mercenaries in front and stationed behind them a double phalanx of Macedonians, one line close behind the other; he did this because of the narrowness of the ground.

The signal arranged for the Illyrians to begin their assault on the hill was the moment they should see a linen flag raised from the direction of Olympus—for these men had been positioned during the night along the river Gorgylus, right at the foot of the hill—

and likewise for the Megalopolitans and the cavalry, when a scarlet flag was raised by the king.

## Book 2 Chapter 67: Battle Begins

When the moment for action came, the signal was given to the Illyrians, and those responsible passed the word to do what was required; at once they all revealed themselves and began the assault on the hill.

But the light-armed troops who had been posted from the start with Cleomenes's cavalry, seeing that the Achaean companies were left exposed in their rear, fell upon them from behind and brought into the gravest danger the men who were forcing their way up toward the hill—

since Eucleidas's men were bearing down on them from the higher ground in front, while the mercenaries pressed on their rear and laid into them vigorously. At this moment Philopoemen of Megalopolis grasped what was happening,

and, foreseeing what would come of it, first tried to point out the looming result to the officers in command.

When no one paid him any heed—because he had never yet held a command and was still quite young—he rallied his own fellow citizens and charged the enemy boldly.

When this happened, the mercenaries who were pressing the rear of the men climbing the hill quickly heard the shouting, saw the cavalry locked in combat, abandoned the troops in front of them, and ran back to their original posts to help their own cavalry.

Once this happened, the body of Illyrians, Macedonians, and the others advancing with them, now free of harassment, surged eagerly and confidently against their opponents.

From this it later became clear that Philopoemen was responsible for the success against Eucleidas.

## Book 2 Chapter 68: Skill and Gallantry of Philopoemen

This is why, they say, Antigonus afterward tested Alexander, the commander of the cavalry, by asking him why he had begun the engagement before the signal was given.

When Alexander denied it, saying that some young man from Megalopolis had taken the initiative against his own wishes, Antigonus replied that the young man had acted like a good commander by seizing the moment, while Alexander, though a commander, had behaved like any ordinary youngster.

In any case, when Eucleidas's men saw the companies advancing, they failed to make use of the advantages of the ground.

What they should have done was meet the enemy and fall upon them well in advance, throwing their massed ranks into confusion and breaking them up, and then fall back step by step, steadily withdrawing in safety to the higher ground above.

For in that way, having spoiled and disrupted the special character of the enemy's equipment and formation beforehand, they would easily have routed them, thanks to the natural advantage of the terrain.

But they did none of this; instead, as though victory were already theirs for the taking, they did the very opposite.

For they stayed in their original position on the heights, intent on catching the enemy as high up as possible so that their flight would be all the longer over steep and broken ground.

What happened, naturally, was the reverse: by leaving themselves no room for retreat, and by letting the companies come up fresh and still in close order, they fell into such difficulty that they had to fight it out on the very crest of the hill against men forcing their way up.

Then, as soon as they were pushed back even a step by the weight of the enemy's armor and formation, the Illyrians immediately gained their footing, while Eucleidas's men were driven back, having left themselves no ground for retreat or maneuver.

As a result they were quickly turned and forced into a disastrous flight, since the ground over which they had to withdraw was for a long stretch steep and hard to cross.

## Book 2 Chapter 69: Defeat of Cleomenes

At the same time the cavalry engagement was reaching its climax, with all the Achaean cavalry—and Philopoemen above all—fighting outstandingly, since the whole struggle was for their own freedom.

In the course of this, Philopoemen's horse was struck a fatal blow and fell, and as he fought on foot he received a severe wound through both thighs.

Meanwhile the two kings, over on Olympus, at first carried on the fighting with their light-armed troops and mercenaries, of which each side had about five thousand.

These engaged sometimes in detachments, sometimes in their full bodies, and the fighting on both sides proved exceptional, as both the kings and their armies watched the battle in full view of one another.

They competed with each other man against man and unit against unit in displays of courage.

But when Cleomenes saw that his brother's troops had fled and that the cavalry on the level ground were on the verge of giving way, he grew alarmed that he might be attacked from every side and was forced to tear down his front fortifications and lead out his whole force in line along one side of the camp.

The light-armed troops of both sides were recalled from the ground between by the trumpet, and the phalanxes, raising their war cry together and leveling their pikes, charged one another.

A fierce struggle followed: at one moment the Macedonians fell back step by step, hard pressed by the courage of the Laconians, at another the Lacedaemonians were thrust back by the sheer weight of the Macedonian formation.

At last Antigonus's men locked their pikes together and, employing the special feature of the double phalanx, drove in with force and pushed the Lacedaemonians out of their entrenchments.

The rest of the army fled headlong, cut down as they went; but Cleomenes, with a few cavalry about him, withdrew safely to Sparta with them.

When night came on he went down to Gythium, where the means for a sea voyage had long since been prepared against just such an outcome, and sailed off with his friends to Alexandria.

## Book 2 Chapter 70: End of the Introductory Period

Antigonus, having taken Sparta at the first assault, treated the Lacedaemonians in everything else with magnanimity and kindness; and after restoring their ancestral constitution, within a few days he marched out of the city with his forces, on receiving word that the Illyrians had invaded Macedonia and were ravaging the country.

So it is that Fortune always tends to decide the greatest matters contrary to expectation.

For on this occasion too, if Cleomenes had only put off the engagement by a very few days, or had withdrawn from the battle into the city and held out against the situation even for a short while, he would have kept his throne.

As it was, Antigonus reached Tegea, restored their ancestral constitution there as well, and on the second day went on from there to Argos, arriving just in time for the festival of the Nemean games.

There, having received every honor that pertains to undying fame and glory, both from the Achaean League as a body and from each of the cities individually, he hurried off in haste to Macedonia.

He caught the Illyrians still in the country, met them in a pitched battle, and won the day; but having thrown himself so passionately into encouraging and shouting to his men during the engagement, he brought on a hemorrhage and some such condition, and not long afterward died of illness,

having held out fine hopes of himself to all the Greeks, not only for his service in the field but still more for his whole character and nobility.

He left the kingdom of Macedonia to Philip, son of Demetrius.

## Book 2 Chapter 71: Conclusion of Book 2

Why have I dwelt at such length on the war just described?

Because these times join directly onto the events I am about to relate, and it seemed useful—indeed necessary, in keeping with my original plan—to make plainly clear to everyone the state of affairs that then existed concerning the Macedonians and the Greeks.

About the same period, Ptolemy too died of illness, and Ptolemy surnamed Philopator succeeded to the kingdom.

There died as well Seleucus, son of the Seleucus who bore the surnames Callinicus and Pogon; and Antiochus, his brother, succeeded to the kingdom in Syria.

For something very similar happened to these men as had happened to the first to hold these realms after the death of Alexander—I mean Seleucus, Ptolemy, and Lysimachus.

Those men all died around the 124th Olympiad, as I noted above, and these around the 139th.

Now that I have worked through the introduction and preliminary foundation of the whole history—through which it has been shown when, how, and for what reasons the Romans, after gaining mastery over Italy, first began to lay hands on affairs beyond it and first dared to dispute the sea with the Carthaginians,

and at the same time have set out the condition then existing among the Greeks and Macedonians, and likewise among the Carthaginians—

it would now be fitting, having arrived at these times in accordance with my original plan—the times in which the Greeks were about to enter upon the Social War, the Romans upon the Hannibalic War, and the kings in Asia upon the war for Coele-Syria—

to mark off this book in line both with the outline of the events already recounted and with the deaths of the rulers who had managed affairs up to that point.
