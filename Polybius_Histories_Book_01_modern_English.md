# Polybius — The Histories, Book I

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 1 Chapter 1: Introduction

If it had happened that the writers before us who recorded events had left out praise of history itself, perhaps it would have been necessary to urge everyone toward choosing and accepting works of this kind, since no correction for human beings is readier than knowledge of past actions.

But since not just some writers, or to some degree, but virtually all of them have used this theme from beginning to end, saying that learning from history is the truest education and training for political action, and that the clearest—and indeed the only—teacher of how to bear the changes of fortune nobly is the memory of other people's reversals,

it is obvious that no one should think it his duty to repeat what has already been well said by many, and least of all I.

For the extraordinary nature of the events I have chosen to write about is enough in itself to challenge and stir every reader, young and old alike, to engage with this work.

For who is so base or idle that he would not want to know how, and under what kind of constitution, nearly the whole inhabited world was conquered and brought under the single rule of Rome in less than fifty-three years—a thing for which no earlier example can be found?

And again, who is so absorbed in other spectacles or studies that he would think anything more important than gaining this knowledge?

## Book 1 Chapter 2: Importance and Magnitude of the Subject

As for how astonishing and great the subject of my work is, nothing will make it clearer than comparing the most famous powers of earlier times—those on which historians have spent most of their words—with the superiority of Rome.

The powers worthy of such comparison are these. The Persians acquired a great empire and dominion for certain periods; but whenever they dared to go beyond the limits of Asia, they endangered not only their empire but their very survival.

The Lacedaemonians disputed the leadership of Greece for many years, but once they gained it, they held it undisputed for barely twelve years.

The Macedonians ruled in Europe from the regions by the Adriatic as far as the Danube, and that would appear to be only a very small part of the continent just mentioned.

Afterward they added rule over Asia by overthrowing the Persian empire. Yet though they seemed to have become masters of more lands and affairs than anyone before them, they still left the greater part of the inhabited world outside their power.

For they never once even attempted to contest Sicily, Sardinia, or Libya, and, to speak plainly, they did not so much as know the most warlike peoples of western Europe.

The Romans, by contrast, made subject to themselves not some parts, but almost the whole inhabited world, and they left behind a supremacy that past ages cannot match and future ages will not surpass.

From the narrative itself it will be possible to see this more clearly, and likewise to understand how great and how numerous are the benefits that practical history naturally offers to those eager to learn.

## Book 1 Chapter 3: The Starting-point of the History

My work will begin, in point of time, with the 140th Olympiad; and in point of events, with the war among the Greeks called the Social War, first undertaken by Philip, son of Demetrius and father of Perseus, together with the Achaeans against the Aetolians; in Asia, with the war for Coele-Syria, which Antiochus and Ptolemy Philopator fought against one another;

and in the regions of Italy and Libya, with the war between Romans and Carthaginians, the one most people call the Hannibalic War. These events follow directly on the final point reached in the history of Aratus of Sicyon.

In the times before this, the affairs of the inhabited world were, so to speak, scattered, because each event differed from the others in its plans, in its results, and in its location.

But from this point onward history becomes, as it were, organic: the affairs of Italy and Libya are interwoven with those of Asia and Greece, and all events are referred to one single end.

That is why I have made this period the starting point of my work.

For after defeating the Carthaginians in the war already mentioned, the Romans judged that they had accomplished the chief and greatest part of the task leading to control of the whole world, and so for the first time they dared to stretch out their hands toward what remained and to cross with an army into Greece and the regions of Asia.

Now if the constitutions that contended for world supremacy had been familiar and well known to us, perhaps I would have had no need to explain before my narrative from what purpose or what power they set out when they undertook enterprises of such scale and kind.

But since most Greeks have no ready knowledge of the earlier strength and achievements of either the Roman or the Carthaginian state, I concluded that it was necessary to compose this book and the next before the history proper,

so that no one, once he has come to the narrative itself, may be at a loss and ask with what plans, what forces, and what resources the Romans embarked on those ventures through which they came to control all the land and sea in our part of the world,

but that from these books and the preliminary account in them it may be clear to readers that they set out on their design with entirely reasonable advantages, and that in carrying it through they reached universal rule and dominion.

## Book 1 Chapter 4: Comprehensive View

The special feature of my work, and the marvelous thing about the age we are dealing with, is this: just as Fortune turned almost all the affairs of the inhabited world in one direction and forced them all to incline toward one and the same goal, so too history must bring before its readers, in a single view, the way Fortune handled events to bring the whole to completion.

Indeed this above all is what drew me on and stirred me to undertake this history, along with the fact that no one in our time has attempted a connected account of general events; otherwise I would have been far less eager for this distinction.

As it is, I see many writers treating particular wars and some of the events accompanying them, but as for the general and collective ordering of events—when and from what starting point it began, and how it reached its end—no one, so far as I know, has even tried to investigate it.

I therefore judged it absolutely necessary not to pass over, or allow to pass unnoticed, this finest and most beneficial display of Fortune's workings.

For although Fortune produces many novelties and is constantly contending in the lives of men, she has never before quite simply accomplished such a feat, or staged such a contest, as in our own time.

And this is not something one can grasp from writers of particular histories, any more than someone could think he understood the shape of the whole world, its arrangement and order, by visiting its most famous cities one by one—or, by Zeus, even by looking at them separately in pictures.

In my judgment, those who think they can gain an adequate view of the whole through partial history are much like people who, after seeing the scattered limbs of some once living and beautiful body, imagine that they have become sufficient eyewitnesses of the creature's power and beauty.

But if someone were at once to put the living thing together again and restore it in full, complete in form and in the grace of its life, and then show it to those same people, I think they would quickly admit that earlier they had fallen far short of the truth and had been little better than dreamers.

For from a part one can gain some notion of the whole, but not exact knowledge or sure understanding.

Therefore particular history must be judged to contribute only a very small part toward experience and confidence regarding the whole.

Only through the interweaving and comparison of all the parts together, through their likenesses and differences, can someone attain a full view and, in so doing, draw from history both its usefulness and its pleasure.

## Book 1 Chapter 5: The Romans in Sicily

I shall take as the starting point of this book the first crossing of the Romans from Italy. This follows directly upon the point where Timaeus left off, and it falls in the 129th Olympiad.

So I must explain how and when they secured their position in Italy, and with what opportunities they afterward set out to cross into Sicily; for that was the first land outside Italy on which they set foot.

I must also state the immediate cause of that crossing in simple terms, so that, in searching for the cause of a cause, the beginning of the whole subject and the understanding of it do not become unstable.

We must take as our starting point one that is agreed upon and recognized by everyone in point of time, and in point of events one that can be considered on its own terms, even if this requires us to go back a little in time and make a summary reminder of the events in between.

For if the starting point is unknown, or even disputed, none of what follows can command acceptance or belief; but once an agreed view about the beginning has been established, then the whole continuous narrative finds acceptance with its hearers.

## Book 1 Chapter 6: Roman Dominion in Italy

At that time it was the nineteenth year after the sea battle at Aegospotami and the sixteenth before the battle at Leuctra,

the year in which the Lacedaemonians ratified the peace called the Peace of Antalcidas with the Persian king; in which Dionysius the Elder, after defeating the Greeks of Italy in the battle by the river Elleporus, was besieging Rhegium; and in which the Gauls, after taking Rome itself by storm, held the city except for the Capitol.

The Romans made a treaty and settlement with these Gauls on terms acceptable to them, and, unexpectedly recovering possession of their fatherland, they then took what was in effect the starting point of their rise and in the years that followed made war on their neighboring peoples.

After mastering all the Latins through courage and success in battle, they next fought the Tyrrhenians, then the Celts, and afterward the Samnites, whose territory bordered Latium to the east and north.

Some time later the Tarentines, because of the outrage they had committed against Roman envoys and the fear this caused them, brought over Pyrrhus—in the year before the Gaulish invasion of the region around Delphi, when some of those Gauls were destroyed and others crossed into Asia.

By this time the Romans had made the Tyrrhenians and Samnites subject to themselves and had already defeated the Celts of Italy in many battles; then for the first time they set out against the remaining parts of Italy, fighting not so much for foreign territory as, for the most part, for lands already their own and rightfully attached to them, since their struggles with the Samnites and Celts had made them true athletes in the labors of war.

They bore this war nobly, and in the end drove both Pyrrhus and his forces out of Italy. After that they again made war on the peoples who had joined him and subdued them.

Thus, in a remarkable way, they gained control of all Italy's inhabitants except the Celts; and after this they undertook to besiege the Romans who were then holding Rhegium.

## Book 1 Chapter 7: Messene and Rhegium

A peculiar and similar misfortune befell both the cities built on either side of the strait, Messene and Rhegium.

Not many years before the time now under discussion, Campanians serving as mercenaries under Agathocles had long had greedy eyes on Messene because of the city's beauty and prosperity, and as soon as they found an opportunity they at once attempted a treacherous seizure.

They entered as friends, took possession of the city, drove some of the citizens out, and slaughtered others.

After this, they took for themselves the wives and children of the dispossessed men, each keeping whatever Fortune had assigned him at the very moment of the lawless deed; then they divided up the rest of the property and the land and held them.

Once they had swiftly and easily become masters of a fine land and city, they at once found imitators of their act.

For the people of Rhegium, when Pyrrhus was crossing into Italy, were alarmed by his invasion and also afraid of the Carthaginians because they controlled the sea, and so they drew from the Romans a garrison to guard and support them.

The troops who entered, four thousand in number, under the command of Decius the Campanian, for a while preserved both the city and their pledged faith.

But in the end, envying the Mamertines and taking them as accomplices, they too betrayed the Rhegians, being captivated both by the favorable position of the city and by the prosperity of the Rhegians in their private fortunes; and, after driving some citizens out and killing others, just as the Campanians had done, they seized the city.

The Romans were distressed by what had happened, but they could do nothing because they were occupied by the wars already mentioned.

Once they were free of these, they shut the men up and besieged Rhegium, as I said above.

When they took it, they killed most of them in the actual assault, since the men defended themselves desperately because they foresaw what was coming; but they captured alive more than three hundred.

These men were sent to Rome, and the consuls brought them into the forum, scourged them all according to Roman custom, and then beheaded them, wanting by the punishment of those offenders, so far as they could, to restore their credit for good faith among their allies.

The territory and city they immediately gave back to the Rhegians.

## Book 1 Chapter 8: The Rise of Hiero II

The Mamertines—for that was the name the Campanians gave themselves after they had taken possession of Messene—so long as they enjoyed the alliance of the Romans who held Rhegium not only remained securely in control of their own city and territory, but also caused no small trouble to the Carthaginians and Syracusans in the neighboring region, and exacted tribute from many parts of Sicily.

But when they were deprived of that support, after the men who held Rhegium had been shut up under siege, they in turn were quickly driven back into their city by the Syracusans, for reasons such as these.

Not long before this, the forces of Syracuse had quarreled with the citizens and, while encamped near Mergane, appointed commanders from among themselves: Artemidorus and Hiero, who afterward became king of Syracuse. He was still very young, but he had a natural gift for royal leadership and practical administration.

When he took over the command and, through certain relatives, made his way into the city and gained control over his political opponents, he handled affairs so mildly and nobly that the Syracusans, though they did not usually approve officers elected by the soldiers, were on this occasion unanimous in ratifying Hiero as their general.

From his very first measures it was immediately clear to those who judged rightly that he was aiming at ambitions greater than the office of general.

## Book 1 Chapter 9: Hiero Defeats the Mamertines

For he saw that whenever the Syracusans sent out their forces and their commanders with them, they themselves at home were always falling into faction and some new political disturbance.

And since he knew that Leptines stood far above the other citizens both in influence and in trustworthiness, and was especially well regarded by the common people, he formed a marriage connection with him, wanting to leave him in the city as something like a reserve support whenever he himself had to go out with the army on campaign.

After marrying the daughter of the man mentioned, and observing that the old mercenaries were disaffected and turbulent, he led out an expedition ostensibly against the foreigners who held Messene.

He encamped opposite them near Centuripa and drew up his line by the river Cyamosorus. The citizen cavalry and infantry he kept with himself at a distance, as though he intended to engage the enemy at another point; but he pushed the mercenaries forward and allowed them all to be destroyed by the foreigners.

At the moment of their rout, he himself safely withdrew with the citizens to Syracuse.

Thus he carried out his purpose with skill and eliminated the whole turbulent and seditious element in the army. After recruiting for himself a sufficient number of mercenaries, he now managed the government in security.

Seeing that the foreigners, emboldened by their previous success, were behaving with daring and recklessness, he thoroughly armed and drilled the citizen forces, led them out, and engaged the enemy on the Mylaean plain near the river called Longanus.

He inflicted a severe rout on them and captured their leaders alive, thereby putting an end to the boldness of the foreigners; and when he came to Syracuse he was hailed as king by all the allies.

## Book 1 Chapter 10: The Mamertines and Rome

The Mamertines, then, first lost the support that came from Rhegium, as I said above, and then, because of the causes just described, suffered complete failure in their own affairs. Some of them fled to the Carthaginians and put themselves and the citadel into their hands;

others sent envoys to the Romans, surrendering the city and asking for help on the ground that they were of the same stock.

The Romans were at a loss for a long time because the irrationality of giving such aid was obvious.

For after having only a little earlier inflicted the severest punishment on their own citizens because they had broken faith with the Rhegians, to seek at once to help the Mamertines, who had done much the same thing not only to the Messenians but also to the Rhegians, seemed an error impossible to defend.

Yet although they were fully aware of this, they also saw that the Carthaginians had made subject to themselves not only many parts of Libya, but also many parts of Iberia,

and that they furthermore held all the islands in the Sardinian and Tyrrhenian seas. They were anxious that, if the Carthaginians also gained control of Sicily,

they would become exceedingly burdensome and formidable neighbors, surrounding them on every side and threatening every part of Italy.

And it was clear that the Carthaginians would quickly bring Sicily under their power if the Mamertines did not get help.

For once Messene had been handed over to them and they had taken possession of it, they were likely in a short time to take Syracuse as well, since they already controlled almost all the rest of Sicily. Foreseeing this, and judging it necessary not to abandon Messene or allow the Carthaginians, as it were, to bridge the crossing into Italy for themselves, the Romans deliberated for a long time.

## Book 1 Chapter 11: Rome Supports the Mamertines

The senate did not finally ratify the decision because of the reasons just given; for the absurdity of helping the Mamertines seemed evenly balanced against the advantages to be gained from that help.

The people, however, worn down by the previous wars and needing every kind of recovery, and hearing the generals point out not only the public advantage of the war but also the obvious and considerable private benefits that each man would gain, decided to send aid.

Once the decree had been ratified by the people, they appointed one of the consuls, Appius Claudius, as commander and sent him out with orders to assist and to cross over to Messene.

The Mamertines, partly by intimidation and partly by trickery, drove out the Carthaginian commander who had already taken possession of the citadel; and they invited Appius in and placed the city in his hands.

The Carthaginians crucified their commander, judging that he had surrendered the citadel both foolishly and cowardly.

They themselves encamped with their naval force near Pelorias, and with their land army near the place called Synes, and pressed the siege of Messene vigorously.

At this time Hiero, thinking the situation well suited for driving the foreigners who held Messene completely out of Sicily, made an agreement with the Carthaginians.

After this he marched out from Syracuse against the city just mentioned, and encamping on the other side near the place called Chalcidian Mount, cut off the men in the city from that exit as well.

The Roman commander Appius crossed the strait by night in a bold passage and came to Messene.

But when he saw the enemy pressing hard from every side and considered that the siege would be both shameful and dangerous for him, since the enemy controlled both land and sea,

he first sent envoys to both parties, wanting to remove the Mamertines from the war altogether.

When neither side paid attention to him, he was at last compelled to hazard a battle and decided to attack the Syracusans first.

So he led his force out and drew it up for battle, and the king of the Syracusans readily came down to join the contest.

After fighting for a long time, Appius prevailed over the enemy and drove them all back as far as their stockade.

Appius then stripped the dead and returned again to Messene, while Hiero, taking a foreboding about the outcome of the larger struggle, withdrew in haste to Syracuse when night came.

## Book 1 Chapter 12: Appius Drives Off the Carthaginians

On the next day, when Appius learned of the withdrawal of the force just mentioned and so became confident, he decided not to delay but to attack the Carthaginians.

He therefore ordered the soldiers to make their preparations on time, and at first light he led them out.

When he engaged the enemy, he killed many of them and forced the rest to flee headlong into the neighboring towns.

Taking advantage of this success and raising the siege, he then marched through the country without fear, ravaging the territory both of the Syracusans and of their allies, since no one disputed his control of the open country; and at last he sat down before Syracuse itself and undertook to besiege it.

Such was the first Roman crossing from Italy with an army, and such were the causes and circumstances in which it took place.

Judging this to be the most fitting beginning for my whole design, I made it the point at which to take my stand, while going back in time so as to leave no difficulty unresolved in explaining the causes.

For I thought it necessary, if we were to follow what is to come and gain a proper general view of the main source of Rome's present supremacy, to see clearly how and when, after suffering disaster in their own fatherland, the Romans began to advance to a better position, and again how and when, after mastering Italy, they turned to enterprises beyond it.

So there is no need to be surprised if later on as well I sometimes go back in time when speaking of the most distinguished states.

For I will do this in order to take beginnings from which it will be possible to see clearly from what conditions, and when and how, each people set out and arrived at the position in which they now stand. That is exactly what I have just done in the case of the Romans.

## Book 1 Chapter 13: The First Punic War; Plan of the First Two Books

Now that I have said enough about these preliminary matters, it is time to turn to my main subject, after briefly and summarily setting out the events covered in the introductory books.

First in order come the events that befell Romans and Carthaginians in the war over Sicily.

Next comes the Libyan War, followed by the affairs in Iberia carried out by Hamilcar and after him by Hasdrubal on behalf of the Carthaginians.

In the same period occurred the Romans' first crossing into Illyria and those parts of Europe, and after the events already mentioned their struggles with the Celts in Italy.

At the same time among the Greeks the war called the Cleomenic War was in progress, and with that war I have made the conclusion of the whole introduction and of the second book.

To enumerate each separate event in detail from all these matters is neither necessary for me nor useful for my audience.

For I do not propose to write their full history; I intend only to mention them in summary as a preparation for the events I am going to narrate.

Accordingly, by touching the main points in order, I will try to join the end of the introduction continuously to the beginning and purpose of my history.

For if the narrative proceeds in this connected way, it will seem reasonable that I touch on matters already narrated by others, and readers eager to learn will, because of such an arrangement, find their approach to what follows easy and intelligible.

I shall, however, try to discuss with a little more care the first war that arose between Rome and Carthage over Sicily.

For it would not be easy to find a war longer than this one, or one involving fuller preparations, more continuous action, more numerous struggles, or greater reversals on both sides.

Moreover, at that period both states still had their institutions in uncorrupted vigor, their fortunes still moderate, and their powers fairly equal.

Therefore anyone who wants a good view of the character and strength of the two constitutions should make the comparison on the basis of this war rather than the ones that followed.

## Book 1 Chapter 14: Philinus and Fabius Pictor

I was prompted to dwell on this war not only by the reasons already given, but also because the two men thought to write about it with the greatest expertise, Philinus and Fabius, have not in my view reported the truth adequately.

I do not think these men lied deliberately, judging from their lives and their principles; rather, they seem to me to have suffered something much like lovers do.

Because of partisan choice and deep attachment, Philinus thinks the Carthaginians did everything wisely, honorably, and bravely, while the Romans did the opposite; Fabius takes exactly the reverse view.

In ordinary life perhaps one would not reject this kind of feeling altogether. A good man ought to love his friends and his country, to hate his friends' enemies with them, and to love the people they love.

But whenever someone takes up the character of history, he must forget all such considerations, and must often speak well of his enemies and crown them with the highest praise when their actions require it, and often rebuke and censure his closest associates with the sharpest reproach when their errors in conduct make that necessary.

For just as a living creature, once deprived of sight, becomes useless as a whole, so if truth is taken away from history, what is left becomes an unprofitable tale.

Therefore one must not hesitate to accuse friends or praise enemies, nor shrink from blaming and at other times commending the same people, since those engaged in public affairs cannot always succeed, nor is it likely that they always fail.

So we must stand apart from the actors themselves and in our written records fit our statements and judgments to the actions themselves.

And that what I have just said is true can be seen from the following.

## Book 1 Chapter 15: Misrepresentations of Philinus and Fabius

The truth of what I have said can be seen from the following examples. Philinus, at the very beginning of his narrative and of his second book, states that the Carthaginians and the Syracusans were jointly besieging Messene, having sat down before it for war.

He says that when the Romans arrived by sea and entered the city, they at once marched out against the Syracusans; that after taking heavy losses they fell back into Messene; and that when they next went out against the Carthaginians, they not only suffered heavy losses but also lost a considerable number of their soldiers taken alive.

Having said this, he then claims that after the engagement Hiero so far lost his head that he not only immediately burned his palisade and tents and fled by night to Syracuse, but also abandoned every one of the forts that had been positioned to hold down Messenian territory.

In the same way, he says, the Carthaginians too, right after the battle, abandoned their palisade, split up among the various towns, and no longer dared even to contest the open country; and that, in consequence, their commanders, seeing that their men had been thoroughly demoralized, resolved not to decide the issue by battle.

The Romans, he goes on, followed them up, and not only ravaged the territory of the Carthaginians and Syracusans, but even sat down before Syracuse itself and set about besieging it.

All of this, as it seems to me, is utterly absurd, and needs no refutation whatsoever.

For the very men whom he first set up as besieging Messene and winning the engagements, he goes on to present as fleeing, withdrawing from the open country, and in the end being besieged and broken in spirit.

And those whom he first put forward as defeated and besieged, he ends by showing as pursuing the enemy, immediately gaining control of the open country, and finally besieging Syracuse.

These statements cannot possibly be reconciled with one another—how could they?—but it must be that either his opening assumptions are false, or else his account of what followed.

In fact, the latter are true: the Carthaginians and Syracusans did withdraw from the open country, and the Romans did press on the heels of the war against Syracuse, and—as he himself says—against Echetla too, which lies in the middle of the territory of the Syracusans and Carthaginians.

It remains, then, that we must admit that the premises and opening assumptions are false, and that, although the Romans were in fact victorious from the outset in the engagements around Messene, they have been reported to us by this writer as defeated.

One could find Philinus to be like this throughout the whole of his work, and Fabius likewise, as will be demonstrated in connection with the events themselves.

Now that I have said what is fitting about this digression, I will return to the events and try, by always adding the next part of the account, to bring my readers in brief compass to a true understanding of the war I have been describing.

## Book 1 Chapter 16: King Hiero and Rome

When news of the successes of Appius and the legions reached Rome from Sicily, the Romans appointed Manius Otacilius and Manius Valerius as consuls and dispatched their entire force, together with both generals, to Sicily.

The Romans have, all told and apart from the allies, four Roman legions, which they enroll each year; each of these consists of four thousand infantry and three hundred cavalry.

When these forces arrived, most of the cities revolted both from the Carthaginians and from the Syracusans, and went over to the Romans.

Hiero, observing the confusion and dismay of the Sicilians, and at the same time the numbers and crushing weight of the Roman legions, reasoned from all this that the prospects of the Romans were brighter than those of the Carthaginians.

Inclining therefore in his calculations toward this side, he sent messages to the consuls, proposing terms of peace and friendship.

The Romans accepted, chiefly on account of supplies.

For since the Carthaginians commanded the sea, they were anxious that they might be cut off on every side from their necessities—warned by the fact that the troops who had crossed earlier had run into severe shortage of provisions.

And so, supposing that Hiero would be of great service to them in this respect, they gladly accepted his friendship.

They made a treaty on these terms: that the king should return his prisoners to the Romans without ransom, and pay them in addition a hundred talents of silver. From then on the Romans treated the Syracusans as friends and allies.

King Hiero, having placed himself under the protection of the Romans and continually supplying them with what their pressing needs required, reigned securely over the Syracusans for the rest of his days, courting honor and reputation among the Greeks.

For of all such men he is thought to have been the most distinguished, and to have enjoyed the fruits of his own sound judgment for the longest time, both in particular affairs and in general.

## Book 1 Chapter 17: The Siege of Agrigentum

When the treaty was brought back to Rome, and the people had accepted and ratified the settlement with Hiero, the Romans decided no longer to send out all their forces, but only two legions.

For they reckoned that the war had been made lighter for them, now that the king had come over to their side, and at the same time they supposed that in this way their forces would be more easily kept supplied with necessities.

The Carthaginians, seeing that Hiero had become their enemy and that the Romans were getting more thoroughly entangled in Sicilian affairs, concluded that they needed a heavier force, one with which they would be able to face the enemy and to hold together their position in Sicily.

So they recruited mercenaries from the country across the sea—many Ligurians and Celts, and still more Iberians than these—and sent them all to Sicily.

Seeing that the city of the Agrigentines was excellently suited for their preparations and was at the same time the most important city in their own province, they gathered into it their supplies and forces, deciding to use this city as their base of operations for the war.

As for the Romans, the generals who had made the treaty with Hiero had withdrawn, and their successors, appointed in their place—Lucius Postumius and Quintus Mamilius—arrived in Sicily with the legions.

Observing the Carthaginian plan and the preparations around Agrigentum, they resolved to take the matter in hand more boldly.

So they set aside the other operations of the war, and bringing their whole army to bear directly against Agrigentum, they pressed upon it; encamping eight stades from the city, they shut the Carthaginians up inside the walls.

Since it was the height of the grain harvest, and the siege was clearly going to be a long one, the soldiers set out to gather corn more eagerly than they should have.

The Carthaginians, seeing the enemy scattered over the countryside, came out and attacked the foragers. Having routed these easily, some of them rushed to plunder the palisade, others against the supporting pickets.

But the difference in their institutions saved the Roman cause then, as it had often done before. For among the Romans the penalty is death for the man who deserts his post and flees outright from the pickets.

And so on this occasion too, although the enemy were many times their number, they stood their ground gallantly; and though they lost many of their own men, they killed still more of the enemy.

Finally, surrounding the enemy just as they were on the very point of tearing apart the palisade, they destroyed some of them, and the rest, pressing upon them and cutting them down, they drove together in flight into the city.

## Book 1 Chapter 18: Relief Comes from Agrigentum

After this it came about that the Carthaginians grew more cautious about making attacks, and the Romans more careful in their foraging.

Since the Carthaginians would no longer come out against them except as far as skirmishing, the Roman generals divided their force into two parts: with one they remained near the temple of Asclepius in front of the city, and with the other they encamped in the quarters of the city that incline toward Heracleia.

The intervals between the two camps, on either side of the city, they fortified. They threw up the inner trench as a protection against those sallying out of the city, and the outer one they ran around as a guard against attacks from outside and against the men and supplies that customarily slip in and make their way into besieged cities.

The spaces between the trenches and the camps they secured with guard posts, fortifying the convenient positions at intervals.

As for provisions and the rest of their supplies, all the other allies gathered them and brought them to Herbesus, and the Romans themselves, hauling and carrying continually from this town—which was not far off—kept up an abundant supply of their necessities.

So for about five months they remained in the same positions, neither side able to gain any decisive advantage over the other beyond what occurred in the skirmishes themselves.

But as the Carthaginians were being squeezed by famine, owing to the multitude of men shut up in the city—for they were no fewer than fifty thousand—Hannibal, the commander appointed over the besieged forces, now hard pressed by the situation, kept sending continual messages to Carthage, making clear their predicament and calling for help.

The authorities in Carthage loaded their ships with the soldiers and elephants they had assembled and sent them off to Sicily, to Hanno, the other general.

He gathered his supplies and forces at Heracleia, and as a first step seized by a stratagem the city of the Herbesians, depriving the enemy's camps of their markets and their supply of necessities.

As a result, the Romans found themselves at once besieging and besieged in their circumstances. For they were brought by the lack of grain and the scarcity of necessities to such a pass that they more than once deliberated about raising the siege.

And in the end they would indeed have done so, had not Hiero, applying every effort and device, kept them supplied with moderate and necessary provisions.

## Book 1 Chapter 20: The Romans Build Ships

When the report of what had happened at Agrigentum reached the Roman Senate, they were overjoyed and elated in their thinking, and no longer kept to their original calculations, nor were they content with having saved the Mamertines, nor with the gains that had come from the war itself.

Instead, conceiving the hope that it was altogether possible to drive the Carthaginians out of the island—and that, were this done, their own affairs would make great progress—they applied themselves to these calculations and to the schemes bearing on this objective.

As for their land forces, they saw that matters were proceeding for them according to plan;

for after those who had besieged Agrigentum, the generals appointed in their place, Lucius Valerius and Titus Otacilius, seemed to be handling affairs in Sicily as well as circumstances allowed.

But since the Carthaginians held command of the sea without a fight, the war remained evenly balanced for the Romans;

for in the period that followed, although they now held Agrigentum, many of the inland cities went over to the Romans out of fear of their land forces, while still more of the coastal cities fell away from them, terrified of the Carthaginian fleet.

Seeing, therefore, that the war was increasingly tipping now to one side and now to the other for the reasons stated, and moreover that Italy was repeatedly ravaged by the naval force while Libya remained entirely unharmed, the Romans set their hearts on taking to the sea against the Carthaginians as well.

This is indeed among the chief reasons that prompted me to give a fuller account of the war mentioned above, so that this beginning too should not be unknown—how, when, and for what reasons the Romans first went to sea.

Seeing that the war was dragging on for them, it was then that they first set about building ships—a hundred quinqueremes and twenty triremes.

But since the shipwrights were utterly inexperienced in the construction of quinqueremes—because no one in Italy at that time used such vessels—this part of the undertaking gave them great difficulty.

From this, more than anything, one may grasp the greatness of spirit and the daring of the Roman temper.

For not only did they lack adequate resources, but they had no resources at all and had never given a thought to the sea; yet, taking it into their heads then for the first time, they set to work so boldly that, before they had even made trial of the venture, they at once undertook to fight the Carthaginians at sea—men who from their forefathers held undisputed mastery of the sea.

As evidence for the truth of what I am now saying and for the extraordinary character of their daring, one might cite this: when they first undertook to ferry their forces across to Messene, they not only had no decked warship but no long ship at all, not a single galley.

Rather, they borrowed fifty-oared vessels and triremes from the Tarentines and Locrians, and also from the Eleans and Neapolitans, and on these they boldly carried their men across.

On that occasion, when the Carthaginians put out against them in the strait, one of their decked ships, in its eagerness, pressed too far forward and ran aground, so that it fell into the hands of the Romans; using this ship as a model, they then built their entire fleet on that pattern.

So that, had this not happened, it is plain that through their inexperience they would in the end have been prevented...

As he was rounding the headland of Italy he ran into the enemy, who were proceeding on their voyage in good order and formation; he lost most of his ships, and with those that were left escaped against all hope and expectation.

## Book 1 Chapter 22: The Victory of Mylae

After this the Romans drew near to the coasts of Sicily, and when they learned of the disaster that had befallen Gnaeus, they immediately sent word to Gaius Duilius, the commander of the land forces, and waited for him to arrive.

At the same time, hearing that the enemy's fleet was not far off, they set about preparing for a naval battle.

But since their ships were poorly built and hard to maneuver, someone proposed to them, as an aid for the fight, the devices that were afterward called "ravens" (corvi). Their construction was as follows.

A round post stood on the prow, twenty-four feet high and a foot wide in diameter.

At its top this post had a pulley, and around it was fitted a gangway built of cross-planks nailed together, four feet wide and thirty-six feet long.

The opening in the planking was oblong, and the gangway fitted around the post at a point twelve feet along from its near end. Down each of its long sides ran a railing knee-high.

At the far end of the gangway was fastened an iron spike shaped like a pestle, sharpened to a point and fitted with a ring at its top, so that the whole thing looked very much like the machines used for grinding grain.

To this ring a rope was attached. When the ships clashed, the men would hoist up the "ravens" by means of the pulley on the post and drop them onto the deck of the enemy ship—sometimes over the prow, and sometimes, when blows came from the side, swinging the device around to meet them.

Once the "ravens" had bitten into the planks of the decks and lashed the ships together, then, if the vessels lay alongside one another, the men leaped aboard from every quarter; but if they were prow to prow, they made their assault across the "raven" itself, advancing two abreast.

The leading pair shielded their front by holding their shields before them, while those who followed protected the flanks by resting the rims of their shields on the railing.

Having made use of such a preparation, [the Romans watched for the right moment to fight at sea.]

## Book 1 Chapter 23: Operations in Sicily

[Having made these preparations,] they watched for the right moment to fight at sea. As for Gaius Duilius, as soon as he learned of the misfortune that had overtaken the commander of the naval forces, he handed over the land legions to the military tribunes and crossed over himself to join the fleet.

Learning that the enemy was ravaging the territory of Mylae, he sailed against them with his whole fleet.

When the Carthaginians caught sight of him, they put to sea with joy and eagerness, with a hundred and thirty ships, holding the Romans' inexperience in contempt. They all sailed straight at the enemy, prow first, not even thinking the engagement worth drawing up in any order, but as though heading for plunder lying ready for the taking.

Their commander was Hannibal—the same man who had slipped his forces out of the city of Agrigentum by night—sailing in the seven-banked galley that had once belonged to King Pyrrhus.

As they drew near and saw the "ravens" raised aloft on each of the prows, the Carthaginians were for a while at a loss, baffled by the strange construction of the engines. Yet, in utter contempt for their opponents, those sailing in the front rank charged in boldly.

But each time the ships closed and were locked together by the engines, the men crossed at once over the "raven" itself and grappled with the enemy on the decks. Some of the Carthaginians were cut down, while others surrendered, dismayed at what was happening—for the battle had become very much like a fight on land.

So they lost the first thirty ships engaged, crews and all, and with them the commander's ship was captured too; though Hannibal himself, beyond all hope and at great risk, escaped in the ship's boat.

The rest of the Carthaginian force came on as if to charge, but as they drew near and saw what had happened to the ships sailing ahead, they sheered off and dodged the descent of the engines.

Trusting in their speed, they hoped to make their attacks safely by rowing around the enemy, some on the flank and some at the stern.

But the engines swung round in every direction to meet them and dropped down so unfailingly that any ship that came near was bound to be grappled. In the end the Carthaginians gave way and fled, terrified by the novelty of what was happening, having lost fifty ships.

## Book 1 Chapter 24: Further Operations in Sicily

The Romans, having against all expectation made good their hopes at sea, were doubly emboldened in their drive to press the war.

For the moment, then, they put in at Sicily, raised the siege of Segesta, whose people were by now in the last extremity, and on their march back from Segesta took the town of Macella by storm.

After the naval battle, Hamilcar, the Carthaginian commander in charge of the land forces, who was stationed around Panormus, learned that in the Roman camp the allies were quarreling with the Romans over the place of honor in the fighting.

Discovering that these allies were encamped by themselves between Paropus and the Thermae of Himera, he fell upon them suddenly with his whole force while they were striking camp and killed nearly four thousand of them.

After this action Hannibal took the ships that had survived and sailed off to Carthage, and not long afterward crossed from there to Sardinia, having gathered more ships and some of the renowned naval captains.

But not long after, he was hemmed in by the Romans in a certain harbor in Sardinia and lost many of his ships; whereupon he was at once seized by the surviving Carthaginians and crucified.

For the Romans, as soon as they had taken to the sea, set about seizing control of Sardinia as well.

During the following year the Roman armies in Sicily accomplished nothing worth mentioning.

But the next year, after receiving their newly appointed magistrates, Aulus Atilius and Gaius Sulpicius, they set out against Panormus, because the Carthaginian forces were wintering there.

The commanders advanced close to the city with their whole army and drew up for battle. But when the enemy would not come out to meet them, they marched off again, this time against the town of Hippana.

This they took by storm at the first assault, and they also captured Myttistratum, which had withstood the siege for a long time because of the strength of its position.

The city of Camarina, which had revolted from them a little earlier, they now besieged with siege-works, threw down its walls, and occupied. In the same way they took Henna too, and several other small Carthaginian towns.

After these operations they set about besieging the people of Lipara.

## Book 1 Chapter 25: The Battle of Ecnomus

The next year Gaius Atilius, the Roman commander, lay at anchor off Tyndaris, and when he saw the Carthaginian fleet sailing past in disorder, he ordered his own crews to follow the leading ships and set out ahead of the rest himself with ten ships of matching speed.

The Carthaginians, seeing that some of the enemy were still embarking, others just putting out, and the foremost far ahead of the rest, turned and went to meet them.

Surrounding them, they destroyed the rest and very nearly captured the commander's ship, crew and all; but this ship, well manned and fast, escaped the danger against all expectation.

Meanwhile the rest of the Roman ships came sailing up and gradually gathered together. Forming a line abreast, they engaged the enemy, took ten ships with their crews, and sank eight. The remaining Carthaginian ships withdrew to the islands called the Liparae.

As a result of this battle, both sides reckoned that they had fought on equal terms, and so they set about more wholeheartedly building up naval forces and contesting control of the sea.

The land forces in this period did nothing worth recording, but frittered away the time on small, chance engagements.

So, having made the preparations I have described, the Romans put to sea for the coming summer with three hundred and thirty decked warships and put in at Messana.

Setting out from there, they sailed with Sicily on their right, and after rounding Cape Pachynus they crossed over to Ecnomus, because their land army too was in that very district.

The Carthaginians, sailing up with three hundred and fifty decked ships, put in at Lilybaeum, and from there dropped anchor at Heraclea Minoa.

## Book 1 Chapter 26: Preparations for Battle

[There they came to anchor.] Now the Romans' aim was to sail across to Libya and shift the war there, so that the danger for the Carthaginians might concern not Sicily but themselves and their own land.

The Carthaginians took exactly the opposite view: realizing that Libya was easy to invade, and that its whole population would be easily overcome once an enemy had landed, they were unwilling to allow it, but were eager instead to risk everything and fight at sea.

Since the one side was bent on preventing the crossing and the other on forcing it, it was clear that the coming struggle would arise from the rivalry of both.

So the Romans made their preparations to suit both purposes—both for action at sea and for landing in enemy territory.

Accordingly they picked the best men from the land legions and divided the whole force they intended to take aboard into four parts.

Each part had two names: the first was called the "First Legion" and the "First Squadron," and so on in turn for the rest. The fourth had taken on yet a third name besides: they were called "Triarii," following the usage of the land legions.

The total of this whole naval force came to about a hundred and forty thousand men, reckoning each ship as carrying three hundred rowers and a hundred and twenty marines.

The Carthaginians, for their part, made their preparations mostly, indeed almost entirely, with a view to the battle at sea; their numbers, reckoned by the count of their ships, were over a hundred and fifty thousand.

At these figures anyone—not only one who was present and saw it with his own eyes, but even one who merely heard of it—might well be struck with awe at the scale of the contest and at the grandeur and might of both states, judging from the multitude of their men and ships.

The Romans, reasoning that the voyage would be across open sea and that the enemy were fast sailers, tried in every way to make their formation secure and hard to attack.

So they placed first, side by side in the front, the two six-banked galleys on which the commanders Marcus Atilius and Lucius Manlius were sailing.

Behind each of these they stationed ships in single file, one ship to a line—behind the one the first squadron, behind the other the second—setting each successive ship of each squadron at a greater interval from the next. The vessels lay one behind another with their prows angled outward.

Once they had arranged the first and second squadrons simply in a wedge formation, they placed against these, in a single line abreast, the third legion; and with these set in place, the whole formation took the shape of a triangle.

Behind these they stationed the horse-transports, attaching them by tow-lines to the ships of the third squadron.

Behind the transports they placed the fourth squadron, those called the Triarii, extended in a single line so as to overlap at both ends the ships in front of them.

When all had been fitted together in the manner described, the whole formation took the shape of a wedge: the part toward the apex was hollow, the part at the base solid, and the whole was effective and serviceable, and at the same time hard to break apart.

## Book 1 Chapter 27: The Battle of Ecnomus

At the same time the Carthaginian commanders briefly addressed their men, pointing out to them that if they won the sea-battle they would carry on the war over Sicily, but if they were beaten they would be fighting for their own homeland and all that they held dear; and so they ordered them to board the ships.

Since all carried out the order eagerly—because what had been said let them see clearly what lay ahead—they put to sea with confidence and in a manner fit to strike terror.

When the commanders observed the enemy's formation, they arranged their own to match it: three of their four divisions they drew up in a single line, extending the right wing toward the open sea so as to envelop the enemy, and setting all their ships prow-on to the enemy. The fourth division they made the left wing of the whole line,

bending it back at an angle toward the shore.

The Carthaginian right wing was led by Hanno—the man defeated in the battle at Agrigentum—with ships for charging and the fastest quinqueremes for the outflanking movement.

The left was under the charge of Hamilcar, who had fought the sea-battle off Tyndaris; and he, fighting now in the center of the line, employed in the engagement the following stratagem.

When the Romans saw the Carthaginians drawn out in a thin line and launched their charge against the center, the battle began in this way.

The Carthaginian center, by prearranged order, quickly turned to flight so as to break up the Roman formation. These men retreated at full speed, while the Romans followed eagerly behind.

So the first and second squadrons pressed hard upon the fleeing ships, while the third and fourth divisions fell behind—the one because it was towing the horse-transports, and the Triarii because they stayed with them and kept guard over them.

But when the Carthaginians judged that they had drawn the first and second squadrons far enough from the rest, a signal was raised from Hamilcar's ship, and all at once they swung about and engaged their pursuers.

A fierce struggle followed. In speed, the Carthaginians had much the better of it, rowing around the enemy, closing in easily and pulling back swiftly;

but in close, forceful fighting—grappling with the "ravens" any ship that once came near, and fighting under the eyes of both their commanders, who shared in the action in full view of their men—the Romans had hopes no less bright than the Carthaginians.

Such, then, was the course of the fighting in this quarter.

## Book 1 Chapter 28: The Siege of Aspis

Such was the state of affairs [in the center]. At the same time Hanno, commanding the right wing, which had held off at a distance during the first encounter, crossed the open sea and charged the ships of the Triarii, causing them great trouble and difficulty.

Meanwhile the Carthaginians stationed near the shore re-formed from their original position into a line abreast, brought their ships prow-on, and charged the vessels towing the horse-transports. These cast off their tow-lines, grappled with the enemy, and fought it out.

So the whole engagement fell into three parts, and three separate sea-battles were going on at a wide distance from one another;

and since the forces on each side were evenly matched, in keeping with the original disposition, the fighting in each case was hard fought.

Nonetheless the outcome in each battle followed the natural course—as is to be expected when all the contending forces are evenly balanced.

For those who engaged first were the first to be decided: in the end Hamilcar's squadron was overpowered and took to flight.

So while Lucius was busy securing the captured ships, Marcus, seeing the struggle in which the Triarii and the horse-transports were caught up, hurried to their aid with the undamaged ships of the second squadron.

When he had reached and closed with Hanno's division, the Triarii quickly took heart, and although by now they were faring badly, they rallied again to the fight.

The Carthaginians, attacked in front by some and assailed in the rear by others, found themselves in difficulty and—unexpectedly surrounded by the relieving force—gave way and withdrew toward the open sea.

At this same moment Lucius, who was now sailing back and saw the third squadron pinned against the shore by the Carthaginian left wing, and Marcus, who had left the horse-transports and Triarii safe, both hurried to rescue their endangered comrades.

For by now what was happening looked very much like a siege; and these men would plainly all have perished had not the Carthaginians, in fear of the "ravens," merely fenced them in and held them penned against the shore, wary of charging because of the grappling and so keeping their distance.

But the commanders came up swiftly, surrounded the Carthaginians, and captured fifty enemy ships with their crews, while a few escaped by slipping away along the shore.

Such was the course of the fighting in its separate parts; but the outcome of the whole sea-battle went to the Romans.

Of their ships, twenty-four were destroyed, and of the Carthaginians over thirty. Not a single Roman ship fell into enemy hands with its crew, but sixty-four of the Carthaginian ships did.

## Book 1 Chapter 29: The Siege of Aspis

After this the Romans again took on fresh supplies, fitted out the captured ships, and gave the crews the care their success deserved. Then they put to sea and made their voyage toward Libya.

Their leading ships came to land below the headland called Hermaeum, which lies at the mouth of the whole gulf by Carthage and projects out into the open sea toward Sicily. There they waited for the ships sailing up behind them, and when they had gathered the whole fleet together they sailed along the coast until they reached the city called Aspis.

There they landed, hauled the ships ashore, and enclosed them with a trench and palisade. Since the people holding the city would not submit willingly, they began to besiege it.

The Carthaginians who had escaped the danger of the sea battle sailed back as well. Convinced that the enemy, elated by the advantage they had won, would immediately sail against Carthage itself, they stationed their land and naval forces at the points lying in front of the city.

But when they learned that the Romans had disembarked safely and were besieging Aspis, they gave up trying to watch for an attack by sea and instead concentrated their forces and busied themselves with guarding both the city and the countryside.

The Romans, after taking Aspis and leaving a garrison to hold the city and its district, also sent envoys to Rome to report what had happened and to ask what they should do next and how they should handle the situation. After this they broke camp with all their forces in eager haste and set out to ravage the country.

Since no one opposed them, they destroyed many country houses built with exceptional luxury, seized a great quantity of livestock, and carried off to the ships more than twenty thousand people.

Meanwhile messengers arrived from Rome explaining that one of the commanders must remain with an adequate force, while the other was to bring the fleet back to Rome.

So Marcus remained behind with forty ships, fifteen thousand infantry, and five hundred cavalry.

Lucius took the crews and the mass of prisoners aboard, sailed safely along the coast of Sicily, and arrived at Rome.

## Book 1 Chapter 30: Regulus in Africa

The Carthaginians, seeing that the enemy intended to remain in the country for a considerable time, first chose two generals from among themselves, Hasdrubal son of Hanno and Bostar, and then sent to Hamilcar at Heraclea, summoning him to come with all speed.

He took with him five hundred cavalry and five thousand infantry and came to Carthage. Having been appointed a third general, he consulted with Hasdrubal and the others about how they should deal with the present situation.

They decided to come to the aid of the countryside and not to stand by while it was plundered with impunity.

A few days later Marcus advanced, seizing by assault and looting the strongholds that were unwalled, and laying siege to those that were fortified.

When he came to a notable city called Adys, he encamped around it and energetically set about siege works and an assault.

The Carthaginians, eager to relieve the city and judging that they must contest control of the open country, led out their army.

They occupied a hill that stood above the enemy, but it was unsuitable for their own forces, and there they pitched camp.

Though they placed their greatest hopes in their cavalry and elephants, they abandoned the flat ground and shut themselves up in steep and difficult terrain. In doing so, they were about to teach the enemy exactly what ought to be done against them. And that is exactly what happened.

For the Roman commanders, recognizing with practical good sense that the most effective and most formidable part of the enemy's strength had been rendered useless by the ground, did not wait for them to come down into the plain and draw up for battle.

Instead, taking advantage of the opportunity before them, they advanced at daybreak from both sides against the hill.

The Carthaginian cavalry and elephants were therefore completely useless. The mercenaries, however, came out bravely and with great spirit, and at first forced one Roman division to give ground and flee.

But when they pushed too far forward and were surrounded by the troops ascending from the other side, they were routed, and after that the whole army was immediately driven out of the camp.

The elephants and cavalry, as soon as they reached level ground, made their withdrawal safely.

The Romans pursued the infantry only a short distance, then plundered the camp, and after that moved through the whole countryside and sacked the cities without resistance.

They also gained possession of the city called Tunes. Since it was well suited for the operations before them and was conveniently placed both with regard to Carthage itself and the neighboring countryside, they encamped there.

## Book 1 Chapter 31: Distress at Carthage

The Carthaginians, after suffering a setback at sea not long before and now another on land—not because of any cowardice among the rank and file, but because of the incompetence of their leaders—had fallen into difficulties of every kind.

At the same time the nation of the Numidians was attacking them as well, and was doing the country damage no less than the Romans, indeed even greater damage.

As a result, because the people from the countryside fled in fear and crowded into the city, there was complete discouragement and famine: discouragement because of the panic, and famine partly because of the numbers gathered there and partly because they expected a siege.

Marcus, seeing that the Carthaginians had failed both by land and by sea and thinking that he would very soon take the city, but anxious that the commander coming from Rome might arrive first and take the credit for the whole achievement, invited the Carthaginians to negotiate a settlement.

They gladly listened and sent out their leading men. But when they met with him, they were so far from inclining toward any of his proposals that they could hardly bear even to listen to the weight of his demands.

For Marcus, as though he already had complete control of everything, thought that whatever concessions he granted ought to be accepted by them as a favor and an act of generosity.

But the Carthaginians saw that even if they became his captives, nothing harsher could follow than the commands he was now imposing. So they not only came back rejecting the proposals, but were also deeply offended by Marcus's severity.

When the Carthaginian council heard what the Roman general was demanding, although it had almost given up hope of safety, it nevertheless took so manly and noble a stand that it chose to endure anything and to try every action and every opportunity rather than submit to anything dishonorable or unworthy of its past deeds.

## Book 1 Chapter 32: Xanthippus of Sparta

About this time one of the recruiting officers previously sent to Greece sailed into Carthage, bringing a large number of soldiers, among them a certain Xanthippus, a Lacedaemonian, a man trained in the Spartan discipline and possessed of solid experience in warfare.

When he heard about the defeat, how it had happened, and in what way, and when he considered the rest of the Carthaginian resources as well as the number of their cavalry and elephants, he quickly concluded and told his friends that the Carthaginians were being beaten not by the Romans but by themselves, through the inexperience of their commanders.

Because the situation was critical, what Xanthippus said was soon spread among the crowd and among the generals, and the men in authority decided to summon him and test what he could do.

When he appeared before them, he explained to the magistrates why they had now met with defeat, and showed that if they followed his advice and made use of flat ground in marching, encamping, and drawing up for battle, they would easily be able both to secure their own safety and to defeat the enemy.

The generals accepted what he said, were persuaded by him, and immediately entrusted the forces to him.

Even before this, there had already been among the masses an encouraging murmur and hopeful talk based on what Xanthippus was saying.

But once he led the army out before the city, drew it up in proper order, began to maneuver some units in formation, and gave commands according to military rules, he showed such a contrast to the incompetence of the previous generals that the multitude responded with loud cheers and were eager to engage the enemy at once, convinced that so long as Xanthippus led them, they would suffer nothing disastrous.

When the generals saw that the spirits of the masses had been restored so unexpectedly, they addressed them in a way suited to the moment, and after a few days set out with the army.

Their force consisted of twelve thousand infantry, four thousand cavalry, and nearly a hundred elephants.

## Book 1 Chapter 33: The Defeat of Regulus

The Romans, seeing that the Carthaginians were now making their marches through level country and placing their camps on flat ground, were puzzled and disturbed by this new development, but all the same they were eager to come to close quarters with the enemy.

After advancing, they spent the first day encamped about ten stades from their opponents.

On the next day the Carthaginian leaders were considering what should be done and how they ought to act in the present circumstances.

But the rank and file, full of eagerness for battle, gathered in groups and shouted Xanthippus's name one by one, convinced that he ought to lead them out at once.

The generals, seeing the army's rush of enthusiasm and eagerness, and with Xanthippus also insisting that they must not let the moment pass, ordered the troops to prepare themselves and gave Xanthippus authority to manage affairs as he thought best.

Once he had this power, he brought out the elephants and stationed them in a single line at the front before the whole army. Behind them, at a suitable distance, he placed the Carthaginian phalanx.

Of the mercenaries, he posted some on the right wing, while he placed the most mobile troops together with the cavalry in front of each wing.

When the Romans saw the enemy drawing up for battle, they advanced out readily to meet them.

Alarmed and anticipating the charge of the elephants, they put the light-armed troops in front and behind them stationed the maniples in many successive lines, while they divided the cavalry between the two wings.

By making their whole formation narrower than before but deeper, they had taken reasonable measures against the elephants, but against the cavalry—who greatly outnumbered their own—they had completely misjudged the situation.

When both sides had arranged, both generally and in detail, each part in the positions appropriate to it, they stood in order watching for the moment to attack one another.

## Book 1 Chapter 34: Roman Defeat

As soon as Xanthippus gave the order to the men on the elephants to advance and break up the enemy's line, and to the cavalry on each wing to wheel around and attack the enemy,

then the Roman army too, according to their custom, clashed their weapons together, raised their battle cry, and charged the enemy.

The Roman cavalry on both wings quickly fled, because the Carthaginian cavalry were so much more numerous.

Of the infantry, those posted on the left wing, partly avoiding the elephants' charge and partly despising the mercenaries, attacked the Carthaginian right. After routing them, they pressed on and pursued them as far as the camp rampart.

But among those stationed opposite the elephants, the front ranks, once engaged, were forced back and trampled underfoot by the sheer power of the animals, and perished in heaps where they stood. Still, because of the depth of the formation behind them, the main body remained unbroken for some time.

But when the men in the rear ranks found themselves surrounded on every side by cavalry and were forced to turn and fight them, while those who had forced their way through the elephants to the front, with the beasts now behind them, ran into the Carthaginian phalanx still intact and in good order, they were cut down.

Then, hard pressed from every side, most of the Romans were trampled to death by the overwhelming force of the animals, while the rest were shot down by the mass of cavalry on the very ground of the battle line. Only a very few broke away and tried to flee.

But since the routes of retreat lay over open plain, some of these too were killed by the elephants and cavalry, and perhaps five hundred who fled with the general Marcus were soon overtaken and captured alive together with him.

On the Carthaginian side about eight hundred mercenaries fell, namely those posted opposite the Roman left.

Of the Romans, about two thousand survived—those who, by pursuing the troops mentioned above, had stayed outside the main danger. All the rest perished except the general Marcus and those who fled with him.

The Roman units that escaped made their way with difficulty back to Aspis.

The Carthaginians stripped the dead, took the general along with the prisoners, and returned to the city overjoyed at the turn events had taken.

## Book 1 Chapter 35: Lessons from the Event

At this point a thoughtful observer could rightly note many things that contribute to the correction of human life.

For the need not to trust in Fortune—especially in times of success—was then made perfectly clear to all by what happened to Marcus.

The man who a little earlier had shown no pity or indulgence to the fallen was himself at once led away to beg those very things for his own safety.

And indeed the old saying of Euripides, that one wise plan overcomes many hands, was then confirmed by the facts themselves.

For one man and one mind brought down forces that seemed unconquerable and effective in action, and led back to a better state a government that appeared plainly ruined beyond recovery, along with the utterly disheartened spirits of its armies.

I have mentioned these things for the sake of improving those who read these memoirs.

For there are two ways by which all people may change for the better: through their own misfortunes and through those of others. The lesson drawn from one's own experience is more vivid, but the lesson drawn from other people's is safer.

Therefore one should never deliberately choose the first, since it brings correction only through great suffering and danger, but one should always seek the second, since through it one may perceive the better course without harm.

From this one ought to conclude that the experience gained from practical history is the finest education for real life.

For history alone, without causing us injury, produces true judges of what is better in every situation and circumstance. Let this be enough on these matters.

## Book 1 Chapter 36: Xanthippus Leaves Carthage

The Carthaginians, now that everything had turned out according to their wishes, gave free rein to their overflowing joy, both in thanksgiving offered to the god and in warm congratulations exchanged among themselves.

But Xanthippus, after bringing about so great an advance and so decisive a turn in the affairs of Carthage, sailed away home again not long afterward, having made his decision wisely and shrewdly.

For brilliant and extraordinary achievements breed heavy jealousy and sharp slander; and while native citizens, supported by their kinsmen and their many friends, may be able to fend these off for a long time, foreigners are quickly overcome by one or the other of them and put in danger.

There is also another account given of Xanthippus's departure, which I will try to explain when I come to a more fitting occasion than the present.

The Romans, when news reached them—against all their expectations—of what had happened in Libya, at once set about refitting their fleet and rescuing the men who had survived there.

The Carthaginians, meanwhile, after these events encamped and laid siege to Aspis, eager to get into their hands the men who had escaped from the battle.

But being wholly unable to take the place, owing to the bravery and daring of these men, they finally abandoned the siege.

When word reached them that the Romans were fitting out their fleet and intended once more to make the voyage against Libya, they set to work, repairing some vessels and building others from the keel up.

Quickly manning two hundred ships, they put to sea and kept watch for the enemy's approach.

The Romans, at the beginning of summer, launched three hundred and fifty vessels and, placing the consuls Marcus Aemilius and Servius Fulvius in command, sent them out. Putting to sea, they sailed along Sicily as if heading for Libya.

Falling in with the Carthaginian fleet off Hermaeum, they routed it easily at the first charge and captured a hundred and fourteen ships together with their crews.

## Book 1 Chapter 37: The Roman Fleet Wrecked

they sailed back once more toward Sicily. They crossed the strait safely, but on reaching the territory of Camarina they were caught in so great a storm and so great a disaster that one could not even describe it adequately, so far did the catastrophe surpass all bounds.

For out of their three hundred and sixty-four ships, only eighty survived; of the rest, some went down beneath the waves, while others, dashed by the surf against the reefs and headlands, were broken to pieces, filling the shore with corpses and wreckage.

No greater catastrophe at sea, occurring all at once, is even recorded in history. The blame for it must be laid not so much on fortune as on the commanders.

For although the pilots had repeatedly protested against sailing along the outer coast of Sicily, the side facing the Libyan sea, because it was rough and offered no safe anchorage, and because, too, one threatening constellation had not yet set while another was on the point of rising—for they were making the voyage between the rising of Orion

and that of the Dog Star—the commanders heeded none of these warnings and sailed out into the open sea, eager to overawe some of the cities along the coast by the impression of their recent success and so to win them over.

But by chasing small hopes they met with great misfortunes, and only then recognized their own folly.

In general, the Romans rely on force in everything; they think that once they have set themselves a goal they must carry it through at all costs, and that nothing they have once resolved upon can be impossible. Because of this drive they succeed in many things, but in some they fail conspicuously—above all at sea.

On land, where their undertakings are directed against men and the works of men, they usually succeed, because they apply their force against powers comparable to their own, and only rarely fail.

But when they pit themselves against the sea and the surrounding elements and try to fight them by sheer force, they meet with great disasters.

This is what befell them then, and has befallen them many times since, and will continue to befall them, until they correct this rashness and this reliance on force, by which they suppose that every season must be open to them for sailing as well as for marching.

## Book 1 Chapter 38: The Romans Build More Ships

The Carthaginians, learning of the destruction that had overtaken the Roman fleet, and reckoning that they were a match for the enemy on land because of their earlier success, and at sea because of the disaster the Romans had just suffered, turned with greater eagerness to their naval and land preparations.

They immediately dispatched Hasdrubal to Sicily, giving him both the troops already on hand and those that had come from Heracleia, and along with them a hundred and forty elephants.

After sending him off, they fitted out two hundred ships and made all the other preparations for the voyage.

Hasdrubal, having crossed safely to Lilybaeum, set about training his elephants and drilling his forces, and clearly meant to contest control of the open country.

The Romans, when they heard in detail from those who had been brought back from the shipwreck what had happened, took the disaster hard; but unwilling to give in once and for all, they resolved once again to build two hundred and twenty vessels from the keel up.

These were completed within three months—a thing hard even to believe—and the newly appointed magistrates, Aulus Atilius and Gnaeus Cornelius, at once fitted out the fleet and put to sea.

Sailing through the strait, they took on at Messene the ships that had been saved from the shipwreck, and putting in at Panormus in Sicily with three hundred ships—Panormus being the most important city of the Carthaginian province—they set about besieging it.

Throwing up siege-works at two points and making the other preparations, they brought up their engines.

When the tower by the sea easily fell and the soldiers forced their way in there, what is called the New Town was taken by storm; and the so-called Old Town, endangered by this, was quickly surrendered by its inhabitants.

Having made themselves masters of the place, they sailed back to Rome,

leaving a garrison in the city. After this, when summer came on, the newly appointed magistrates Gnaeus Servilius and Gaius Sempronius put to sea with the whole fleet, crossed to Sicily, and set out from there for Libya.

Coasting along the country, they made very many landings, but accomplished nothing worth mentioning in any of them, and came to the island of the Lotus-eaters, which is called Mēninx and lies not far from the Lesser Syrtis.

There, through inexperience of the waters, they ran upon some shallows, and when the tide ebbed and their ships settled aground, they fell into utter helplessness.

Nonetheless, after a while the sea unexpectedly flowed back, and by throwing all their heavy cargo overboard they barely managed to lighten the ships.

When this happened, their departure was more like a flight. Reaching Sicily and rounding Lilybaeum, they came to anchor at Panormus.

From there, recklessly making the voyage to Rome across the open sea, they again ran into a storm so great in magnitude that they lost more than a hundred and fifty ships.

The Romans, when this had happened—though they are beyond all others determined in every undertaking—now, because of the scale and number of their misfortunes, were forced by circumstances to give up gathering yet another fleet.

Resting their remaining hopes on their land forces, they sent out the generals Lucius Caecilius and Gaius Furius, along with their legions, to Sicily, and manned only sixty ships to carry supplies to the armies.

As a result of the disasters I have described, the fortunes of the Carthaginians once more rose higher.

For they commanded the sea without fear, now that the Romans had withdrawn from it, while in their land forces they had great hopes.

And they felt this not without reason. For when the report of the battle in Libya had spread—that the elephants had broken up the Roman ranks and destroyed most of their men—

the Romans were so terrified of the elephants that for the two years following the events just described, although they often drew up in battle order against the enemy in the territory of Lilybaeum and often in that of Selinus, within five or six stades of them, they never dared to begin the fight, nor to descend at all onto level ground, for fear of an elephant charge.

During these times they captured by siege only Therma and Lipara, keeping all the while to mountainous and difficult country.

And so the Romans, observing the panic and despondency in their land forces, changed their minds and resolved once more to take to the sea.

Appointing as generals Gaius Atilius and Lucius Manlius, they built fifty vessels and energetically enrolled crews and assembled a fleet.

## Book 1 Chapter 40: Roman Victory at Panormus

Hasdrubal, the Carthaginian commander, seeing that the Romans had lost their nerve in the recent engagements, and learning that one of the generals had gone off to Italy with half the army, while Caecilius was lingering at Panormus with the remaining part of his force, intending to guard the allies' crops just as the harvest was at its height,

took his army from Lilybaeum, set out, and encamped on the borders of the territory of Panormus. But Caecilius,

seeing how overconfident he had become and eager to provoke his rashness, kept his soldiers within the gates.

Emboldened by this, Hasdrubal—convinced that Caecilius did not dare to come out against him—pushed boldly forward with his whole army and marched down through the narrows into the territory of Panormus.

While he was destroying the crops right up to the city, Caecilius held to his intended plan, until he had lured him into crossing the river that lay before the city.

When the Carthaginians had brought their elephants and their force across, Caecilius then began sending out his light-armed troops to harass them, until he had compelled them to deploy their entire army.

Seeing that what he had planned was coming to pass, he stationed some of his nimble troops in front of the wall and the ditch, ordering them, if the elephants came close, to use their missiles freely,

and whenever they were hard pressed, to take refuge in the ditch and then, sallying out from it again, to hurl javelins at whichever of the beasts came near.

He ordered the workmen from the marketplace to carry the missiles and lay them down outside, along the foundation of the wall.

He himself, with his standards, took up his post at the gate lying opposite the enemy's left wing, continually sending more and more men to support the skirmishers.

As the engagement there grew more general, the officers in charge of the elephants, vying with one another for Hasdrubal's favor and wishing the success to be their own doing, all charged the men fighting in the front, easily routed them, and chased them to the ditch.

But when the beasts attacked, wounded by the archers on the wall and pelted with a vigorous, thick shower of javelins and darts by the fresh troops drawn up in front of the ditch,

riddled with missiles and badly wounded, they soon fell into confusion, turned upon their own side, and went charging through them, trampling and destroying the men and throwing their own ranks into disorder and tearing them apart.

Seeing this, Caecilius vigorously led out his force; and falling upon the enemy from the side, against their flank while they were in disorder, with troops that were fresh and in good order, he inflicted a heavy rout upon them, killing many and forcing the rest to flee headlong.

He captured ten elephants together with their Indian drivers, and the rest, whose Indians had thrown themselves off, he rounded up after the battle and got them all into his possession.

By accomplishing this, he was by common agreement held to have been the cause, for the Romans, of their land forces recovering their courage and regaining control of the open country.

## Book 1 Chapter 41: Siege of Lilybaeum

When news of this success reached Rome, the people were overjoyed—not so much because the enemy had been weakened by the loss of their elephants, as because their own men had regained their confidence by mastering the elephants.

For this reason they took heart again, and, in keeping with their original intention, resolved to send out their generals on campaign with a fleet and naval force, eager to bring the war to an end as far as they could.

When the supplies for the expedition had been prepared, the generals sailed for Sicily with two hundred ships. It was the fourteenth year of the war.

Coming to anchor off Lilybaeum, where the land forces had also come to meet them, they set about besieging it, reckoning that if they took this city they could easily transfer the war to Libya.

On this point, more or less, the Carthaginian leaders were of the same mind and reasoned just as the Romans did.

And so, treating everything else as secondary, they set themselves to relieve the city, to take risks, and to endure anything on its behalf, since no other base would be left to them—the Romans now controlling all the rest of Sicily except Drepana.

But so that what I say may not be unclear to those who do not know the region, I will try to bring my readers, in a few words, to an understanding of the favorable situation and position of these places.

## Book 1 Chapter 42: The Topography of Lilybaeum

Sicily as a whole is so situated in relation to Italy and its southern extremity much as the Peloponnese is situated in relation to the rest of Greece and its outermost points,

differing in just this one respect: that the Peloponnese is a peninsula, while Sicily is an island; for in the one case the intervening land can be crossed on foot, in the other only by sea.

The shape of Sicily is a triangle, the vertices of whose angles are formed by promontories.

Of these, the one inclining to the south and stretching out into the Sicilian Sea is called Pachynus;

the one leaning toward the north bounds the western part of the strait and lies about twelve stades from Italy, and is called Pelorias;

the third points toward Libya itself, conveniently situated opposite the promontories that lie out in front of Carthage, at a distance of about a thousand stades; it inclines toward the winter sunset, divides the Libyan from the Sardinian Sea, and is called Lilybaeum.

On this promontory lies a city of the same name as the place, around which the Romans were then forming their siege—exceptionally well fortified with walls, and surrounded by a deep ditch and by lagoons on the seaward side, through which the approach into the harbors requires great experience and familiarity.

The Romans, encamping against the city on both sides, and fencing off the space between their camps with a ditch, palisade, and wall, began to bring up their siege-works against the tower nearest the sea, on the side facing the Libyan main.

Continually building onto what they had already constructed and extending their works, they at last brought down six towers adjoining the one mentioned, and set about battering all the rest at once with rams.

As the siege was pressed with vigor and terrible force, with the towers laboring and crumbling day by day, while the works pushed ever farther into the city,

there was deep dismay and consternation among the besieged—even though, apart from the civilian population, there were in the city as many as ten thousand mercenaries.

Yet their general Himilco left undone nothing that was possible; building counter-walls here and driving counter-mines there, he caused the enemy no small difficulty.

Moreover, every day he went out and attacked the siege-works, trying somehow to set fire to them, and on this account he fought many desperate engagements, both by day and by night, so that sometimes more men fell in such clashes than usually fall in a pitched battle.

## Book 1 Chapter 43: Treason in Lilybaeum

Around this same time, some of the officers who held the highest commands among the mercenaries conferred privately with one another about handing the city over to the Romans. They were convinced that the men under their command would do as they were told, so they slipped out of the city by night to the enemy camp and began negotiating the matter with the Roman general.

But Alexon the Achaean—the same man who in earlier times had saved the people of Agrigentum when the Syracusan mercenaries there were planning to break faith and betray the city—was once again the first to grasp what was afoot, and he reported it to the Carthaginian commander.

The moment the commander heard of it, he summoned the officers who had remained behind. He appealed to them earnestly, promising large gifts and rewards if they would keep faith with him and not join the plot of those who had left the city.

They eagerly accepted his proposal, and he immediately sent them out together with his own men. To deal with the Celts he sent Hannibal—the son of the Hannibal who had died in Sardinia—because of the familiarity he had built up with them during an earlier campaign; and to the rest of the mercenaries he sent Alexon, because of the regard and trust in which they held him.

These men called the troops together, urged them to stay loyal, and on top of that guaranteed the rewards the commander had promised to each of them. In this way they easily persuaded the men to remain at their posts.

As a result, when the officers who had slipped away afterward came openly up to the walls, wanting to make their appeal and say something about the Romans' offer, the men not only refused to pay them any attention but would not even consent to listen. Instead they pelted them with stones and javelins and drove them away from the wall.

So because of these events the Carthaginians came within a hair's breadth of losing everything, betrayed as they were by their own mercenaries.

But Alexon, who earlier through his loyalty had preserved for the people of Agrigentum not only their city and territory but also their laws and freedom, was on this occasion the man responsible for keeping the Carthaginians from total ruin.

## Book 1 Chapter 44: A Fruitless Sortie

The Carthaginians at home knew nothing of all this, but they could work out for themselves what a city under siege would need. So they manned fifty ships with soldiers and gave suitable orders to the officer placed in charge of the operation, Hannibal—son of Hamilcar, a trierarch and the leading friend of Adherbal. They dispatched him in all haste, instructing him not to delay but to seize the right moment and use boldness to relieve the besieged.

He put to sea with ten thousand soldiers and anchored at the islands called the Aegusae, which lie midway between Lilybaeum and Carthage, where he watched for his chance to sail. When he caught a favorable and fresh wind,

he spread all his sails and ran straight before the wind right at the very mouth of the harbor, making the passage with his men fully armed and ready for battle on the decks.

The Romans, partly taken aback by his sudden appearance and partly afraid that the force of the wind might carry them along with the enemy into their opponents' harbor, gave up any attempt to block the relief force as it sailed in. Instead they took up a position out at sea, dumbstruck at the enemy's audacity.

The whole population of the city had gathered on the walls, at once in agony over what would happen and overjoyed at this unexpected ray of hope, and they cheered on the incoming ships with clapping and shouting.

Hannibal, recklessly and with great confidence, ran in and brought his ships safely to anchor in the harbor, disembarking his soldiers without loss.

Yet all those in the city were not so much delighted at the arrival of the relief force—great as the boost was to their hopes and their strength—as they were at the fact that the Romans had not dared to stop the Carthaginians from sailing in.

## Book 1 Chapter 45: Running the Blockade

Himilco, the general in command of the city, saw the eagerness and high spirits of both groups—the men already in the city, because of the arrival of the relief force, and the newcomers, because they had no experience yet of the hardships besetting the place.

Wanting to make use of the enthusiasm of both groups while it was still fresh, for an attack on the siege-works with fire, he called everyone together into an assembly.

He spoke at some length on what the occasion called for and roused them to overwhelming enthusiasm, both by the scale of the rewards promised to those who distinguished themselves individually and by the favors and gifts they would all receive collectively from the Carthaginians.

When they responded as one, shouting that he should not wait but lead them out, he praised them, welcomed their eagerness, and dismissed them for the time being, telling them to rest while there was time and to obey their officers.

Not long afterward he called together their commanders, assigned each of them the positions suited to the attack, made known the watchword and the moment for the assault, and ordered the officers to be at their assigned posts with all their men at the morning watch.

They obeyed, and at first light he led out his force and attacked the works at several points at once.

But because they had foreseen what was coming, the Romans were neither idle nor unprepared; they readily brought help wherever it was needed and fought the enemy hard.

In a short time everyone was at grips with one another, and there was a fierce struggle all around the wall: those from the city numbered no fewer than twenty thousand, and those outside were more numerous still.

And the more the men fought out of formation, mingled together and each following his own impulse, the more brilliant the contest became—for out of so great a multitude, man against man and rank against rank, the rivalry among the fighters became almost a series of single combats.

Even so, the shouting and the press of bodies were at their height right at the works themselves.

For the men posted from the start by both sides for this very purpose—the one group to dislodge those at the works, the other to keep from giving them up—threw themselves into it with such rivalry and zeal, the one straining to drive the enemy off, the other refusing absolutely to yield, that in their determination they ended up dying right where they had first been stationed.

Meanwhile those mixed in among them, carrying torches, tow, and fire, fell upon the engines so boldly and from every side at once that the Romans were brought to the brink of disaster, unable to overcome the enemy's assault.

But the Carthaginian general, seeing many of his men dying in the fight while still unable to take the works—the very object of the attack—ordered his trumpeters to sound the recall.

So the Romans, after coming within an inch of losing all their siege equipment, in the end kept possession of the works and preserved them all safely.

## Book 1 Chapter 46: Hannibal the Rhodian

After this exploit Hannibal sailed out by night with his ships, again escaping the enemy's notice, and made for Drepana to join Adherbal, the Carthaginian commander-in-chief.

Because of the favorable location and the excellence of the harbor at Drepana, the Carthaginians always took great care to keep it well guarded.

This place happens to lie about a hundred and twenty stades from Lilybaeum.

The authorities at Carthage wanted to know how matters stood at Lilybaeum, but they could not find out, since one side was shut up inside and the other was keeping a vigilant watch. Then a man of distinction, called Hannibal the Rhodian, offered to sail into Lilybaeum, see the situation with his own eyes, and report everything in detail.

They were glad to hear his offer, but they did not believe it could be done, since the Roman fleet lay at anchor right at the mouth of the entrance channel.

He fitted out his own ship and put to sea. He crossed to one of the islands lying off Lilybaeum, and the following day, catching a favorable wind at the right moment, around the fourth hour he sailed in—in full view of all the enemy, who were stunned at his audacity.

The next day he immediately set about putting out again.

The Roman general, wanting to keep a more careful watch over the entrance channel, had during the night fitted out his ten fastest ships. He himself took up a position by the harbor and watched what would happen, and so did the whole army.

The ships, on both sides of the channel mouth, drew in as close to the shallows as they possibly could and lay there with oars poised, ready to ram and seize the ship as it tried to sail out.

But the Rhodian made his departure openly and so far outdid the enemy in both daring and speed that he not only sailed out with his ship and crew unscathed—running past the enemy vessels as though they were standing still—

but after sailing a little way ahead he even brought his ship to a halt with oars poised, as if challenging the enemy.

And when none of them dared to put out against him, because of the speed of his rowing, he sailed away, having with a single ship defied the entire enemy fleet.

From then on he did the same thing many times over and proved of great service: he kept the Carthaginians constantly informed of urgent matters, kept up the spirits of the besieged, and dismayed the Romans

## Book 1 Chapter 47: The Rhodian is Captured

by his recklessness. What contributed most to his boldness was that, from experience, he had carefully marked out the entrance by means of the shoals.

For after crossing the open sea and coming into view, he would take his bearings from the Italian side and bring the tower by the sea onto his bow in such a way that it covered all the towers of the city facing toward Libya—and this is the only way it is possible, with a following wind, to aim accurately at the mouth of the entrance channel.

Trusting in the Rhodian's daring, a good many of those who knew the waters likewise grew bold enough to do the same. As a result, the Romans, frustrated by what was happening, set about trying to dam up the mouth of the harbor.

For the most part their attempt accomplished nothing, because of the depth of the sea and because none of the material they dumped in would settle or hold together at all; instead, the swell and the force of the current swept whatever was thrown in aside and scattered it as soon as it began to sink.

But at one spot where there were shoals a mole did, with great effort, take shape. On it a quadrireme that was running out by night ran aground and fell into the enemy's hands—a ship of exceptionally fine construction.

The Romans seized it, fitted it out with a picked crew, and used it to watch for all who tried to sail in, and especially for the Rhodian.

As it happened, he had sailed in by night, and afterward was again putting out openly. But seeing the quadrireme set out alongside him from the start, he recognized the ship and was thrown into confusion.

At first he made a dash to outrun her, but as he was being overtaken by the skill of her crew, in the end he was forced to come about and engage the enemy.

Getting the worst of it in the fight between marines—both because of their numbers and because the men were specially chosen—he fell into the enemy's hands.

The Romans, having captured this finely built ship as well and fitted it out with everything needed for the service, in this way put a stop to those who were boldly running in to Lilybaeum.

## Book 1 Chapter 48: The Roman Siege-Works Burnt

While the besieged were actively building counterworks, they had given up any hope of damaging or destroying the enemy's equipment.

But then a windstorm arose with such violence and force, directed against the very approach-works of the engines, that it shook the galleries loose and lifted by sheer force the towers set out in front of them.

At that moment some of the Greek mercenaries, realizing how suited the circumstances were for destroying the works, put the idea to the general.

He took up the suggestion and quickly got ready everything needed for the job. The young men gathered together and set fire to the works at three points.

Since the structures, owing to their age, were in just the right state to catch fire easily, and since the force of the wind was blowing straight against the towers and engines themselves, the spread of the fire turned out to be vigorous and effective, while the Romans' efforts to relieve and rescue them proved in the end useless and impossible.

So great was the consternation the scene caused among those trying to help that they could neither think clearly nor see what was happening. Blinded by the soot and sparks blown into their faces, and by the sheer volume of smoke, quite a few perished and fell, unable even to get close enough to fight the fire.

And the greater the difficulty the enemy faced for the reasons just given, the greater the advantage to those setting the fires.

For everything that could blind or harm them was blown clean away and driven against the enemy; while whatever they shot or hurled at the rescuers and at the works to destroy them landed accurately—because the men loosing it could see the ground in front of them—and struck hard, since the force of the wind worked together with those throwing.

In the end the destruction was so complete that even the bases of the towers and the timbers of the rams were rendered useless by the fire.

After this, the Romans gave up trying to press the siege by means of works. Instead they dug a ditch and threw a palisade around the whole city, built a wall to protect their own camp as well, and left the outcome to time.

Meanwhile the men in Lilybaeum rebuilt the section of wall that had fallen and now endured the siege with good courage.

## Book 1 Chapter 49: The Roman Fleet Sails for Drepana

When news of these events reached Rome, followed by further reports that most of the fleet's crews had been lost, both at the works and in the conduct of the siege generally,

they hurriedly enrolled sailors, and once they had gathered about ten thousand, sent them off to Sicily.

These men crossed the strait and reached the camp on foot. The Roman general, Publius Claudius, then assembled his military tribunes and said that now was the time to sail against Drepana with the whole fleet.

For Adherbal, the Carthaginian commander stationed there, was unprepared for what was coming: he did not know that the new crews had arrived, and he was convinced that the Roman fleet could not put to sea because of the losses of men suffered during the siege. When they readily agreed,

he at once put on board both the existing crews and those that had recently arrived, and for marines he picked the best volunteers from the whole army—seeing that the voyage was a short one and the prospect of gain plainly at hand.

With these preparations made, he put to sea around midnight, unnoticed by the enemy. At first he sailed in close order, keeping the land on his right.

As soon as it was light and his leading ships came into view off Drepana, Adherbal caught sight of them and was at first thrown off by the unexpected sight.

But quickly collecting himself and grasping that the enemy was attacking, he resolved to attempt anything and endure everything rather than stand by and let his men be shut up in an obvious blockade.

So he at once gathered his crews on the beach and, by proclamation, mustered the mercenaries from the city.

When they had assembled, he set about bringing them, in a few words, to grasp both the hope of victory that lay before them if they dared to fight at sea, and the hardships of a blockade if they hung back out of foreseen danger.

When they readily roused themselves for the sea-battle and shouted at him to lead on and not delay, he praised them, welcomed their eagerness, and ordered them to embark with all speed, to keep their eyes on his ship, and to follow it astern.

Having made these instructions clear, he hurriedly led the way out himself, taking his fleet out close under the very rocks, toward the far side of the harbor away from where the enemy was entering.

## Book 1 Chapter 50: The Roman Fleet Meets Resistance

Publius, the Roman commander, saw that the enemy, contrary to his expectation, were neither giving way nor alarmed at his approach, but were set on fighting a sea-battle.

Some of his own ships were already inside the harbor, others were right at its mouth, and still others were bearing down toward the entrance; so he gave the order for all of them to turn about and sail back out again.

At this, with some of the ships in the harbor and others at the entrance colliding because of the reversal, there arose not only an immense uproar from the men, but the ships, crashing into one another, also broke their oars.

Even so, as each ship ran back out the captains kept forming them up close along the shore and quickly brought their prows to face the enemy.

Publius himself had from the first been sailing astern of the whole fleet, but now, turning aside during the maneuver toward the open sea, he took up his position on the left wing of the entire force.

At the same moment Adherbal, sailing past the left of the enemy [the Romans] with five ships, brought his own ship to a halt facing the enemy on the seaward side;

and at the same time, through his officers, he passed the word to each of the ships coming up behind to do the same as those who joined the line ahead of them.

When they had all formed into a single front, he gave the signal through the agreed tokens and at first advanced in good order, while the Romans stayed close to the land because they were waiting for the ships still running out from the harbor.

As a result, the Romans were at a great disadvantage right up against the shore itself.

## Book 1 Chapter 51: Naval Battle Off Drepana

...joining battle. When the two fleets were close to one another, the signals were raised on each of the flagships and they engaged.

At first the contest was evenly matched, since both sides were using the best of their land troops as marines on board;

but the Carthaginians steadily gained the upper hand, since throughout the whole engagement they held many advantages.

They were far superior in speed, owing to the better construction of their ships and the fitness of their crews, and the lay of the ground greatly helped them, since they had drawn up their line with the open sea behind them.

For whenever any of them were hard pressed by the enemy, they could withdraw safely to the rear, thanks to their speed, into the open water;

and then, wheeling about against the foremost of their pursuers—sometimes circling around them, sometimes striking them broadside as the Roman ships swung clumsily about because of their weight and the inexperience of their crews—they kept ramming again and again and sank many of the vessels.

And whenever one of their allies was in danger, they readily came to the rescue, out of harm's way and in safety, since they made their passage along the sterns of their own ships on the seaward side.

For the Romans the very opposite happened. Those who were hard pressed had no way to retreat to the rear, since they were fighting close to the shore, and any ship that was crowded by the one in front of it either ran onto the shallows and stuck fast in the stern, or was driven against the land and ran aground.

To sail through the gaps in the enemy's line and to appear astern of ships already engaged with others—the most effective maneuver in a sea-battle—was impossible for them, both because of the heaviness of their ships and, still more, because of the inexperience of their crews.

Nor were they able to come to the aid from astern of those who needed it, because they were penned in against the shore and there was not even a little space left for those who wished to help one in distress.

With such difficulty pervading the whole battle—some ships sticking fast on the shallows, others being driven ashore—the Roman commander, seeing what was happening, turned to flight, slipping away from the left wing along the shore, with about thirty ships that happened to be near him.

As for the remaining ships, ninety-three in number, the Carthaginians took possession of them and their crews, except for those men who ran their ships up onto the land and got away.

## Book 1 Chapter 52: Roman Transports for the Camp

The sea-battle having turned out so, Adherbal won high standing among the Carthaginians, as having succeeded through himself and through his own foresight and daring;

while Publius lost his good name among the Romans and was bitterly attacked, as having handled affairs rashly and without judgment, and as having, so far as one man could, brought Rome into no small disasters.

For this reason he was later put on trial and exposed to heavy penalties and grave danger.

The Romans, however, despite these events, in their ambition for the whole cause left undone nothing that was possible, but pressed on with the next steps.

And so, when the time for the elections came round, they appointed consuls and at once sent off one of them, Lucius Junius, to convey supplies of grain to those besieging Lilybaeum, along with the other provisions and stores for the army; and they manned sixty ships as escorts for these.

Junius, arriving at Messana and taking on the ships that met him there from the army and from the rest of Sicily, made his way in haste to Syracuse, with a hundred and twenty warships and his provisions in about eight hundred cargo vessels.

From there he handed over to the quaestors half the cargo ships and some of the warships and sent them off, eager to get the army what it needed.

He himself remained at Syracuse, waiting for those that were lagging behind on the voyage from Messana and taking on additional grain from the allies in the interior.

## Book 1 Chapter 53: Carthalo Attacks the Roman Transports

About this same time Adherbal sent off to Carthage the men captured in the sea-battle and the captured ships,

and gave his colleague Carthalo thirty ships, in addition to the seventy with which he himself had sailed in, and sent him off

with orders to fall suddenly upon the enemy's ships lying at anchor off Lilybaeum, to seize all he could, and to set fire to the rest.

Carthalo obeyed, made his attack just before dawn, and began burning some of the ships and towing others away, so that great confusion arose throughout the Roman camp.

For as the Romans rushed to the rescue of their ships and a shout went up, Himilco, the commander of the garrison at Lilybaeum, grasped what was happening and, seeing it as day was now breaking, sent out the mercenaries from the city against them.

The Romans, with danger pressing in on them from every side, fell into no slight or ordinary confusion.

The Carthaginian admiral, after towing off a few of the vessels and shattering others, then drew off a little way from Lilybaeum, as if making for Heracleia, and lay in wait, intending to block those who were sailing toward the Roman camp.

When his scouts reported that a considerable number of ships of all kinds were bearing down and drawing near, he put out to sea and sailed to meet them, eager to engage because his earlier success had made him contemptuous of the Romans.

In the same way, the light boats accustomed to sail ahead of the squadron reported the enemy's approach to the quaestors who had been sent on in advance from Syracuse.

These men, judging that they were not strong enough for a sea-battle, came to anchor off a small town subject to them—one without a harbor, but with roadsteads and headlands jutting out from the land that conveniently enclosed them.

There they made their landing and, setting up the catapults and stone-throwers from the town, waited for the enemy's attack.

The Carthaginians, on drawing near, at first set about blockading them, supposing that the men, terrified, would withdraw into the little town and leave them to take possession of the ships in safety.

But when this expectation came to nothing—the men on the contrary defending themselves stoutly, and the place presenting many difficulties of every kind—they towed off a few of the provision ships and sailed away to a certain river, where they anchored and watched for the Romans to resume their voyage.

## Book 1 Chapter 54: Shipwreck of the Roman Fleet

The commander left behind at Syracuse, once he had finished what he intended, rounded Pachynus and held his course toward Lilybaeum, knowing nothing of what had happened to those who had sailed ahead.

The Carthaginian admiral, when his scouts again signaled the appearance of the enemy, put out and sailed in haste, wishing to engage them as far as possible from their own ships.

Junius, sighting the Carthaginian fleet and the number of its vessels from a long way off, dared neither to engage nor was he any longer able to escape by flight, since the enemy were close at hand; so he turned aside to a rough and altogether treacherous stretch of coast and came to anchor there,

judging it preferable to suffer whatever he must rather than let his squadron, men and all, fall into the enemy's hands.

The Carthaginian admiral, seeing what he had done, rejected the idea of risking a battle and bringing his ships up to such dangerous ground; instead he seized a certain headland and anchored there between the two enemy squadrons, keeping watch and giving his attention to both.

But when a storm came on and a more general disturbance loomed up from the sea, the Carthaginian pilots, foreseeing what was coming from their knowledge of the locality and of seamanship and predicting the outcome, persuaded Carthalo to flee the storm and round the headland of Pachynus.

He sensibly took their advice, and these men, after great toil and barely getting around the headland, anchored in safety;

but the Roman fleets, with the storm coming on and the coast being utterly without harbors, were so completely destroyed that not even the wreckage proved of any use—both squadrons alike were utterly and unaccountably ruined.

## Book 1 Chapter 55: The Romans Take Mount Eryx

When this had happened, the fortunes of the Carthaginians rose again and their hopes once more grew more favorable;

while the Romans, who had earlier suffered setbacks only up to a point, now withdrew entirely from the sea but kept control of the open country. The Carthaginians were masters of the sea, yet did not wholly give up hope of the land.

After this everyone—both those at Rome and the troops around Lilybaeum—was indignant at the situation as a whole, on account of the disasters described above;

yet they did not abandon their resolve to carry on the siege, but the one side kept up supplies by land without excuse, while the other persisted in the siege as far as they were able.

Junius, brought back to the camp after the shipwreck and deeply distressed, was eager to make some new move and to do what was needed, anxious to recover from the reverse that had occurred.

And so, when even a slight opportunity offered, he seized Eryx by a surprise stroke and made himself master both of the temple of Aphrodite and of the city.

Eryx is a mountain by the sea in Sicily, on the side that faces Italy, between Drepana and Panormus—but closer to and almost adjoining Drepana—and in size it is by far the greatest of the mountains in Sicily except for Aetna.

On its very summit, which is flat, stands the temple of Erycine Aphrodite, which is by common agreement the most distinguished of all the temples in Sicily for its wealth and its general grandeur;

and the city is set just below the summit, with a very long and steep approach on every side.

He posted a garrison on the summit, and likewise watched the approach from Drepana, guarding both points carefully—and still more the line of ascent—convinced that in this way he would securely hold both the city and the whole mountain.

## Book 1 Chapter 56: Hamilcar Barca's Seven Years in Hercte

After this the Carthaginians appointed as their commander Hamilcar, surnamed Barca, and put the affairs of the fleet into his hands.

Taking over the naval forces, he set out to ravage Italy. It was the eighteenth year of the war.

After overrunning the territory of Locris and Bruttium, he sailed off from there and put in with his whole fleet at the region of Panormus, and seized the place called Hercte, which lies between Eryx and Panormus by the sea and is reckoned to surpass other sites by far in suitability for the security and long maintenance of an encampment.

For it is a steep-sided mountain rising to a considerable height above the surrounding country. The perimeter of its upper rim is not less than a hundred stades, and the ground enclosed within it is good for pasture and tillage, well placed to catch the sea-breezes and altogether free of deadly wild beasts.

It is ringed by inaccessible cliffs both on the side toward the sea and on the side stretching toward the interior, while the spaces between these need only slight and minor fortification.

It also has within it a knoll that serves at once as a citadel and as a fine lookout point over the country below.

It commands as well a harbor well situated for the run from Drepana and Lilybaeum to Italy, in which there is an abundant supply of water.

It has in all three approaches, all difficult—two from the land and one from the sea. Encamping here at great hazard, Hamilcar,

though he had no friendly city of his own and clung to no other hope of any kind, but had thrown himself into the very midst of the enemy, nevertheless made trouble for the Romans in struggles and dangers that were neither small nor trivial.

For, first, using this as his base, he ravaged by sea the coast of Italy as far as the territory of Cumae;

and second, on land, when the Romans had encamped opposite him before the city of Panormus at a distance of about five stades, he engaged in many and varied combats on land for nearly three years. About these it is not possible, in writing...

## Book 1 Chapter 57: The Two Sides Evenly Matched

Take the case of two outstanding boxers, men matched in both their natural gifts and their physical conditioning. When they have entered the decisive moment of the contest for the crown and are fighting it out, landing blow upon blow without pause, it is impossible—for the combatants and the spectators alike—to keep a precise account of, or to anticipate, each individual feint and stroke.

Yet from the overall vigor of the men, and from the competitive drive of each, one can form an adequate impression of their skill, their strength, and their courage as well. The same holds for the two generals I am now describing.

The reasons for, and the methods by which, they daily set ambushes and counter-ambushes, attacks and assaults against one another could never be set down in full by a writer who tried to enumerate them; and for an audience the recital would be endless and, into the bargain, the reading of it pointless and unprofitable.

But from a general statement about them and about the outcome of their rivalry, one will arrive at a clearer understanding of the events described.

For nothing was left untried—neither the stratagems that could be learned from history, nor the devices prompted by the moment and the immediate situation, nor the undertakings that called for hazardous and forceful daring.

A decisive engagement, however, could not be brought about, and for several reasons. The forces on both sides were evenly matched; the entrenched camps were alike unapproachable because of their strength; and the distance between the two armies was extremely short.

This last was the chief reason why minor clashes occurred ceaselessly day after day, while nothing decisive was ever accomplished.

For it always turned out that the only men killed in these encounters were those who fell into hand-to-hand fighting; while those who once gave way were at once entirely out of danger, safe behind their own defenses, and could then turn about and renew the fight.

## Book 1 Chapter 58: Hamilcar Besieges the Romans at Eryx

Nevertheless, Fortune, like a good umpire of the games, shifted them by a daring move out of the position just described and the struggle they had been engaged in, and shut them up in a more dangerous contest within a narrower space.

For while the Romans were holding Eryx both at its summit and at its base, as I have said, Hamilcar seized the town of the Erycinians, which lay between the summit and the men encamped at the foot.

The result was that the Romans holding the summit endured a siege and faced danger with reckless courage, while the Carthaginians held out with almost incredible tenacity—with the enemy pressing them on every side, and their supplies brought in only with difficulty, since they clung to the sea at a single point by a single approach.

Here once again both sides used against each other every device and force of siege-craft; they bore every kind of privation and made trial of every assault and battle.

In the end—not, as Fabius claims, because they were exhausted and worn down, but like men untouched and unconquerable—they let the crown stand undecided.

For before either side could overcome the other, though they fought on in this place for two more years, the war came to its decision by another means.

Such, then, was the situation at Eryx and among the land forces. The two states were like high-bred fighting birds battling to their last breath.

Such birds, when through exhaustion they have often lost the use of their wings yet hold on by sheer spirit, keep dealing out blows until at last, by chance closing with each other, they seize one another at a vital moment, and once this happens it falls out that one of the two drops first.

So too the Romans and Carthaginians, worn out by their labors through the unbroken succession of dangers, were utterly past feeling, their strength sapped and their resolve undone—

## Book 1 Chapter 59: The Romans Once More Build a Fleet

—by the long years of taxes and expenses. The Romans were fighting on with the same desperate spirit. For nearly five years now they had entirely withdrawn from operations at sea, both because of their reverses there and because they had become convinced they would decide the war by their land forces alone.

But seeing now that the matter was not going forward as they had reckoned—chiefly because of the daring of the Carthaginian commander—they resolved for a third time to stake their hopes on naval power,

believing that by this plan, if they seized the right moment for the attempt, they could in this way alone bring the war to a successful close. And so in the end they did.

The first time, they had quit the sea, yielding to the blows of fortune; the second time, they had been worsted in the sea-battle off Drepana.

Now they made this third attempt, and by it they were victorious; cutting off the Carthaginian armies around Eryx from their supplies by sea, they brought the whole struggle to its conclusion.

The undertaking was, for the most part, an act of sheer determination. The public treasury held no funds for the purpose; nevertheless, through the patriotism and generosity of the leading men toward the commonwealth, the means were found to carry it through.

According to the resources at their command, men came forward singly, in pairs, or in threes to furnish a fully fitted quinquereme, on the understanding that they would recover the cost if affairs went well.

In this way two hundred quinqueremes were quickly made ready, built on the model of the Rhodian's ship. The Romans then appointed Gaius Lutatius as commander and sent him out at the start of summer.

He appeared unexpectedly off the coasts of Sicily and seized both the harbor at Drepana and the anchorages near Lilybaeum, since the entire Carthaginian fleet had withdrawn home.

He threw up works around the city of Drepana and made his other preparations for the siege; and while he pressed on with this, doing everything in his power,

he also kept watch for the arrival of the Carthaginian fleet, and, mindful of his original purpose—that only through a sea-battle could the whole issue be decided—he let no time be wasted or left idle.

Instead, each day he held practice drills and exercises for the crews suited to his plan, and by attending carefully to the rest of their daily regimen, he turned his sailors, in a very short time, into trained athletes ready for the task before them.

## Book 1 Chapter 60: Battle of the Aegusian Islands

The Carthaginians, when news reached them—contrary to all expectation—that the Romans had put to sea with a fleet and were once again contesting the mastery of the sea, at once fitted out their ships.

Loading them with grain and other provisions, they dispatched the fleet, determined that their forces around Eryx should lack none of the necessities.

They appointed Hanno commander of the naval force. He put out to sea and made landfall at the island called Holy Isle, intent on slipping across to Eryx unobserved by the enemy. There he meant to unload his cargo and lighten his ships, then take on board as marines the suitable men from among the mercenaries, along with Barcas, and only then to engage the enemy.

But Lutatius grasped the arrival of Hanno's force and worked out their intention. Taking the best men from his land army, he sailed to the island of Aegusa, which lies off Lilybaeum.

There he addressed his forces with words fitting the occasion and informed the helmsmen that a sea-battle would take place the next day.

Toward dawn, as day was just breaking, he saw that the wind was bearing down fresh and full astern of the enemy, while for himself the run out against the wind would be difficult, the sea being hollow and rough. At first he was at a loss as to what to do in the circumstances.

But he reasoned that if he risked battle while the storm held, he would be fighting only against Hanno, against the bare naval crews, and against ships still heavily laden;

whereas if he waited for fair weather and held back, letting the enemy cross over and join their land forces, he would be fighting against ships that were nimble and lightened, against the best men of the land armies, and—most formidable of all—against the daring of Hamilcar, than which nothing at that time was more to be feared.

He therefore decided not to let the present opportunity slip; and seeing the enemy's ships running under sail, he put out in haste.

His crews, with their fine physical condition, rode the swell with ease, and he quickly drew up his fleet in a single line, prow facing—

## Book 1 Chapter 61: The Battle of Aegusa

—the enemy. The Carthaginians, seeing that the Romans were blocking their crossing, lowered their masts and, after each ship's company had exhorted one another, closed with their opponents.

Since the state of preparation on each side was the exact opposite of what it had been in the sea-battle off Drepana, the outcome of the fight for each side naturally turned out the opposite as well.

For the Romans had remodeled their shipbuilding and had stripped their vessels of all encumbrances except what was needed for the battle; their crews, well drilled and working together, served them with outstanding effect; and their marines were picked men from the legions, men who would never give ground.

With the Carthaginians the reverse was true. Their ships were laden and so unwieldy for the engagement; their crews were utterly untrained and had been thrown aboard for the emergency; and their marines were newly levied recruits, raw to all hardship and danger.

For because they had never expected the Romans to dispute the sea again, they had grown contemptuous and let their naval forces fall into neglect.

And so, the moment battle was joined, they were worsted at many points of the fighting and quickly gave way. Fifty of their ships were sunk, and seventy captured with their crews.

The rest of the host hoisted their sails and, running before the wind, made off again to Holy Isle, the wind luckily and unexpectedly shifting to serve their need at just the right moment.

The Roman commander, for his part, sailed back to Lilybaeum and busied himself with the army over the disposal of the captured ships and prisoners—a considerable task, for those taken alive in the battle fell little short of ten thousand.

## Book 1 Chapter 62: End of the First Punic War

When the defeat fell upon them unexpectedly, the Carthaginians, as far as their fighting spirit and ambition went, were still ready to carry on the war, but in their reckoning they were at a complete loss.

For they could no longer supply their forces in Sicily, since the enemy controlled the sea; yet to abandon those forces—and so, in a sense, to betray them—would leave them without the men or the leaders to keep fighting.

They therefore quickly sent word to Barcas and entrusted him with the decision over the whole matter. And he indeed acted thoroughly as a good and prudent commander should.

For as long as there was any reasonable hope in the existing circumstances, he left untried nothing that seemed hazardous or daring, but, if any commander ever did, he tested to the full every hope of victory in the war.

But once the situation had turned and no reasonable means was left to save the men under his command, he yielded to circumstances with great good sense and practical judgment, and sent envoys to negotiate a truce and a settlement.

For it is the mark of one and the same commander to be able to see both the moment for victory and, equally, the moment for giving way.

Lutatius readily accepted the overtures, since he was well aware that his own side was worn down and already exhausted by the war; and so it came about that the conflict was brought to an end, on terms drawn up roughly as follows:

"On these conditions there shall be friendship between the Carthaginians and the Romans, provided the Roman people also approve. The Carthaginians shall withdraw from the whole of Sicily, and shall not make war on Hiero, nor bear arms against the Syracusans or against the allies of the Syracusans.

The Carthaginians shall give back to the Romans all prisoners without ransom. The Carthaginians shall pay to the Romans, over twenty years, two thousand two hundred Euboic talents of silver."

## Book 1 Chapter 63: The Longest and Harshest War in History

When these terms were referred back to Rome, the people did not accept the treaty, but sent out ten commissioners to investigate the matter.

On their arrival they made no change in the substance of the terms, but they tightened them somewhat against the Carthaginians.

They cut the time allowed for paying the tribute in half, added a thousand talents to the sum, and further required the Carthaginians to withdraw from all the islands lying between Italy and Sicily.

Such, then, were the terms, and such the conclusion, of the war that arose between the Romans and Carthaginians over Sicily—a war fought continuously for twenty-four years, the longest, the most unbroken, and the greatest war we know of by report.

In it, apart from the other battles and preparations I have described above, the two sides fought against one another at sea once with more than five hundred quinqueremes between them, and a second time with not far short of seven hundred.

In this war the Romans lost, counting those destroyed in shipwrecks, close to seven hundred quinqueremes, and the Carthaginians about five hundred.

So that those who marvel at the sea-battles and fleets of Antigonus, Ptolemy, and Demetrius, had they looked into these events, might well have been astounded at the overwhelming scale of these operations.

And if one cared to weigh the difference in size between quinqueremes and the triremes with which the Persians fought the Greeks, and again the Athenians and Spartans fought one another, one would find that nowhere in all history have such great forces contended at sea.

From this it is clear—as I proposed at the outset—that it was not by chance, as some of the Greeks suppose, nor by accident, but with very good reason that the Romans, having trained themselves in such great affairs, not only set their hands boldly to the rule and dominion of the whole world, but also—

## Book 1 Chapter 64: Why Rome is No Longer a Naval Power

…they achieved their purpose. And here one might naturally be puzzled as to the reason why, now that they have mastered the whole world and possess a superiority many times greater than before, they would be unable either to man so many ships or to put to sea with such great fleets as they once did.

The causes of this puzzle, however, will be clearly understood once we come to the account of their constitution—a subject that we must not treat as a mere aside, nor one to which our listeners should give only idle attention.

For it is a fine thing to contemplate, and one that remains, so to speak, virtually unknown down to the present day, thanks to those who have written about it.

Some of them have simply been ignorant of it, while others have produced an account that is unclear and altogether useless.

Still, in the war just mentioned one would find that the policies of the two states were closely matched—not only in their plans of attack but also in their greatness of spirit, and most of all in their ambition for supremacy.

In the courage of the individual men, however, the Romans were in every respect far the more gallant; while as a commander, the finest of that age in both judgment and daring, we must rank Hamilcar, surnamed Barcas, the natural father of the Hannibal who afterwards made war on the Romans.

## Book 1 Chapter 65: The Sequel of the First Punic War

After this settlement, both states happened to undergo an experience that was peculiar and yet much alike.

For an internal war broke out to receive the Romans—the one against the people called the Faliscans—which they brought to a swift and profitable conclusion, gaining control of their city within a few days.

The Carthaginians, at about the same time, faced a war that was neither small nor to be despised: the one against their foreign mercenaries, the Numidians, and the Libyans who joined them in their revolt.

In the course of it they endured many great terrors, and in the end were in danger not only over their territory but over their own survival and the very soil of their homeland.

It is worth dwelling on this war for several reasons, but, in keeping with my original plan, I shall give an account of it only in summary and briefly.

For the war commonly called the "truceless" war—what its nature and character were—one would best come to understand from what happened then;

and those who employ mercenary forces would see most vividly, from the situation of that time, what they must foresee and guard against well in advance; and besides this, how greatly, and by how much, characters that are mixed and barbarous differ from those reared under education, laws, and civic customs.

But most important of all, one would grasp, from what was done in those days, the causes from which the war between the Romans and Carthaginians in the time of Hannibal arose.

Since the causes of that war are still disputed not only among historians but also among those who took part in it, it is useful to set before students of the subject the truest possible account of them.

## Book 1 Chapter 66: Mischief Brewing Among the Mercenaries

As soon as the aforesaid settlement had been carried out, Barcas brought the forces around Eryx back to Lilybaeum and immediately laid down his command; and Gesco, the commandant of the city, set about transporting the soldiers across into Libya.

Foreseeing what was coming, he prudently embarked them in separate detachments, leaving intervals between each dispatch,

with the aim of giving the Carthaginians time, so that those who had sailed across and been paid the arrears of their wages might get clear of Carthage and reach home before the next group to be ferried over arrived to overtake them.

Gesco, then, holding to this idea, was managing the dispatch in this way.

But the Carthaginians—partly because they were short of funds owing to their recent expenses, and partly because they were convinced that the mercenaries would forgo some portion of the wages owed them if they gathered and received them all together in Carthage—kept the arrivals there in this hope and held them in the city.

When this led to more and more wrongdoing, both by night and by day, the Carthaginians at first grew wary of the crowd and the lack of discipline that was developing, and asked the officers that, until arrangements for their pay rations were ready and the rest had arrived, all the men should withdraw to a certain town called Sicca, each taking a gold piece for his immediate needs.

The men readily agreed to the departure and wished to leave their baggage there, as they had at first, on the grounds that they would soon return for their payment.

But the Carthaginians were anxious that the men, having been away for a long time and longing—some for their children, some also for their wives—might either not set out at all, or, having set out, might turn back again for these reasons, so that in this way wrongdoing in the city would be no less than before.

Foreseeing this, they compelled the men, with much ill feeling and entirely against their will, to take their baggage away with them.

The mercenaries, gathered together at Sicca and having for a long time enjoyed rest and leisure—which is the most ruinous thing for foreign forces and is, one might almost say, the chief and sole cause of mutiny—passed their time without restraint.

And, in their idleness, some of them began to reckon up the wages owed them at an inflated figure, and, totaling it at many times what was actually due, declared that this was what they should demand from the Carthaginians.

## Book 1 Chapter 67: The Beginning of the Outbreak

…of the redress they expected. So, as soon as they had all assembled at Sicca, and Hanno—who was then the Carthaginian general in Libya—arrived and, far from fulfilling their hopes and the promises made to them, spoke instead of the weight of the tribute and the general financial straits of the city, and tried to get them to forgo even part of the wages acknowledged to be owed them,

at once dissension and faction arose, and continual gatherings took place, sometimes by nationality, sometimes of them all together.

And since they were neither of one race nor of one tongue, the camp was full of disorder, uproar, and what is called sheer confusion.

For the Carthaginians, always relying on varied mercenary forces, are right in their aim, by making the army up of many races, that the men should not quickly conspire and become disobedient, nor be hard for their commanders to overawe;

but when anger, or slander, or faction has arisen, they utterly fail in instructing, calming, and bringing back to their senses men who have gone astray.

For such forces, once they have fallen into anger and mutual suspicion toward anyone, no longer behave with merely human wickedness, but in the end turn savage like beasts and take on a frenzied disposition.

This is exactly what happened to them on this occasion. For some were Iberians, some Celts, some Ligurians and Balearic Islanders, and not a few were half-Greeks, most of these deserters and slaves; but the largest part of them were Libyans.

So it was impossible to gather them all together into one assembly, or to devise any other means to that end.

How could it be done? It was impossible for the general to know all their languages; and to hold an assembly through many interpreters, saying the same thing four or five times over, was, one might say, even more impossible than the former.

The only course left was to make his requests and appeals through the officers, which is what Hanno kept trying to do at the time.

Yet here too it turned out that the officers either did not grasp what was said, or, having sometimes agreed with the general, reported the opposite to the men—some out of ignorance, some out of malice—so that everything was full of obscurity, mistrust, and confusion.

For on top of everything else, they suspected that the Carthaginians were deliberately not sending them the generals who knew the services they had rendered in Sicily and who had made them their promises, but had dispatched the one man who had been present at none of these dealings.

In the end, then, they rejected Hanno, lost confidence in their individual officers, and, enraged at the Carthaginians, set off for the city; they encamped about a hundred and twenty stades from Carthage at the place called Tunes, being more than twenty thousand in number.

## Book 1 Chapter 68: The Mercenaries Accept an Arbitration

Nonetheless, terrified by the encampment, they endured everything in their eagerness to appease the men's anger;

## Book 1 Chapter 71: Despair at Carthage

There are three things to bear in mind about the Carthaginians. First, the private citizens always supported their own livelihoods from the produce of the land; second, they assembled their public resources and supplies for war from the revenues of Libya; and third, they were accustomed to wage war with foreign mercenary forces.

At this moment they were not only stripped of all these resources at once, against every expectation, but they actually saw each one of the things just mentioned turned against themselves. They sank into utter dejection and despair, since events had fallen out so contrary to their hopes.

For, worn down by the unbroken strain of the war over Sicily, they had hoped that, once the peace settlement was concluded, they would gain some breathing space and a period of welcome stability.

But the opposite befell them: the opening of a still greater and more terrifying war was now upon them.

Previously they had been contesting Sicily with the Romans; now they were about to risk everything for their own survival and for their homeland, taking up a civil war.

On top of this, they had no abundance of weapons, no naval force, and no fleet in good repair, having lost so much in all those sea-battles; nor was there any store of supplies, nor any hope whatsoever of friends or allies coming to their aid from outside.

So it was then that they came to understand clearly how great a difference there is between a foreign war fought across the sea and an internal uprising and upheaval.

And they themselves, more than anyone, had been the cause of such great evils...

## Book 1 Chapter 72: Revolt in the Country

...and of such grievous troubles. For during the previous war, supposing that they had reasonable grounds for it, they had governed the people of Libya with great harshness.

They had taken half of all the other crops, doubled the tribute imposed on the towns from what it had been before, and granted no pardon or consideration whatever to those in difficulty in any of these exactions.

And of their successive generals they admired and honored not those who treated the populace gently and humanely, but those who secured for them the most abundant supplies and provisions, even while dealing most cruelly with the country people—one of whom was Hanno.

So it was that the men needed not even any urging to revolt, but only a single messenger.

And the women, who in earlier times had looked on while their husbands and parents were being led off for the levies, now bound themselves by oath, town by town, to hide none of their possessions; and, stripping off their ornaments, they contributed them without reservation toward the soldiers' pay.

In this way they provided Mathos and Spendius with such ample means that they were able not only to pay off the wages still owed to the mercenaries, as they had promised when inciting the revolt, but also to keep themselves well supplied for the future.

So true it is that those who plan rightly must always look not only to the present, but still more to what lies ahead.

## Book 1 Chapter 73: The Carthaginians Prepare

Nevertheless, even in such a desperate state, the Carthaginians appointed Hanno as their general—because he was thought to have, on an earlier occasion, brought Hecatompylos in Libya under their control—and they began to gather mercenaries and to arm those of their citizens who were of military age.

They also trained and organized the citizen cavalry, and made ready what ships remained to them—triremes, penteconters, and the largest of their light boats.

Meanwhile Mathos, joined by as many as seventy thousand Libyans, divided these forces and kept up the sieges of the people of Utica and of Hippo Zarytus in security, while firmly holding the encampment at Tunes, and so cut the Carthaginians off from the whole of outer Libya.

For Carthage itself lies in a gulf, projecting outward like a peninsula by its situation, surrounded for the most part partly by the sea and partly by a lake.

The isthmus that joins it to Libya is about twenty-five stades wide. On the side of this isthmus that faces the open sea, and at no great distance, lies the city of Utica; on the other side, beside the lake, lies Tunes.

Encamping at both of these points, the mercenaries had now cut the Carthaginians off from the countryside and went on to lay plans against the city itself,

approaching the wall sometimes by day and sometimes by night, and throwing those inside into utter fear and confusion...

## Book 1 Chapter 74: Failures of Hanno

...they struck panic into those within. As for Hanno, he was capable enough when it came to preparations, for he had a natural talent for this side of things;

but once he set out with his army he was a different man, for he made poor use of his opportunities and handled affairs as a whole with inexperience and sluggishness.

Thus, when he first marched to the relief of the besieged at Utica and struck terror into the enemy with the multitude of his beasts—for he had no fewer than a hundred elephants—and then, having gained the beginning of a decisive advantage, he made such bad use of it that he came close to destroying the besieged as well.

For he brought out from the city the catapults, the missiles, and, in short, all the equipment for the siege, and, encamping before the city, undertook to attack the enemy's stockade.

When the beasts forced their way into the enemy's camp, the foe, unable to withstand their weight and the force of their charge, all fled from the encampment.

Many of them died from wounds inflicted by the beasts, while the part that survived took up position on a certain steep, thickly wooded hill, trusting in the natural security of the ground.

But Hanno, used to fighting against Numidians and Libyans—who, once they give way, keep up their flight for two or three days, putting as much distance between themselves and the enemy as they can—supposed that on this occasion too the war was at an end and that he had won a complete victory.

So he neglected his soldiers and the camp as a whole, and went into the city himself to attend to his own comfort.

But the mercenaries who had taken refuge on the hill had been bred up in the daring tactics of Barcas, and were accustomed from their battles in Sicily to retreat at one moment and then, in a sudden reversal, to attack the enemy again, often several times in the same day.

Now, seeing that the general had withdrawn into the city, and that most of the men, because of their victory, had grown careless and were drifting away from the camp,

they formed up together and fell upon the stockade. They killed many of the soldiers and forced the rest to flee in disgrace beneath the city walls and gates,

and they seized all the baggage and all the siege equipment of the besieged, which Hanno, on top of everything else, had brought out from the city and so delivered into the hands of the enemy.

## Book 1 Chapter 75: Hamilcar Barcas Becomes Commander in Chief

At his very first sortie he struck the enemy with dismay by the unexpectedness of his move, broke their spirit, raised the siege of Utica, and showed himself worthy of his earlier deeds and of the people's high expectations.

What he accomplished in this operation was as follows. The hills that bridge the neck of land joining Carthage to Libya are hard to cross, and have only man-made passes leading out to the countryside; and it happened that Mathos had occupied with garrisons all the conveniently placed points along these hills.

In addition, the river called Macaras likewise blocks, at certain points, the route from the city to the countryside, being for the most part impassable because of the volume of its current; and since there was only one bridge over it, Mathos was guarding this crossing securely, having built a town upon it.

The result was that the Carthaginians could not even set foot in the country with an army—indeed, not even private individuals wishing to slip through could easily do so without being detected by the enemy.

With his eye on these difficulties, and testing every plan and every opportunity because of the trouble of getting out, Hamilcar devised the following scheme.

He had observed that at the river's mouth, where it discharges into the sea, the opening became silted up under certain conditions of wind, so that the passage right at the mouth turned shallow and marsh-like. He therefore got everything ready in the camp for the march out, keeping his plan hidden within himself, and waited for this condition to occur.

When the moment presented itself, he set out by night and, unobserved by anyone, brought his force across the place just mentioned by daybreak.

...who were guarding the bridge. When Spendius and his men realized what had happened, they came out into the plain and moved to support one another: those from the town at the bridge numbered no fewer than ten thousand, and those from Utica more than fifteen thousand.

In the end, some of them ran into their own men coming up behind, stumbled, and destroyed both themselves and their comrades; but the greater number were trampled down, with the cavalry and the beasts bearing down upon them at close quarters.

## Book 1 Chapter 80: The Murder of Gesco

...bringing a message similar to the one from Sardinia. Following up on this, Autaritus the Gaul declared that there was only one path to safety for their cause: to give up entirely every hope that rested on the Carthaginians.

As long as anyone clung to the prospect of the Carthaginians' kindness, he said, that man could not become a genuine ally to them.

## Book 1 Chapter 81: Atrocities of the Mutineers

When this calamity was reported to them, the Carthaginians could do nothing. In their grief and anguish at the disaster, they sent envoys to Hamilcar and to the other general, Hanno, begging them to come to their aid and to avenge those who had suffered.

To the perpetrators of the sacrilege they sent heralds to ask for the recovery of the dead bodies.

But the mutineers neither gave them up nor allowed it; they warned those present to send them no further herald or envoy, declaring that anyone who came would suffer the same punishment that Gesco had just undergone.

And for the future they passed a resolution, and exhorted one another to keep it: that any Carthaginian they caught they would torture and kill, and any of Carthage's allies they would send back to Carthage with his hands cut off. And this they continued to carry out scrupulously.

Looking at all this, one would not hesitate to say that it is not only the bodies of men — and certain ulcers and tumors that grow in them — that become so inflamed and utterly beyond cure, but, far more so, their souls as well.

For with ulcers, if you apply treatment, they are sometimes irritated by the very treatment and spread the faster; but if, on the other hand, you leave them alone, by their own nature they keep destroying the surrounding flesh and do not stop until they have consumed what lies beneath.

In much the same way, such gangrenous and festering growths often fasten upon men's souls, so that no creature becomes more impious or more savage than man.

Toward such men, if you offer any pardon and kindness, they take what happens to be a plot and a trick, and they grow more distrustful and more hostile toward those who treat them kindly.

But if you retaliate, then, vying with them in fury, there is no forbidden or dreadful act they will not undertake, counting such audacity a fine thing — until in the end, brutalized, they pass out of human nature altogether.

The chief and largest cause of such a disposition we must reckon to be wicked customs and a bad upbringing from childhood; but there are several contributing causes as well, and the greatest of these is the persistent arrogance and greed of those in command.

This was exactly what was happening at that time, both with the body of mercenaries as a whole and, still more, with their leaders.

## Book 1 Chapter 82: Revolt of Hippo Zarytus and Utica

Hamilcar, hard pressed by the desperate fury of the enemy, summoned Hanno to join him, convinced that once the two armies were combined he would more quickly bring the whole affair to an end.

Of the enemy he overpowered, some he destroyed in hand-to-hand combat, while those who were brought to him alive he threw to the elephants — seeing this as the one solution, if he could utterly wipe out his foes.

But just when the Carthaginians seemed to have brighter hopes regarding the war, a complete and unexpected reversal of their fortunes came over them.

For the two generals, having joined forces, fell into such a quarrel with each other that they not only let slip their chances against the enemy but, through their rivalry with one another, even handed the enemy many opportunities against themselves.

When the Carthaginians realized this, they ordered one of the generals to leave and the other to remain — whichever one the troops should choose.

At the same time, the supplies being brought to them from the place they call the Emporia, on which they had pinned their greatest hopes for food and other necessities, happened to be completely destroyed at sea by a storm.

And the affairs of Sardinia, as I said above, had been lost to them — an island that had always rendered them great service in their times of crisis.

But the greatest blow of all was the revolt of the cities of Hippo Zarytus and Utica. These were the only cities in all Libya that had not only borne the present war nobly, but had also gallantly endured the times of Agathocles and the invasion of the Romans — in short, had never once adopted any policy hostile to Carthage.

But now, quite apart from their irrational revolt to the Libyans, they immediately showed by their defection the greatest friendship and loyalty toward them, while toward the Carthaginians they conceived an implacable anger and hatred.

For they killed the men who had come from Carthage to help them — about five hundred — along with their commander, and threw them all down from the wall; they handed the city over to the Libyans; and they would not even permit the Carthaginians to bury their unfortunate dead, though they asked to do so.

While these things were happening, Mathos and Spendius, emboldened by these events, undertook to lay siege to Carthage itself.

Barcas, meanwhile, took on the general Hannibal — for the citizens had sent this man out to the army after the troops decided that Hanno must withdraw, in accordance with the authority the Carthaginians had granted them regarding the quarrels that had arisen between the generals.

And so Hamilcar, with Hannibal and Narávas, advanced through the country, cutting off the supply lines of Mathos and Spendius — in this and in everything else receiving the greatest service from Narávas the Numidian.

Such, then, was the situation of the forces in the field...

## Book 1 Chapter 83: Hippo and Utica Join the Rebels

...this was their state. The Carthaginians, hemmed in on every side, were compelled to fall back on their hopes from the cities allied with them.

Now Hiero, throughout the present war, had always shown great zeal in everything they asked of him,

and at this point he was even more eager, convinced that it was in his own interest — both for his sovereignty in Sicily and for his friendship with Rome — that the Carthaginians should survive, so that the dominant power should not be left entirely free to accomplish its purpose without resistance. His reasoning was thoroughly shrewd and sensible.

For one should never overlook such matters, nor help anyone build up so great a power that it becomes impossible even to dispute with him over rights that are universally acknowledged.

The Romans, too, observing the terms of justice laid down in their treaty, fell short in no point of goodwill.

At the outset, indeed, a dispute had arisen between the two sides, for the following sort of reasons.

The Carthaginians had been bringing in to themselves those who sailed from Italy to Libya carrying supplies to the enemy, and when nearly five hundred of these men had been collected in custody, the Romans were angered.

After that, however, they sent an embassy and recovered all the men by negotiation, and were so well satisfied that they at once gave back to the Carthaginians, in return, the prisoners still remaining in their hands from the war over Sicily.

From that time on they responded readily and generously to each request made of them.

For this reason they permitted their merchants to export to the Carthaginians whatever was urgently needed, while they forbade exports to the enemy.

And later, when the mercenaries in Sardinia — at the time they revolted from Carthage — tried to draw the Romans onto the island, they did not respond; and when the people of Utica offered to put themselves under Roman control, they did not accept them, keeping faith with the terms of the treaty.

## Book 1 Chapter 84: Hamilcar's Tactical Superiority

...siege. But Mathos and Spendius found themselves no less besieged than besieging.

For Hamilcar reduced them to such a shortage of provisions that in the end they were forced to break off the siege.

After some time they gathered the best of the mercenaries and the Libyans — in all about fifty thousand, among whom was Zarzas the Libyan with the men under his command — and set out once more to maneuver against Hamilcar in the open country and keep watch on him.

They kept away from the level ground, terrified of the elephants and of Narávas's cavalry, but they tried to seize the hill country and the narrow passes in advance.

In these operations it turned out that, in daring and in their plans of attack, they fell in no way short of their opponents, but because of their inexperience they were often worsted.

It was then, it seems, that one could see in actual fact how great a difference there is between methodical experience and the skill of a general on the one hand, and inexperience and mere unreasoning, soldierly routine on the other.

For in many of the smaller engagements he cut them off and hemmed them in, like a skilled draughts-player, and destroyed them without a fight;

and in the larger, decisive encounters he killed many by luring them into ambushes they never suspected, and threw others into panic by appearing suddenly and unexpectedly, sometimes by day and sometimes by night. All those he took alive he threw to the elephants.

Finally, having camped against them unexpectedly in ground unsuited to their needs but well suited to his own strength, he brought them to such a pass that — neither daring to risk a battle nor able to flee, because they were hemmed in on every side by trench and palisade — they were at last driven by famine to eat one another.

So the divine power was bringing upon them a fitting recompense for their impiety and lawlessness toward their neighbors.

For they did not dare to come out to battle, since defeat was certain and punishment plain for any who were captured; and as for terms, they did not even think to make mention of them, conscious of what they had done.

Still clinging always to the hope of relief from Tunes, because of the promises of their leaders, they endured to do anything, even against themselves.

## Book 1 Chapter 85: Hamilcar Captures Spendius

When they had impiously consumed their prisoners by using them for food, and had then likewise consumed the bodies of their slaves, and still no relief came from Tunes,

the maltreatment in store for the leaders now became unmistakable, given the desperate fury rising among the rank and file. So Autaritus, Zarzas, Spendius, and their companions decided to put themselves into the hands of the enemy and to open negotiations with Hamilcar about a settlement.

They therefore sent a herald, obtained safe-conduct for an embassy, and came to the Carthaginians, ten men in all.

With them Hamilcar concluded an agreement on these terms: the Carthaginians might pick out any ten of the enemy they wished, and would release the rest, each man keeping a single tunic.

As soon as the terms were settled, Hamilcar declared that, in accordance with the agreement, he chose the men then present. In this way the Carthaginians gained possession of Autaritus, Spendius, and the other most prominent leaders.

When the Libyans learned that their leaders had been seized, they assumed they had been betrayed—since they did not know the terms of the agreement—and on that account rushed to arms.

Hamilcar then surrounded them with his elephants and the rest of his force and destroyed them all, more than forty thousand men, near the place called the Saw, which, it turns out, owes its name to the resemblance of its shape to that tool.

## Book 1 Chapter 86: Mathōs Captures Hannibal

By accomplishing what has just been described, Hamilcar once again gave the Carthaginians great hope of a better outcome, though they had already despaired of survival. He himself, together with Naravas and Hannibal, went about the countryside and the cities.

As the Libyans came over and shifted their allegiance to them on account of the recent success, they brought most of the cities under their control and then advanced on Tunes and set about besieging Mathōs and his forces.

On the side facing Carthage, Hannibal pitched his camp; on the opposite side, Hamilcar.

After this they brought Spendius and the other captives up to the walls and crucified them in full view.

But Mathōs and his men, observing that Hannibal was behaving carelessly and over-confidently, attacked his palisade, killed many of the Carthaginians, drove them all out of the camp, seized the entire baggage train, and took the general Hannibal alive.

They brought him at once to Spendius's cross, and after torturing him cruelly, they took Spendius down, fastened Hannibal up alive in his place, and slaughtered around Spendius's body thirty of the most prominent Carthaginians,

Fortune, as if on purpose, giving each side in turn, by way of contrast, the occasion to outdo the other in vengeance.

Barcas was late in realizing that an attack had been made by the men from the town, because of the distance between the camps; and even once he did realize it, he could not bring help in time because of the rough ground that lay between.

He therefore broke camp from Tunes, marched on to the river Macaras, and encamped near the mouth of the river by the sea.

## Book 1 Chapter 87: Final Defeat of the Mutineers

This unexpected reversal once more left the Carthaginians dejected and despondent; for just as they were regaining their spirits, their hopes collapsed again in an instant.

Even so, they did not give up doing what they could for their own survival.

And so they appointed thirty members of the senate, and along with them Hanno—the general who had earlier withdrawn and was now brought back into command—and, arming all the remaining men of military age, they sent these off to Barcas as though making their last throw of the dice,

charging the members of the senate emphatically to settle, by any means possible, the dispute between the generals that had arisen earlier, and to force them to act in concert, in view of the present state of affairs.

The envoys put forward many and varied arguments, and once they had brought the generals face to face, Hanno and Barcas were compelled to give way and to be persuaded by what was said.

From then on they acted in concert, of one mind, and everything went according to the Carthaginians' wishes,

so that Mathōs and his men, finding themselves at a disadvantage in the engagements that took place piecemeal—for they fought many around the town called Leptis and some of the other cities—finally pressed to decide the issue by a pitched battle, the Carthaginians being eager for this course as well.

Both sides, therefore, having set their minds on this, summoned all their allies to the conflict and gathered the garrisons from the cities, intending to stake everything on the outcome.

When each side had its preparations ready for the attempt, they drew up in line and joined battle by mutual agreement.

The victory went to the Carthaginians: most of the Libyans were destroyed in the battle itself, while the rest fled together to a certain town and surrendered themselves not long afterward; and Mathōs fell into his enemies' hands alive.

## Book 1 Chapter 88: Aftereffects in Hippo, Utica, and Sardinia

After the battle, the other parts of Libya immediately submitted to the Carthaginians;

but the cities of the Hippacritae and the Uticans held out, having no opening for a settlement, since their initial undertakings had left them no room for mercy or pardon.

So true is it that, even in wrongdoing of this kind, there is a great difference made by moderation and by deliberately avoiding any irreparable act.

Nevertheless, by encamping against them—Hanno against the one, Barcas against the other—they quickly forced them to make terms and agreements satisfactory to the Carthaginians.

The Libyan War, then, after bringing the Carthaginians into such straits, had an outcome such that the Carthaginians not only regained control of Libya but also took fitting vengeance on those responsible for the revolt;

for at the very end, the young men led a triumphal procession through the city and inflicted every kind of torment upon Mathōs and his followers.

For three years and about four months the mercenaries waged this war against the Carthaginians—a war that, of all those we know of by report, far surpassed the rest in cruelty and lawlessness.

About this time the Romans, invited by the mercenaries who had deserted to them from Sardinia, set about sailing against that island. When the Carthaginians took offense at this,

on the ground that sovereignty over Sardinia belonged rather to them, and prepared to proceed against those who had made the island revolt from them,

the Romans seized on this as a pretext and voted for war against the Carthaginians, alleging that their preparations were directed not against the Sardinians but against themselves.

The Carthaginians, having against all expectation just escaped the war described above, and being in every way ill-disposed at that moment to take up their hostility toward the Romans again,

yielded to the circumstances and not only withdrew from Sardinia but also paid the Romans an additional twelve hundred talents on condition that they not have to take on the war for the present. So this was how these things came about.
