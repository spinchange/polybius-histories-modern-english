# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXIV

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 24 Chapter 1: The Spartan Exiles Refused

Olympiad 149, year 3. Affairs in Italy.

When envoys had arrived at Rome from the Spartans, from the Spartan exiles, and from the Achaeans, and at the same time also from Eumenes, from king Ariarathes, and from Pharnaces, the senate dealt first with these latter embassies.

A short time earlier the envoys with Marcus, whom they had sent regarding the war between Eumenes and Pharnaces, had reported back and had made clear both Eumenes' moderation in every respect and Pharnaces' greed and general arrogance.

So after hearing the envoys now present, the senate did not require many arguments, but answered that it would again send commissioners to investigate more carefully the matters in dispute between the kings.

After this the exiles from Sparta entered, together with the envoys from the city itself; and after listening to them at length, the senate did not censure the men from the city for what had taken place.

To the exiles, however, it promised that it would write to the Achaeans about securing their return home.

A few days later the envoys headed by Bippus of Argos, who had been sent by the Achaean people, entered and explained the settlement of the Messenian question.

The senate, showing displeasure at none of the arrangements, gave the envoys a gracious reception.

## Book 24 Chapter 2: Treaty Between the Achaeans and the Messenians

Affairs in Greece.

When the Spartan exiles arrived in the Peloponnese from Rome and brought letters from the senate to the Achaeans asking them to make provision for the exiles' return and safety in their homeland,

the Achaeans decided to postpone deliberation on the matter until their own envoys should come back.

After giving this reply to the exiles, they set up the inscribed treaty with the Messenians, granting them, besides the other generous terms, exemption from taxes for three years, so that the devastation of the territory might burden the Achaeans no less than the Messenians.

But when Bippus and his companions returned from Rome and explained that the letters about the exiles had been written not because the senate was eager for the measure, but because the exiles had pressed the matter,

the Achaeans resolved to stand by the existing arrangements.

## Book 24 Chapter 3: Mount Haemus in Thrace

In Crete the beginning of serious troubles was being set in motion—if indeed one should speak of a "beginning" of troubles in Crete.

For because civil wars there are unceasing, and because the savagery they show one another is extreme, beginning and end are the same thing in Crete; and what some people might think a paradoxical statement can there be seen happening constantly.

## Book 24 Chapter 4: Troubles in Crete

Affairs in Macedonia.

Mount Haemus lies toward the Pontus, the greatest and highest of the mountains in that region, dividing Thrace more or less in the middle. Polybius says that from it both seas can be seen, but he is not right; for the distance to the Adriatic is great, and many things obstruct the view.

## Book 24 Chapter 5: Attalus Goes to Rome

Olympiad 149, year 4. Affairs in Italy.

After Pharnaces, Attalus, and the rest had made terms with one another, all withdrew with their own forces to their respective countries.

At this time Eumenes had recovered from his illness and was staying at Pergamum. When his brother arrived and explained what had been arranged, he approved what had been done and decided to send all his brothers to Rome,

partly because he hoped to bring the war with Pharnaces to an end through their embassy, and partly because he was eager to introduce his brothers, both to their own private friends and hosts at Rome and to the senate as a body.

Since Attalus and those with him were also eager for the journey, they set out.

When they arrived at Rome, everyone in private life welcomed the young men warmly, since they had become acquainted with them during the campaigns in Asia; and the senate gave their presence an even more splendid reception.

It provided them with generous hospitality and the fullest support, and met them graciously in audience.

When Attalus and his companions entered the senate, they first renewed at greater length the previous expressions of goodwill, and then, after accusing Pharnaces, asked the senate to take some action by which he might receive the punishment he deserved.

After hearing them graciously, the senate replied that it would send commissioners who would use every possible means to end the war.

That was the situation in Italy.

## Book 24 Chapter 6: Murder of Apollonides At Sparta

Affairs in Greece.

About the same time king Ptolemy, wishing to involve himself with the Achaean League, sent an embassy promising to provide a complete squadron of ten fifty-oared ships.

The Achaeans gladly accepted the offer, both because the gift seemed worthy of gratitude and because the expense appeared to amount to not much less than ten talents.

Having resolved this, they appointed Lycortas, Polybius, and with them Aratus son of Aratus of Sicyon as envoys, to thank the king both for the arms and the money he had previously sent, to receive the ships, and to make arrangements for bringing them back.

They chose Lycortas because, when Ptolemy renewed the alliance, he was serving as general and had zealously cooperated with him at that time;

Polybius, though younger than the legal age, because his father had acted as envoy to Ptolemy in renewing the alliance and had brought to the Achaeans the gift of the arms and the money;

and similarly Aratus because of his inherited ties of friendship with the royal house.

This embassy, however, never set out, because Ptolemy died about that time.

## Book 24 Chapter 7: Chaeron the Spartan Demagogue

At the same time there was in Sparta a certain Chaeron, who had happened to serve the previous year as envoy to Rome, a man clever and effective in action, but young, obscure, and brought up in a popular style.

By stirring up the mob and venturing on measures no one else had dared to attempt, he quickly won a reputation with the masses.

First he seized the land which the tyrants had granted to the sisters, wives, mothers, and children left behind by the exiles, and distributed it among the poor at random and unevenly on his own authority.

After that, treating public property as though it were his own, he spent the revenues without regard for law, public decree, or magistrate.

This angered certain men, and they pressed to have auditors of the public accounts appointed according to the laws.

Chaeron saw what was happening, and, conscious that he had badly mishandled the city's affairs, he had Apollonides—the most distinguished of the auditors and the man best able to expose his greed—attacked as he was leaving a bathhouse in broad daylight; he sent men against him and had him stabbed to death.

When news of this reached the Achaeans and the people were outraged at what had happened, the general set out at once and came to Sparta. He brought Chaeron to trial for the murder of Apollonides, condemned him, and placed him in custody.

He also urged the remaining auditors to make a genuine investigation into the public funds, and to see that the relatives of the exiles recovered once more the properties of which Chaeron had deprived them a short time before.

## Book 24 Chapter 8: Pharnaces Invades Cappadocia

At this time, when Hyperbatus the general brought before the assembly the question of what should be done about the letters from Rome concerning the return of the Spartan exiles,

the party of Lycortas urged that they should stand by the existing arrangements, because the Romans, they said, were only doing what suited them by listening within reasonable limits to men who seemed to have been wronged in the matter of their property.

But whenever someone showed them that some of the requests were impossible, and others would bring great disgrace and damage upon their friends, they were not accustomed to persist or to force such demands through.

Therefore, even now, if anyone shows them that by obeying what is written the Achaeans would violate the oaths, the laws, and the inscribed agreements that hold our federal union together,

they will retreat and agree that we are right to stand firm and to ask to be excused from what they have written. Such was what Lycortas and his party said.

But Hyperbatus and Callicrates urged obedience to the Roman letters, and said that neither law nor inscription nor anything else should be considered more binding than this.

As this dispute went on, the Achaeans decided to send envoys to the senate to explain the points made by Lycortas.

They immediately appointed as envoys Callicrates of Leontium, Lydiadas of Megalopolis, and Aratus of Sicyon, and after giving them instructions in accordance with this policy, sent them off.

But when they arrived at Rome, Callicrates entered the senate and was so far from presenting the case in accordance with his instructions that, from the very outset, he undertook not only to denounce his political opponents boldly, but even to lecture the senate.

## Book 24 Chapter 9: Eumenes and Pharnaces

For he said that the Romans themselves were responsible for the Greeks' refusal to obey them, and for their disregard of both written orders and verbal instructions.

For in all democratic states at present there were two tendencies: one held that people ought to follow what the Romans wrote, and should regard neither law nor inscription nor anything else as more important than Rome's decision;

the other appealed to the laws, the oaths, and the inscribed agreements, and urged the masses not to violate these lightly.

This latter position, he said, was by far the more Achaean in character and the more successful with the multitude.

As a result, disrepute and slander among the crowds attended those who chose the Roman side, while the opposite fell to those who resisted.

So if the senate gave some clear sign, the politicians would quickly shift toward the Roman preference, and the masses too would follow them out of fear.

But if this side were neglected, everyone would incline to the other position, because it appeared more honorable and finer in the eyes of the people.

Thus even now certain men, though offering no other claim to justice but ambition, were receiving the highest honors in their own states simply because they seemed to oppose your written instructions, on the pretext of keeping their own laws and decrees in force.

Therefore, if the Romans are indifferent to having the Greeks obey them and comply with what they write, he said they should continue the course they are now following.

But if they want their instructions to be carried out and none of their written orders treated with contempt, they must give this matter the attention it deserves.

Otherwise, they should clearly understand that the opposite of their intentions will happen—which, he said, had in fact already happened.

For quite recently, in the Messenian affair, although Quintus Marcius had taken many steps to ensure that the Achaeans made no decision about the Messenians without Roman approval,

they disregarded him and voted for war; they not only devastated the whole country unjustly, but also drove some of the noblest citizens into exile, and others, after taking them in surrender, tortured in every way and put to death, merely because they appealed to Rome concerning the disputed matters.

And now again, although the Romans had for a long time been writing about the return of the Spartan exiles, the Achaeans were so far from obeying that they had even set up an inscribed stele and sworn agreements with those who held the city, pledging that the exiles should never be restored.

In view of all this, he urged them to take thought for the future.

## Book 24 Chapter 10: Callicrates Sent to Rome

After Callicrates had spoken these and similar words, he withdrew.

Then the exiles came in, briefly explained their own case, added some appeals to common pity, and departed.

The senate judged that Callicrates had said things advantageous to its own interests, and learned from him that it ought to strengthen those who supported its decrees and humble those who opposed them.

Thus, from that time onward, it first set itself to weakening in their own states the men who took the noblest course,

and to gathering around itself those who ran to it, whether justly or unjustly. As time went on, the result for Rome was that it came to have plenty of flatterers but few genuine friends.

At that moment, regarding the return of the exiles, it wrote not only to the Achaeans urging them to lend support, but also to the Aetolians, Epirotes, Athenians, Boeotians, and Acarnanians, summoning them all, as it were, as joint witnesses in order to crush the Achaeans.

And as for Callicrates himself, passing over his fellow envoys in silence, it inserted into its reply a remark that each state ought to have men like Callicrates.

Armed with these replies, the man returned to Greece exultant, not knowing that he had become the originator of great evils for all the Greeks, and especially for the Achaeans.

For even at that time it was still possible for them to maintain something like equal frankness in their dealings with the Romans, because they had kept faith in the most critical times from the moment they chose the Roman side—I mean in the wars against Philip and Antiochus.

And since the Achaean nation had grown and advanced in the best direction from the period covered by my history, this in turn became the beginning of its change for the worse: the audacity of Callicrates.

The Romans, being human beings and possessed of a noble spirit and good intentions, feel pity for those who have suffered misfortune and try to grant favors to all who seek refuge with them.

But whenever someone reminds them of what is just, having kept faith, they draw back and, so far as they can, correct themselves in most matters.

Callicrates, however, when he went to Rome at this time, was supposed to speak for the rights of the Achaeans; instead he acted in the opposite way, and by dragging in the Messenian affair—a matter about which the Romans were not even making accusations—returned to Achaea magnifying the fear inspired by Rome.

And by his embassy he overawed and broke the spirit of the masses, since the common people had no idea what he had truly said in the senate. First of all, he was elected general, and, to add to the other evils, he did so by bribery;

and after taking office he restored the exiles from Sparta and from Messene.

## Book 24 Chapter 11: Callicrates Turns Traitor

Philopoemen and Aristaenus, both Achaeans, were unlike each other both in natural character and in political choice.

Philopoemen was by nature well fitted, in both body and spirit, for military action; the other for civil deliberation.

In political judgment they differed in this way. Once Roman supremacy had become fully entangled with Greek affairs, both in the period of the wars against Philip and that against Antiochus,

Aristaenus conducted the policy of the state so as to do at once everything that suited the Romans, and in some matters even before they gave orders.

Still, he tried to appear to hold to the laws, and he cultivated that impression, giving way whenever any of them clearly conflicted with what the Romans wrote.

Philopoemen, on the other hand, agreed to and supported without excuse everything the Romans asked that was consistent with the laws and the alliance,

but whenever they ordered anything beyond those limits, he could not bring himself to comply willingly. Instead he said they must first make a legal case,

and then press their claim again; and if even then they failed to persuade, one should at last yield under protest and only then do what was required.

## Book 24 Chapter 12: Rome and the Achaean League

Aristaenus used such arguments as these before the Achaeans in defense of his own policy. He said that it was impossible, while holding out both the spear and the herald's staff at once, to preserve friendship with Rome.

"But if we are able to look them in the face and have the power to do so ...

if not, and if not even Philopoemen dares to say this ... one must be of one mind with the Romans in present circumstances,

why, by reaching for impossibilities, should we throw away what is possible?" For, he said, every policy has two aims: what is honorable and what is advantageous. Those for whom the attainment of honor is possible should cling to it if they govern rightly; but those for whom it is impossible must take refuge in advantage.

To miss both, he said, is the clearest mark of poor judgment. And this plainly happens to those who agree without excuse to everything ordered, yet do so unwillingly and with hesitation.

Therefore we must either show that we are capable of refusing obedience, or, if we do not dare even to say that, we must be ready to comply promptly with all commands."

## Book 24 Chapter 13: Comparison Between Philopoemen and Aristaenus

Philopoemen said one should not judge him so ignorant that he was unable to measure either the difference between the Roman and Achaean constitutions or the superiority of Roman power.

But since it is the nature of every superiority always to treat those under it more harshly, he asked whether it was really advantageous to cooperate with the impulses of the stronger power and put no obstacle in its way, so that we might as quickly as possible experience its heaviest commands—or rather, to struggle against it and hold out, so far as we are able, for as long as we can.

And even if they do issue commands, by reminding them of these considerations and checking their impulse we will at least restrain to some extent the bitterness of their power—especially since, as you yourself say, Aristaenus, the Romans up to the present care greatly for keeping oaths, treaties, and faith with allies.

But if we ourselves condemn our own rights from the outset and immediately, like men taken by the spear, present ourselves as ready for everything commanded, what difference will there be between the Achaean nation and the Sicilians or the Capuans, who have long been acknowledged slaves?

Therefore, he said, one must either concede this: that no justice has any force with the Romans; or, if one does not dare say that, one must make use of what is just and not abandon one's own cause, especially when we possess the greatest and noblest advantages in dealing with Rome.

For he said that he knew clearly this time would one day come for the Greeks, when they would be forced to do everything commanded.

"But would one wish to see that time come as quickly as possible, or as slowly as possible? Surely," he said, "as slowly as possible."

"That," he said, "is the difference between Aristaenus' policy and my own. He is eager to see the inevitable arrive as soon as possible and to assist it as far as he can; but I resist it and try to push it back, so far as it is possible for me."

From what has been said, it is clear that the policy of the one was honorable, and that of the other decorous, and that both were in fact safe.

Thus, when the greatest crises then confronted both Romans and Greeks in the wars against Philip and Antiochus, both men nevertheless preserved unimpaired the rights of the Achaeans in relation to Rome.

Yet a report circulated that Aristaenus was more favorably disposed toward the Romans than Philopoemen.

## Book 24 Chapter 14: Aristaenus's Attitude to Rome

Affairs in Asia.

In Asia king Pharnaces, once again disregarding the appeal that had been made to Rome, sent Leocritus during the winter with ten thousand troops to ravage Galatia,

while he himself, as spring was appearing, gathered his forces with the intention of invading Cappadocia.

When Eumenes learned this, he was deeply troubled because Pharnaces was overstepping every limit of good faith, yet he was compelled to act in much the same way.

And when he had already assembled his forces, Attalus and those with him sailed back from Rome.

Once they had come together and exchanged views, they immediately marched out with the army.

When they reached Galatia they no longer found Leocritus there. Cassignatus and Gaizatorix, who had taken Pharnaces' side the year before, sent messages to them asking for safety and promising to do whatever was ordered.

But Eumenes and his party rejected them because of their earlier disloyalty, and then advanced with their entire army against Pharnaces.

Starting from Calpitus, they reached the river Halys on the fifth day, and after six more days' march they moved on to Parnassus.

There Ariarathes, king of the Cappadocians, joined them with his own forces, and together they entered the territory of the Mocissi.

Just as they had encamped, news came that Roman envoys had arrived to negotiate a settlement.

When king Eumenes heard this, he sent Attalus to receive them, while he himself doubled his forces and carefully put them in order, both to meet the actual demands of the campaign and because he wished to show the Romans that he was capable, by his own power, of defending himself against Pharnaces—

## Book 24 Chapter 15: Philopoemen's Policy

—and of defeating him in war. When the envoys arrived and urged that the war be ended, Eumenes and Ariarathes said that they were ready for anything asked of them,

but they requested the Romans, if possible, to bring them together in conference with Pharnaces, so that by hearing the arguments face to face they might see more fully his faithlessness and cruelty.

If that was not possible, they asked the Romans themselves to act as fair and impartial judges of the dispute.

The envoys undertook to do everything possible and honorable, but they demanded that the army be withdrawn from the country,

for it was absurd that, while envoys were present and making proposals for peace, the operations of war should continue and the two sides should keep harming one another.

Eumenes and his party agreed, and immediately broke camp and marched off toward Galatia.

The Romans then met with Pharnaces and first demanded that he enter into discussions with Eumenes' party, since in that way the business would most likely be brought to a conclusion.

But when he resisted this point and finally refused outright, it was at once clear to the Romans that he was plainly condemning himself and had no confidence in his own case.

Nevertheless, since they were determined by every means to end the war, they persisted until he agreed to send plenipotentiaries by sea to Pergamum to conclude the peace on whatever terms the envoys should prescribe.

When the envoys had arrived and the Romans and Eumenes' party came together, the latter proved ready to accept everything for the sake of bringing the peace to completion.

But the representatives from Pharnaces disputed every point, would not stand by what had already been agreed, and kept introducing fresh demands and changing their minds. The Romans quickly saw that they were laboring in vain, for Pharnaces was not the sort of man to come to terms.

So when the conference accomplished nothing, the Romans departed from Pergamum, the envoys of Pharnaces were dismissed to return home, the war was left to continue by itself, and Eumenes' party again turned to preparations for it.
