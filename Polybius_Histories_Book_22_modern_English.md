# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 22 Chapter 1: Contents

Olympiad 147, year 4. Affairs in Greece.

Embassies came to Rome from Philip and from the peoples bordering on Macedonia; and the senate made its decisions concerning those envoys.

Philip's dispute in Greece with the Thessalians and Perrhaebians about the cities that Philip had held in Thessaly and Perrhaebia since the war with Antiochus.

The defense made on these matters before Quintus Caecilius at Tempe, and the judgments delivered by Caecilius and his colleagues.

Another hearing about the cities in Thrace, between Philip on the one side and the envoys from Eumenes and the exiles from Maroneia on the other; what was said about these matters at Thessalonica, and what Caecilius and his colleagues decided.

The massacre at Maroneia brought about by King Philip; the arrival of envoys from Rome and the orders they delivered; and the reasons why war arose between the Romans and Perseus.

In the Peloponnese, the arrival of envoys from King Ptolemy, from Eumenes, and from Seleucus.

The decisions of the Achaeans both about the alliance with Ptolemy and about the gifts offered to them by the kings just mentioned.

The arrival of Quintus Caecilius and his censure of the arrangements made at Sparta.

How Areus and Alcibiades, two of the earlier exiles from Sparta, went as envoys to Rome and brought accusations against Philopoemen and the Achaeans.

## Book 22 Chapter 2

In the 148th Olympiad Roman envoys arrived at Cleitor, and a synod of the Achaeans was held.

Their speeches on both sides about Spartan affairs, and the decisions taken by the Achaeans, are given here in summary.

## Book 22 Chapter 3: Sparta and the League

After the execution of the men at Compasium, some Spartans were offended by what had happened and thought that Philopoemen had destroyed both the power and the protected standing of Sparta. So they went to Rome and brought charges against Philopoemen and his measures.

In the end they obtained a letter to the Achaeans from Marcus Lepidus, who later became chief priest, but at that time held the consulship.

He wrote to the Achaeans saying that they had not handled Spartan affairs rightly.

When these envoys were on their mission, Philopoemen at once appointed Nicodemus of Elis and his colleagues and sent them to Rome.

At this same time Demetrius of Athens also arrived as envoy from Ptolemy, to renew the existing alliance between the king and the Achaean people.

The Achaeans gladly accepted the renewal, and appointed as envoys to Ptolemy my own father Lycortas, together with Theodoridas and Rositeles of Sicyon, so that they might swear the oath on behalf of the Achaeans and receive the king's oath in return.

At this time there also occurred an incident that may perhaps seem incidental, but is worth remembering. After the renewal of the alliance had been completed, Philopoemen entertained the envoy on behalf of the Achaeans.

During the gathering, when mention was made of the king, the envoy launched into a long speech praising Ptolemy, and offered various examples of his skill and daring in hunting, and then of his ability and training in horsemanship and the use of arms.

At the end he gave as proof of what he had said that once, while hunting, the king had struck a bull from horseback squarely with a javelin.

## Book 22 Chapter 4: The Murderers of Brachylles

In Boeotia, after the Romans had completed their treaty with Antiochus and the hopes of all those bent on revolution had been cut away, the governments of the cities began to take on a different spirit and direction.

So too, since the administration of justice among them had been dragging on for almost twenty-five years, people now began to say in the several cities that there ought to be a settlement and conclusion to their disputes with one another.

But because there was much controversy over this, since the discontented were more numerous than the wealthy, an accidental development occurred that helped the party which preferred the better course.

For Titus had long been active at Rome in trying to get Zeuxippus restored to Boeotia, since he had made much use of him as an associate during the wars with Antiochus and Philip.

And at this time he had persuaded the senate to write to the Boeotians that Zeuxippus and the exiles with him should be brought home again.

When this message arrived, the Boeotians, fearing that if these men returned they would be drawn away from Macedonian friendship, wanted the judgments against Zeuxippus and his associates, which had previously been entered against them, to be publicly proclaimed.

Thus in one case they condemned them for sacrilege, on the ground that they had stripped the silver from the table of Zeus, and in another to death for the murder of Brachylles.

After arranging matters in this way, they no longer paid attention to what was written, but sent envoys to Rome headed by Callicritus, saying that they could not annul measures that had been enacted among them according to law.

At the same time Zeuxippus himself sent an embassy to the senate, and the Romans wrote to the Aetolians and Achaeans about the attitude of the Boeotians, ordering them to restore Zeuxippus to his country.

The Achaeans refrained from making an incursion with an army, but chose envoys to go and urge the Boeotians to obey the Romans and to bring their legal disputes with the Achaeans to a conclusion, just as they administered justice among themselves.

For disputes between the two peoples too had already been dragging on for a long time.

When the Boeotians heard this, while Hippias was their general, they immediately promised to do what was asked.

But after a short time they paid no attention to any of it. Therefore Philopoemen, when Hippias had laid down office and Alcetas had taken it up, granted those who asked permission to make reprisals on the Boeotians.

From this there arose between the two nations the beginning of a quarrel not easy to despise.

For when there was a clash over the seizure of the herds of Myrrichus and Simon, the dispute ceased to be political and became the opening and first prelude of open war.

If the senate had pressed the next step in the restoration of Zeuxippus and his party, war would quickly have burst into flame.

But as it was, the senate let the matter drop, and the Megarians put a stop to the reprisals after envoys intervened and settlements were arranged.

## Book 22 Chapter 5: Quarrel Between Lycians and Rhodians

A quarrel arose between the Lycians and the Rhodians for the following reasons.

At the time when the ten commissioners were arranging affairs in Asia, envoys arrived from Rhodes, Theaetetus and Philophron, asking that Lycia and Caria should be granted to them in return for the goodwill and zeal they had shown in the war with Antiochus.

Envoys also came from the Ilians, Hipparchus and Satyrus, asking that out of regard for their kinship with the Lycians, pardon should be granted to the Lycians for their offenses.

After hearing both sides, the ten commissioners tried as far as possible to satisfy each party. For the sake of the Ilians they resolved to do nothing irreparable to the Lycians, but to gratify the Rhodians they assigned the Lycians to them as a gift.

This settlement became the source of a serious political division and quarrel between the Lycians and the Rhodians.

For the Ilians, as they went through the Lycian cities, reported that they had turned aside Roman anger and had become the authors of Lycian freedom.

But Theaetetus and his colleagues announced in their own country that Lycia and the parts of Caria as far as the Maeander had been granted by the Romans to the Rhodians as a free gift.

After this the Lycians sent envoys to Rhodes about an alliance, while the Rhodians selected some of their citizens and sent them out to give orders to the cities in Lycia and Caria about what each was to do.

Since the two sides understood the situation in entirely different ways, for some time the disagreement between them was not obvious to everyone.

But when the Lycian envoys entered the assembly and began speaking about an alliance, and after them Pothion the Rhodian prytanis stood up and made clear the intention of each side and sternly rebuked the Lycians, they declared that they would endure anything rather than do what the Rhodians ordered.

## Book 22 Chapter 6: Egypt Under Ptolemy Epiphanes After the Death of Aristomenes (18, 53, 54）

At the same time envoys came to Rome from King Eumenes to explain Philip's encroachments on the cities in Thrace.

The exiles from Maroneia also came, bringing accusations and laying the blame for their banishment on Philip.

Along with them came Athamanians, Perrhaebians, and Thessalians, insisting that the cities which Philip had taken from them during the war with Antiochus ought to be restored.

Envoys also arrived from Philip to answer all these accusers.

After many speeches had been made by all the parties just mentioned against Philip's envoys, the senate decided at once to appoint a commission to examine Philip's conduct and to provide security for any who wished to speak openly in the king's presence and accuse him.

So Quintus Caecilius, Marcus Baebius, and Tiberius Claudius were appointed.

And it happened that the people of Aenus had long been torn by civil strife, and had lately divided, some inclining toward Eumenes and others toward Macedonia.

## Book 22 Chapter 7: Seeds of the Third Macedonian War

In the Peloponnese, says Polybius, we have already explained how, while Philopoemen was still general, the Achaean nation sent envoys both to Rome on behalf of the city of the Spartans and to King Ptolemy to renew the alliance that already existed with him.

At the present time, when Aristaenus was general, envoys from King Ptolemy arrived while the Achaean assembly was in session at Megalopolis.

In addition to these, King Eumenes had sent envoys promising to give the Achaeans one hundred and twenty talents, on condition that the money should be lent out and the interest used to pay the Achaean council at the federal assemblies.

Envoys also came from King Seleucus to renew friendship and to promise the Achaeans a gift of ten decked warships.

When the assembly entered on business, the first to speak were Nicodemus of Elis and his colleagues, who explained to the Achaeans what they had said in the senate on behalf of Sparta and read out the replies.

From these replies one could gather that the senate disapproved of both the treatment of the walls and the killings at Compasium, yet did not mean to invalidate any of the measures taken.

Since no one either spoke against this or supported it, the matter was allowed to pass in that fashion.

After them the envoys from Eumenes entered, renewed the ancestral alliance, and explained to the crowd the king's offer of money.

After speaking further on these subjects and making the king's goodwill and generosity toward the nation plain, they ended their address.

## Book 22 Chapter 8: Origin of the Last Macedonian War

After they had ended, Apollonidas of Sicyon stood up and said that, in view of the amount of the money being offered, the gift was worthy of the Achaeans; but in view of the giver's purpose and of the use for which it was intended,

it was the most disgraceful and illegal of all gifts.

For since the laws forbade any man, private citizen or magistrate, to accept gifts from a king on any pretext whatever, it would be utterly unlawful for them all openly to accept this money and so all together to take bribes; and besides that, it would plainly be shameful.

For the council to draw yearly pay from Eumenes and then deliberate about public matters after swallowing such a bait had in it an obvious disgrace and an obvious injury.

For now Eumenes was offering money, but after him Prusias would do so, and after that Seleucus again.

Since the interests of kings and of democracies are opposed in character, and since most of our greatest deliberations are always about matters in dispute between ourselves and kings,

it is plainly inevitable that either the advantage of the kings will come to outweigh our own interest, or else, if that does not happen, we shall appear ungrateful to all men by opposing those who pay our wages.

Therefore he urged the Achaeans not only to refuse the offer, but even to hate Eumenes for conceiving such a gift.

After him Cassander of Aegina rose and reminded the Achaeans of the misfortune that had befallen the Aeginetans because they shared citizenship with the Achaeans, when Publius Sulpicius sailed in with his fleet and sold the poor Aeginetans into slavery.

We have already explained how the Aetolians, after gaining control of the city by treaty with Rome, handed it over to Attalus for thirty talents.

So, setting these facts before the eyes of the Achaeans, he urged that Eumenes should not try to hunt for Achaean goodwill by offering things of a different kind, but should win every expression of gratitude beyond dispute by giving the city back.

And he urged the Achaeans not to accept such gifts, by which they would show themselves to be cutting off for the future every hope of safety for the Aeginetans.

When such speeches had been made, the crowd was so moved that no one dared speak in support of the king; instead everyone shouted together and drove away the proposed gift, although the amount offered made it hard to look away from.

## Book 22 Chapter 9: The Senate Investigates Philip

After the matters already mentioned, the assembly took up the question of Ptolemy.

The envoys sent by the Achaeans to Ptolemy were called forward, and Lycortas came out with his fellow envoys and first explained how they had given and received the oaths of alliance.

Next he stated that they were bringing, as a gift for the Achaeans collectively, six thousand bronze peltast shields and two hundred talents of stamped bronze coin.

Besides this he praised the king, and after saying a few words about his goodwill and zeal toward the nation, he brought his speech to an end.

Then Aristaenus, the Achaean general, stood up and asked both the envoy from Ptolemy and the men sent by the Achaeans for the renewal exactly what alliance it was that they had renewed.

Since no one answered, and everyone began talking among themselves, the council chamber was full of bewilderment.

The reason for this absurdity was as follows. The Achaeans had more than one alliance with the kingdom of Ptolemy, and these differed greatly according to the circumstances of the times.

Yet the envoy from Ptolemy had made no distinction when he renewed it, but had spoken about the matter only in general terms, and the envoys sent from Achaea had done the same.

Acting as though there were only one alliance, they had both given the oath themselves and received it from the king.

Therefore, when the general produced all the alliances and distinguished them one by one, showing how great the differences were, the crowd demanded to know which one had been renewed.

Neither Philopoemen, who had held office when the renewal was made, nor Lycortas and the other envoys to Alexandria were able to give an explanation.

So these men seemed to have handled public business carelessly, while Aristaenus gained a great reputation as the only man who knew what he was saying; and in the end he did not allow the proposal to be ratified, but postponed it because of the blunder just described.

When the envoys from Seleucus entered, the Achaeans resolved to renew friendship with Seleucus, but for the present to decline the gift of the ships.

Having deliberated on these matters, they then broke up and each returned to his own city.

## Book 22 Chapter 10: A Meeting of the Achaean League Parliament

After this, while the festival was at its height, Quintus Caecilius came from Macedonia on his way back from the embassy on which he had gone to Philip.

Aristaenus the general gathered the magistrates in the city of Argos, and when Quintus entered he reproached them, saying that they had dealt with the Spartans more heavily and bitterly than was right, and urged them at length to correct the earlier mistake.

Aristaenus remained silent, making it plain by that very silence that he was dissatisfied with what had been done and approved what Caecilius said.

But Diophanes of Megalopolis, a man more soldierly than statesmanlike, stood up and, far from making any defense of the Achaeans, actually suggested to Caecilius, out of his friction with Philopoemen, another accusation against the Achaeans.

For he said that not only had Spartan affairs been mishandled, but so had those of Messene.

In fact there were among the Messenians certain disputes with one another about the matter of the exiles, involving the decree of Titus and Philopoemen's settlement of it.

So Caecilius, thinking that even among the Achaeans themselves he had some men who agreed with him, was all the more annoyed that those who had gathered did not readily follow his advice.

But Philopoemen and Lycortas, and with them Archon, set forth many various arguments to show that the settlement at Sparta had been made rightly and with advantage, most of all for the Spartans themselves, and that it was impossible to disturb any of the existing arrangements without violating both justice toward men and reverence toward the gods.

Therefore those present decided to stand by the existing arrangements and to give the envoy that answer.

Seeing their attitude, Caecilius demanded that the people be assembled in a public meeting.

But the Achaean magistrates told him to produce the instructions that he had from the senate on these matters. Since he kept silent, they said they would not convene the assembly for him.

For, they said, their laws did not allow it unless a man brought written instructions from the senate about the matter on which he thought the assembly ought to be called.

Caecilius became so angry because none of his demands was granted that he would not even accept the magistrates' answer, but departed without receiving any answer at all.

The Achaeans laid the blame both for the earlier visit together with Marcus Fulvius and for the present one of Caecilius upon Aristaenus and Diophanes, saying that through their rivalry with Philopoemen they had drawn the Romans in.

And many in the crowd felt suspicion toward those men. Such, then, was the condition of affairs in the Peloponnese.

## Book 22 Chapter 11: Apollonidas and Cassander Urge Rejection of Eumenes' Gifts

Olympiad 148, year 2. Affairs in Italy.

After the envoys with Caecilius had withdrawn from Greece and had made the senate fully aware of affairs in Macedonia and the Peloponnese, the senate introduced the envoys who had come on these matters.

First entered those from Philip and from Eumenes, and also the exiles from Aenus and Maroneia; and when they spoke in a way consistent with what had already been said before Caecilius and his colleagues at Thessalonica,

the senate decided to send other envoys again to Philip, to see first whether he had given up the cities in Thessaly and Perrhaebia in accordance with the answer he had made to Caecilius and his colleagues,

and then to order him to withdraw the garrisons from Aenus and Maroneia, and in general to depart from the coastal strongholds, places, and cities of Thrace.

After these men, they introduced those who had arrived from the Peloponnese.

For the Achaeans had sent envoys headed by Apollonidas of Sicyon to explain to Caecilius why he had received no answer and more generally to instruct the senate about the Spartan business.

And from Sparta Areus and Alcibiades had come as envoys; these were among the old exiles who had recently been restored to their country by Philopoemen and the Achaeans.

This above all stirred the Achaeans to anger, because it seemed to them a monstrous ingratitude that men who had so recently received such a great benefit should proceed immediately to an embassy of accusation against the very rulers who had unexpectedly saved them and brought them home again.

## Book 22 Chapter 12: Offers of Eumenes and Seleucus Declined

After these men too had made their opposing defenses in agreement with one another, and had instructed the senate—those with Apollonidas of Sicyon trying to show that Spartan affairs could not possibly have been handled better than the Achaeans and Philopoemen had handled them,

while those with Areus tried to say the opposite, alleging first that the city's power had been broken by the forcible removal of the multitude, and second that the constitution left behind among those who remained was insecure and speechless,

insecure because they were few and their walls had been torn down, and speechless because they not only had to obey the common decrees of the Achaeans but also had to serve individually whatever magistrates were from time to time set over them—

after hearing these men as well, the senate decided to give the same envoys instructions on these matters too, and appointed Appius Claudius and his colleagues as envoys to Macedonia and Greece.

The envoys from the Achaeans also defended the magistrates before Caecilius in the senate, saying that they had done nothing wrong and deserved no accusation for not summoning the assembly.

For among the Achaeans, they said, there was a law not to call together the people unless a deliberation had to be held about alliance or war, or unless someone brought letters from the senate.

Therefore the magistrates had acted rightly in deciding at that time to summon the Achaeans to an assembly, but were prevented by the laws because Caecilius neither brought letters from the senate nor was willing to give the magistrates his instructions in writing.

When this had been said, Caecilius rose and accused Philopoemen and Lycortas, and in general the Achaeans and the policy they had pursued toward the city of Sparta.

After hearing what was said, the senate gave the Achaeans this answer: on Spartan affairs it would send commissioners to examine the matter.

And it advised the envoys whom the Achaeans regularly sent from time to time to pay close attention and to show the reception that was fitting, just as the Romans themselves did for envoys who came to them.

## Book 22 Chapter 13: Caecilius In the Achaean Assembly

Affairs in Macedonia.

When King Philip learned, from messages sent to him by his own envoys from Rome, that he would necessarily have to withdraw from the cities in Thrace,

he took it badly, feeling that his power was being cut away on every side, and vented his anger on the wretched people of Maroneia.

For he summoned Onomastus, the officer placed over Thrace, and discussed the business with him.

Onomastus then withdrew and sent Cassander to Maroneia, since he was familiar to many there because he spent most of his time in that city.

Philip had long made a practice of placing courtiers in these cities and of making the inhabitants accustomed to their visits.

After some days, when the Thracians had been prepared and had been secretly brought in by Cassander during the night, a great slaughter took place and many of the Maronites were killed.

Having punished those who opposed him in this way and satisfied his own rage, Philip waited for the arrival of the envoys, convinced that no one would dare accuse him because of fear.

But after some time Appius and his colleagues arrived, quickly learned what had happened at Maroneia, and reproached Philip harshly for it.

Philip wanted to defend himself, saying that he had taken no part in the outrage, but that the Maronites themselves had fallen into this disaster because they were divided by civil conflict, some leaning in goodwill toward Eumenes and others toward him.

And he ordered that, if anyone accused him, he should be brought face to face with him.

He did this because he was convinced that no one would dare, since those who opposed him thought Philip's punishment was close at hand, while Roman help was far away.

But Appius and his colleagues replied that they had no need of his defense, since they knew perfectly well what had happened and who was responsible.

At this Philip was thrown into confusion, and so the first interview ended there.

## Book 22 Chapter 14: Philopoemen on Archon

On the following day Appius and his colleagues ordered Philip to send both Onomastus and Cassander at once to Rome, so that the senate might learn the truth of what had happened.

The king, utterly shaken and at a loss for a long time, said that he would send Cassander, the man who had actually carried out the deed, so that the senate, as they said, might learn the facts from him.

But he asked that Onomastus be excused, both on that occasion and later in his interviews with the envoys, using as his pretext that Onomastus had not been in Maroneia at the time of the massacre, nor even anywhere near the neighboring district.

In reality he feared that, if Onomastus appeared at Rome, since he had shared with him in many similar actions, he would reveal to the Romans not only the business at Maroneia but every other matter as well.

In the end he procured the exemption of Onomastus, but after the envoys had departed he sent Cassander off and escorted him as far as Epirus, where he had him killed with poison.

Appius and his colleagues, however, left Philip having already judged him guilty both of the lawless act against the Maronites and of estrangement from Rome.

When the king was again by himself, and shared the situation with his friends Apelles and Philocles, he clearly recognized that his quarrel with the Romans had advanced a long way, and that it was no longer hidden but plain to most people.

In general he was eager to defend himself and to take revenge on them by every means; but because he was not yet ready for some of the plans he had in mind, he turned his attention to how there might still be some reversal of the situation and some gain of time for preparations for war.

So he decided to send his youngest son Demetrius to Rome, partly to make a defense against the charges, and partly to ask pardon as well, if in any respect some mistake had occurred on his side.

For he was thoroughly convinced that through Demetrius he would obtain everything he wanted from the senate, because of the exceptional favor shown to the young man during his time as a hostage.

Having formed this plan, he set himself at once to arranging the dispatch of Demetrius and of the friends who were to be sent with him.

At the same time he promised help to the Byzantines, not so much because he cared about them as because, under cover of their cause, he wanted to overawe the Thracian dynasts who lived beyond the Propontis and so advance the design he had in mind.

## Book 22 Chapter 15: Caecilius Reports and Ambassadors Respond

In Crete, while Cydas son of Antalces was cosmus at Gortyn, the Gortynians, since they were doing everything possible to weaken the Cnossians, cut off parts of their territory, assigning the district called Lycastium to the Rhaucii and Diatonium to the Lyctians.

About this time envoys from Rome, Appius and his colleagues, arrived in Crete to settle the disputes then existing among the Cretans. After speaking on these matters before the Cnossians and Gortynians, they persuaded the Cretans to entrust their affairs to Appius and his commission.

They restored the territory to the Cnossians, and ordered the Cydoniatae to recover the hostages whom they had formerly left behind in the hands of Charmion, and to surrender Phalasarna without removing anything from it.

As for participation in the common judicial system, they granted that those who wished might share in it.

Those who did not wish might also stand apart.

But in that case they, together with the exiles from Phalasarna, were to keep away from the rest of Crete altogether; and after a gap in the text, we are told that they killed Menoetius and his associates, men of the highest standing among the citizens.

## Book 22 Chapter 16: Another Commission For Greece

Calling him king and offspring of gods, they said that if he abused such a name he might perhaps obtain some pity and pardon for what had happened; yet it would have been more kingly not to be the first to break faith, but rather to let others violate agreements before he did so himself.

They said that all men admire Philip for his greatness of spirit: although he suffered evil treatment, and not merely abuse, from the Athenians, yet after defeating them in the battle at Chaeronea he was so far from using the occasion to injure his enemies that he buried the Athenian dead with due rites, and the prisoners he clothed and sent back to their families without ransom.

Yet men imitate such conduct least of all; instead they compete in anger and punishments against those with whom they are at war, for that very reason.

Ptolemy, by contrast, bound the men naked to carts, dragged them along, and after inflicting punishment killed them.

## Book 22 Chapter 17: Philip and the Massacre At Maroneia

When King Ptolemy of Egypt laid siege to the city of Lycopolis, the Egyptian dynasts were terrified by what had happened and surrendered themselves to the king's good faith.

He treated them cruelly, and thereby brought himself into many dangers.

Something similar happened again at the time when Polycrates subdued the rebels.

For Athinis, Pausiras, Chesuphus, and Irobastus, who were still surviving among the dynasts, yielded to circumstances and came to Sais, committing themselves to the king's protection.

But Ptolemy broke faith with them, tied the men naked to carts, dragged them along, and then put them to death after punishment.

Then he came with his army to Naucratis; and when Aristonicus presented to him the mercenary troops he had recruited in Greece, he accepted them and sailed away to Alexandria.

He had taken no part in the actual operations of the war because of Polycrates' misguided policy, though he was already twenty-five years old.

## Book 22 Chapter 18: Philip Hopes to Defer War With Rome

Olympiad 148, year 4. Affairs in Macedonia and Greece.

Polybius says in the twenty-second book that already from this time there began to grow for the royal house in Macedonia the beginning of disasters beyond remedy.

I am not unaware that some historians, when they wish to show us the causes of the war that arose between the Romans and Perseus, first point to the expulsion of Abrupolis from his own principality, after he had raided the mines around Pangaeum following Philip's death.

Perseus came to the rescue, defeated him, and completely drove him out of his dominion.

After that they cite the invasion of Dolopia and the appearance of Perseus at Delphi,

and further the plot laid against King Eumenes at Delphi and the murder of the envoys from Boeotia, from which some say the war of Perseus against the Romans sprang.

But I maintain that it is of the highest importance both for writers and for serious learners to know the causes from which each event is generated and grows; yet most historians confuse these matters because they fail to keep hold of the distinction between a pretext and a cause, and again between a pretext and the beginning of a war.

And now, since the events themselves remind me of this, I have been compelled to renew the same discussion.

For of the matters just mentioned, the earlier ones are pretexts; but the later ones—the plot against King Eumenes, the murder of the envoys, and other similar things that happened at the same time—are obvious beginnings of the war that arose between the Romans and Perseus and of the overthrow of Macedonian power.

But in the strict sense none of these is its cause. This will be clear from what follows.

For just as we say that Philip son of Amyntas conceived and determined upon carrying out the war against the Persians, while Alexander became the executor of the enterprises his father had resolved on, so too now we say that Philip son of Demetrius first conceived the last war against Rome and had all the preparations ready for this design, while after his death Perseus became the man who carried the plan into action.

If this is true, then this too is clear: the causes cannot have arisen later than the death of the man who resolved upon and planned the war. Yet that is what follows from the account of the other historians, since everything they mention took place after Philip's death.

## Book 22 Chapter 19: Disputes in Crete

Philopoemen had some disagreement in argument with Archon the general. Philopoemen, however, being pleased at the moment by what had been said and changing his mind, praised Archon warmly as a man who had handled the occasion with both skill and cunning.

But for my own part, says Polybius, the remark did not please me then when I was present, since it amounted to praising a man while at the same time doing him moral harm; nor has it pleased me since, as my years have advanced.

For it seems to me that there is a very great difference in moral choice between a practical statesman and a mischievous intriguer, just as there is a similar difference between genuine dexterity and mere clever roguery. The one set of qualities is, so to speak, among the noblest of human things; the other is the very opposite.

But because of the lack of judgment now common among men, the things just mentioned, though they have very little in common, receive the same approval and the same eager admiration.

## Book 22 Chapter 20: Apollonias, Widow of Attalus

Affairs in Asia.

Apollonis, the wife of Attalus, father of King Eumenes, was a woman of Cyzicus and for many reasons worthy of remembrance and distinction.

For the fact that, though she was originally a private citizen, she became a queen and preserved that exalted place to the very end—not by the attractions of a courtesan, but by modesty, dignity in public life, and noble character—makes her deserve an honorable memory.

And further, though she outlived her husband by no short time, after bearing four sons she preserved toward all of them without surpassing measure the same goodwill and motherly affection down to the end of her life.

Her sons won a fine reputation during their visit by rendering their mother the gratitude and honor that were her due.

For taking their mother between them by both hands, they escorted her around the sanctuaries and through the city with all due attendance.

At this sight the spectators were greatly moved.

They welcomed and honored the young men, and recalling the story of Cleobis and Biton, compared their conduct with theirs, giving to the splendor of those old examples a further height through the lofty rank of kings.

This happened at Cyzicus after the settlement with King Prusias.

## Book 22 Chapter 21: Ostiagon Tries to Become King of All the Gauls

Ostiagon the Galatian, king of the Gauls in Asia, set his mind on transferring to himself the sovereignty of all the Gauls.

He possessed many resources for this aim, both by nature and by experience.

For he was generous, high-minded, gracious in personal dealings, and intelligent.

And what counted most among the Gauls, he was manly and capable in matters of war.

## Book 22 Chapter 22: Aristonicus

Affairs in Egypt.

Aristonicus, the eunuch of Ptolemy king of Egypt, had been brought up with the king from childhood.

As he grew older, he showed a courage and cast of mind more manly than one would expect in a eunuch.

For he was by nature a soldierly man and spent most of his time in such pursuits.

He was likewise effective in his dealings with others and possessed sound common sense, which is rare.

Besides all this, he was naturally well disposed toward acts of kindness to other people.
