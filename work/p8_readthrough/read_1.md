## 1.63 — The Longest and Harshest War in History

- 1.63 | "## Book 1 Chapter 63: The Longest and Harshest War in History" | The title is the 1889 rubric, and STYLE_SHEET says titles do not follow the crib. The Greek's superlatives are πολυχρονιώτατος καὶ συνεχέστατος καὶ μέγιστος ("longest, most continuous, greatest"). Nothing in the chapter says "harshest", and the first half of the chapter is the commissioners' revision of the peace terms. | "End of the First War: The Longest and Greatest War Known" (or "The Longest and Greatest War in History") | MED

Body text: CLEAN. The figures check against the Greek: ten commissioners, the payment period halved, plus 1,000 talents, more than 500 ships in one battle and not far short of 700 in the other, losses of about 700 Roman and 500 Carthaginian ships. The Greek runs on mid-sentence (ἐξ ὧν δῆλον …), and that sentence was correctly moved to the start of 1.64. "Spartans" is correct for narrative.

Flow verdict: smooth. The only problem is the title.

## 3.10 — Second Cause of the War

- 3.10 | "But he was forestalled by the civil disturbances, and in these he was occupied, taken up with those events." | This is a repeated clause. The Greek (end of 9 + start of 10: προκαταληφθεὶς δὲ ταῖς ἐμφυλίοις ταραχαῖς ἐν τούτοις καὶ περὶ ταύτας διέτριβε τὰς πράξεις) says a single thing: forestalled by the civil disturbances, he was taken up with them. The English renders ἐν τούτοις and περὶ ταύτας τὰς πράξεις as two separate clauses. | "But he was forestalled by the civil disturbances, and his time was taken up with them." | MED
- 3.10 | "This too must be counted a cause—the third—\n\nI mean the prosperous course of Carthaginian affairs in Iberia." | One sentence (ἣν δὴ καὶ τρίτην αἰτίαν νομιστέον, λέγω δὲ τὴν εὔροιαν …) is broken across a paragraph break and left hanging on a dash. | "This too must be counted a cause—the third: I mean the prosperous course of Carthaginian affairs in Iberia. For it was by relying on the strength gained there that they confidently entered upon the aforementioned war." (one paragraph) | HIGH
- 3.10 | "in the earlier books— without which" | STYLE_SHEET calls for em dashes without spaces, and here a space follows the dash. | "in the earlier books—without which" | HIGH
- 3.10 | "## Book 3 Chapter 10: Second Cause of the War" | The chapter gives both the second cause (the loss of Sardinia and the extra 1,200 talents) and the third (success in Iberia). The title follows the crib and covers only the second. | "Second and Third Causes of the War" | LOW

Sense: CLEAN. "Would not give way" (οὐκ ἐντρεπομένων), "on condition of not having to take on the war" (ἐφ᾽ ᾧ μή), the figure of 1,200 talents, and "second—and the most important" are all correct.

Flow verdict: mostly sound. The opening sentence (from the seam repair) is garbled by repetition, and the third-cause sentence is broken across a paragraph break.

## 3.54 — The Descent Into Italy

- 3.54 | "where the cliff face had fallen away for nearly a stade and a half, having been broken before but having now collapsed even more freshly" | The sense is right. τρί᾽ ἡμιστάδια = three half-stades = 1.5 stades, and the Greek says "broken away before, but now still more recently broken away". "collapsed even more freshly" is clumsy, though. | "where the cliff had broken away for nearly a stade and a half—it had given way before, but had now fallen away still further in a fresh slip" | LOW

Sense: CLEAN. The Gauls of the Po plain are correctly "Gauls" (Γαλατῶν). "To some extent" (ἐπὶ ποσόν) and "not much smaller than those lost on the ascent" (οὐ πολλῷ λείποντας) are correct. The seam sentence from 3.53 (the returning horses) is properly placed.

Flow verdict: good. There is one clumsy participial phrase at the end.

## 3.113 — The Order of Battle

- 3.113 | "so that the line formed a crescent-shaped bulge and grew thin in its own formation" | This is garbled. In λεπτύνων τὸ τούτων αὐτῶν σχῆμα, Hannibal is the subject: he thins the formation of these troops themselves, the Iberians and Gauls. It is not "the line grew thin in its own formation". | "making the bulge crescent-shaped and thinning out the formation of these troops" | MED

Sense: CLEAN. "Gaius" matches the Greek (Gaius Terentius Varro). The army faces south. The numbers are correct: about 80,000 infantry (εἰς ὀκτὼ μυριάδας) and a little more than 6,000 cavalry. Κελτοί is correctly rendered "Gauls/Gallic". The sides are right: Roman cavalry on the right by the river, Iberian and Gallic cavalry on Hannibal's left by the river, Numidians on his right.

Flow verdict: clear and accurate apart from the one garbled clause in the last sentence.
