## 6.11 — The Roman Constitution

- 6.11 | "The Roman state, reckoning from the crossing of Xerxes into Greece [...] and thirty years later;" | The Greek opens straight into the gap ("ὅτι ἀπὸ τῆς Ξέρξου διαβάσεως εἰς τὴν Ἑλλάδα **** καὶ τριάκοντα ἔτεσιν ὕστερον"). It has no subject; "The Roman state, reckoning" is supplied by the translator at the lacuna, and STYLE_SHEET forbids supplying text there. | "From the crossing of Xerxes into Greece [...] and thirty years later; from that time on it was continually being refined in its particular features, ..." (leave the rest of the sentence as it is) | MED
- 6.11 | "As for which of these forms held sway over each of the respective parts of the constitution—that, both then and still now (with a few exceptions), is as follows." | Wrong subject and object. In "ὧν δ᾽ ἕκαστον εἶδος μερῶν τῆς πολιτείας ἐπεκράτει" each part of the constitution is the subject, and ὧν is the set of things it controls. The English makes the forms control the parts. The sentence introduces the list of powers that follows in ch. 12 onward. | "As for the matters over which each of these parts of the constitution had control, they were then, and with a few exceptions still are, as follows." | MED
- 6.11 | "may appear not merely undesirable, but not even tolerable." | Garbled correlative. "οὐχ οἷον αἱρετόν, ἀλλ᾽ οὐδ᾽ ἀνεκτόν" means "not only not acceptable, but not even tolerable". The English "not merely undesirable, but not even tolerable" does not hold together logically. | "may appear not only unacceptable but not even tolerable." | LOW

Flow verdict: good overall. The opening, which runs straight into the lacuna, is the only rough spot, and the closing seam sentence reads badly because of the inversion noted above.
