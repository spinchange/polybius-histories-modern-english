## Book 6 Chapter 11: The Roman Constitution

The Roman state, reckoning from the crossing of Xerxes into Greece [...] and for thirty years after that period, was continually being refined in its particular features, and it reached its finest and most perfect form in the era of the war with Hannibal—the point from which I made this digression.

Therefore, now that I have given the account of how it came to be put together, I will try to make clear what its character was in those years, in which the Romans, defeated at the battle of Cannae, came to grief in their affairs as a whole.

I am well aware that to those who actually live under this constitution I shall seem to give a somewhat incomplete account of it, since I leave out some of the particular details.

For, knowing every part of it and having had experience of all of it through having been brought up from childhood in its customs and laws, they will not be impressed by what is said, but will look for what has been left out; and they will not suppose that the writer has deliberately omitted the small distinctions, but rather that he has passed over in silence the origins and the essential workings of things out of ignorance.

The things I do say, they would not have admired as being trifling and incidental; but the things I omit, they single out as essential, wishing to appear to know more than the historians themselves.

But a good critic ought not to judge writers by what they leave out, but by what they say; and if he catches any falsehood in this, he should recognize that those omissions too are due to ignorance, but if everything that is said is true, he should grant that those things too are passed over by deliberate judgment and not out of ignorance.

So much, then, let this be said by me against those who criticize historians more out of rivalry than out of fairness.

Every matter, when examined in its proper context, receives sound approval and sound criticism alike; but if that context shifts and the matter is judged against other circumstances, then what historians have often said in the best and truest way may appear not merely undesirable, but not even tolerable.

There were, then, three elements that controlled the constitution, all of which I have mentioned before; and everything was so fairly and fittingly ordered and administered, part by part, through these elements, that no one—not even a native—could ever say with certainty whether the constitution as a whole was aristocratic, democratic, or monarchic.

And this was a natural thing to experience. For whenever we fixed our attention on the power of the consuls, it appeared to be completely monarchic and kingly; whenever on that of the senate, aristocratic again; and indeed, if one looked at the power of the people, it seemed clearly to be democratic.

## Book 9 Chapter 24: Monomachus Proposes Eating Human Flesh

Something similar, it seems to me, happened in Hannibal’s case as well.

For he found himself in extraordinary and shifting circumstances, and his closest friends differed from one another greatly; so that from his conduct in Italy it is very hard to discern his true character.

What influence circumstances had upon him can easily be learned both from what has already been said and from what is still to be said. But one should not pass over the influence of his friends, especially since a single example is enough to make the matter clear.

At the time when Hannibal was planning to march with his forces from Iberia into Italy, the difficulty of food and of preparing supplies for the army appeared immense, both because the route seemed impossibly long and because of the number and savagery of the barbarian peoples living between.

At that time, when this difficulty was being discussed again and again in council, one of his friends, called Hannibal Monomachus, is said to have declared that he saw one and only one way by which it would be possible to reach Italy.

When Hannibal told him to speak, he said they must teach the army to eat human flesh and accustom them to it.

Hannibal could say nothing against the daring and practical force of the proposal, but he could not persuade either himself or his friends to entertain the idea.

Yet it is said that the deeds in Italy that were later attributed to Hannibal as proofs of cruelty were in fact the work of this man, though circumstances were responsible as well.

## Book 16 Chapter 24: War Between Rome and Philip V

At the beginning of winter, in the year when Publius Sulpicius became consul at Rome, King Philip was staying at Bargylia. Seeing that the Rhodians and Attalus were not disbanding their fleet, but actually filling up their crews and watching the coast with even greater energy, he was in a very troubled state and had many and varied thoughts about the future.

He was anxious at once about getting out from Bargylia and foresaw danger by sea, and at the same time he distrusted the situation in Macedonia and by no means wanted to winter in Asia, being afraid of both the Aetolians and the Romans.

For he was well aware of the embassies being sent to Rome against him, now that affairs in Libya had come to an end. So he was in extreme distress, yet for the moment he was forced to stay where he was and, as the saying goes, live the life of a wolf.

For because his army was starving, he fed it now by plundering and stealing from some, now by using force against others, and at times even by fawning in a way unnatural to him; sometimes they got meat, sometimes figs, and sometimes only a very scanty amount of grain.

Some of these supplies were provided by Zeuxis, others by the people of Mylasa, Alabanda, and Magnesia. When any of them gave him something he flattered them, but when they did not, he barked at them and plotted against them.

In the end he tried, through Philocles, to bring about operations against the city of Mylasa, but the attempt failed through the stupidity of the scheme.

As for the territory of the Alabandians, he ravaged it like enemy land, saying that he had to secure provisions for his army.

Polybius of Megalopolis also says in the sixteenth book of his Histories: “Philip, the father of Perseus, when he was overrunning Asia and was in want of provisions for his soldiers, received figs from the Magnesians because they had no grain. For that reason, when he got possession of Myus, he granted the place to the Magnesians in return for the figs.”

## Book 21 Chapter 32c: Philopoemen Unites the Noble and the Advantageous

What is noble and what is advantageous seldom run together, and rare are the men able to unite and harmonize them with each other.

For in most cases we all know that what is noble avoids the character of immediate profit, and profit avoids the character of what is noble.

Yet Philopoemen made this his aim and accomplished it. For it was noble to bring the captive exiles back to Sparta, and advantageous to humble the Spartan state by killing those who had served as bodyguards to the tyranny.

And seeing that money is the source from which every monarchy recovers itself, and being by nature a shrewd and statesmanlike man, he took measures to prevent any importation of funds gathered from outside.

## Book 36 Chapter 17: Fortune, Causes, and Greek Depopulation

Polybius says: in criticizing those who attribute events, whether public affairs or private reversals, to Fortune and Fate, I now wish to distinguish this matter as far as the method of factual history allows.

Where a human being cannot, God knows, discover the causes because they are either impossible or extremely difficult to grasp, one may perhaps have recourse to God and Fortune—for example, in the case of extraordinary and continuous rains or snowstorms, or, by contrast, droughts and frosts that ruin the crops, or similarly with recurring pestilences and other things like these, whose cause is not easy to discover.

Therefore, in such matters, since we cannot understand them, it is reasonable to follow the common opinion and, by prayer and sacrifice, seek to appease the divine and ask the gods what words or actions will be best for us and may bring an end to the evils that afflict us.

But where it is possible to discover the cause from which and through which the event arose, I do not think one should refer such matters to the divine.

Let me explain. In our own times, all Greece was overcome by childlessness and, in general, by a shortage of people, as a result of which the cities were deserted and scarcity followed, although there had been neither continuous wars nor pestilential outbreaks.

If someone were to advise us, in this matter, to ask the gods what we should say or do in order to become more numerous and better govern our cities, would he not seem foolish, since the cause is obvious and the remedy lies in ourselves?

For human beings had turned toward vanity and greed, and still more toward idleness, and did not wish either to marry or, if they did marry, to rear the children they had, but raised only one or at most two, so that they might leave them wealthy and pampered. Thus the evil grew quickly without being noticed.

For when there was only one child or two, and one was carried off by war and another by disease, it was obvious that the homes would be left empty; and, as with beehives, when the swarms are gone, the cities too, little by little, become helpless because of their emptiness.

For such things there is no need to ask the gods how we are to be freed from the harm.

Any chance person will say that we can best help ourselves by changing our habits; or, if not, by making laws so that children will be reared. On such matters there is no need of diviners or portents.

The same argument applies also in particular cases.

But in those matters where the causes are hidden or hard to grasp, one must investigate; and one of these was what happened to the Macedonians.

For the Macedonians had received many and great benefits from the Romans: collectively, they had all been freed from monarchical commands and taxes and had plainly exchanged slavery for freedom; and individually, as cities, they had again been released from great internal strife and civil slaughter through Roman favor. [...]

Yet because of the so-called Pseudo-Philip, many among them [...] they saw many of their own people in a very short time driven into exile, tortured, and killed, more than in earlier times under the true kings [...] they were angered at the Roman encampment itself and regretted their former wrongs against themselves [...].

From these things, who would not be puzzled by what happened? For the cause of them is hard to discover.

Therefore, in such a situation, one might say that the event was a divine affliction and that the wrath of the gods had overtaken all the Macedonians. This will become clear from what follows.
