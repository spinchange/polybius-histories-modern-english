## Book 5 Chapter 50: Death of Epigenes

When the forces had assembled at Apamea, a disturbance arose among the mass of the soldiers over the pay and rations still owed to them.

Seeing that the king was alarmed and frightened by the outbreak at so critical a moment, Hermeias promised to settle all the allowances due to the troops, provided the king would consent not to take Epigenes with him on the campaign.

For, he said, no orderly conduct of the expedition was possible when so much anger and faction had been stirred up among the men.

The king heard this with displeasure and was extremely eager, because of Epigenes’ experience in warfare, to have him accompany the campaign.

But he was so hemmed in and already so dominated by Hermeias’ scheming, watchfulness, and ingratiating service that he was no longer master of himself; so, yielding to the pressure of the moment, he granted what was demanded.

When Epigenes withdrew, as ordered, to his quarters, the men in the council were appalled at the spite behind it.

The troops, on the other hand, once they had received what they asked for, swung around and became well disposed toward the man responsible for setting their pay right, except for the Cyrrhestians.

These mutinied, and being nearly six thousand in number, went over to open revolt and caused a great deal of trouble for a considerable time. But in the end they were defeated in battle by one of the king’s generals; most were killed, and the survivors surrendered themselves to the king’s mercy.

Hermeias, having thus made the king’s friends dependent on him through fear and the troops through the services he had rendered them, broke camp and marched on with the king.

As for Epigenes, he devised the following plot against him, taking as his accomplice Alexis, the commander of the citadel of Apamea.

He wrote a letter as though it had been sent from Molon to Epigenes, and by dazzling one of Epigenes’ servants with great promises persuaded him to slip the letter into Epigenes’ papers.

Once this had been done, Alexis immediately appeared and asked Epigenes whether he had received any letters from Molon.

When Epigenes indignantly denied it, Alexis demanded the right to search. Entering at once, he quickly found the letter, and using it as his pretext, killed Epigenes on the spot.

After this, the king was persuaded that Epigenes had justly perished; those about the court, however, suspected what had really happened, but kept silent out of fear.

## Book 9 Chapter 17: Failures Arising From Ignorance

When Aratus, the Achaean general, planned to seize the city of Cynaetha by treachery, he agreed with his collaborators inside the city on a day when Aratus was to come by night to the river flowing from Cynaetha and wait there quietly with his force.

The men inside, about midday when they got the chance, were to send one of their number out through the gate quietly, wrapped in a cloak, and tell him to stand before the city on the tomb that had been agreed upon as the signal.

The rest were to lay hands on the officials who, around midday, when they were asleep, were accustomed to guard the gate.

Once that was done, the Achaeans were to hurry out from their ambush and make straight for the gate.

When all had been arranged and the time came, Aratus arrived and remained hidden by the river, waiting for the signal.

But about the fifth hour a man who kept fine sheep near the city happened to need to ask a shepherd some ordinary question. He came out through the gate wearing a cloak and stood on the very same tomb, looking around for the shepherd.

Aratus and his men thought the agreed signal had been given and all rushed toward the city in haste.

But the gate was quickly shut by the guards, since the men inside had not yet made any preparations. So Aratus not only failed in the attempt, but brought the gravest misfortunes upon those within who were helping him: for once they were exposed, they were immediately seized and put to death.

What, then, was the cause of this disaster? It was that the general had made the signal too simple, being still young and inexperienced in the precision provided by double signals and counter-signals.

So narrow is the margin by which military operations incline toward success or failure.

## Book 15 Chapter 12: The Battle of Zama Begins

Once everything on both sides was ready for battle, and the Numidian cavalry had already been skirmishing with one another for some time, Hannibal gave the order for the elephant-drivers to charge the enemy.

But when the trumpets and horns sounded all around them, some of the beasts were thrown into confusion and wheeled about against the Numidian allies supporting the Carthaginians; and by this Masinissa quickly stripped the Carthaginian left wing of its mounted support.

The rest of the elephants collided with the Roman javelin-men in the space between the battle lines and both inflicted and suffered heavy damage.

At last, terrified, some of them ran off down the lanes between the Roman formations, the Romans receiving them safely, just as their general had planned, while others, veering to the right and being showered with missiles from the cavalry, were finally driven out beyond the ground between the camps.

At that moment Laelius too, taking advantage of the confusion caused by the elephants, charged and forced the Carthaginian cavalry into headlong flight.

He pursued the fugitives vigorously, and Masinissa did the same.

Meanwhile the infantry lines on both sides advanced upon one another at a measured pace and with proud confidence—all except the troops who had come from Italy with Hannibal, who held their original position.

When the opposing lines came close, the Romans raised their customary battle cry, beat their swords against their shields, and charged.

But the Carthaginian mercenaries raised a confused and discordant clamor, for, as the poet says, “their cry was not one and the same, nor was their speech one,” since they were men of many peoples and many tongues, just as I listed a moment ago.
