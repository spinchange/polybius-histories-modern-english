## Book 7 Chapter 3: Hieronymus Rebuffs the Roman Envoys

At this same time the Roman commander stationed at Lilybaeum learned what was happening and sent envoys to Hieronymus to renew the treaty that had been made with his ancestors.

But Hieronymus, already leaning strongly toward the Carthaginians, said that he pitied the Romans because wicked men had perished miserably in the battles in Italy at Carthaginian hands.

The envoys were shocked by such insolence, but they still asked who had told him such things. He pointed to the Carthaginian envoys who were present and told the Romans to expose them if they happened to be lying.

They replied that it was not their custom to trust enemies, and they urged him to do nothing contrary to the treaty, since that would be both just and greatly to his advantage. He said he would deliberate and later make his answer clear to them.

But he asked them how it had happened that, before his grandfather’s death, they had sailed with fifty ships as far as Pachynus and then turned back again.

What had happened was that the Romans had shortly before heard that Hiero was dead, and had feared that some disturbance might arise in Syracuse because people would despise the age of the boy left behind.

So they admitted that they had made the voyage in order to support his youth and help preserve his rule, but that when they learned his grandfather was still alive, they had sailed back.

At this the young king answered again, “Then allow me as well, Romans, to preserve my rule now by turning back toward Carthaginian hopes.”

The Romans understood the direction of his policy. For the moment they said nothing and returned, reported what had been said to the man who had sent them, and from then on kept watch on Hieronymus as an enemy.

## Book 12 Chapter 15: Timaeus Blinded by Personal Malignity

For neither do I approve of his abuse against Agathocles, even if Agathocles was the most impious of all men. I mean the passages in which, at the close of his whole history, he says that Agathocles in his youth was a common prostitute, available to the most unrestrained, a jackdaw, a buzzard, offering his backside to the front of all who wished.

In addition to this, he says that when Agathocles died, his wife, weeping over him, lamented in this way: “What did I not do for you? What did you not do for me?”

In the face of these passages too one might not only repeat what was said about Demochares, but also marvel at the extravagance of his bitterness.

For that Agathocles must by nature have possessed great advantages is clear from the very things that Timaeus himself declares.

For if he came to Syracuse fleeing the potter’s wheel, the smoke, and the clay, at about eighteen years of age, and after some time, setting out from such a starting point, became master of all Sicily, brought the Carthaginians into the gravest dangers, and finally, having grown old in power, ended his life bearing the title of king—does it not follow that Agathocles must have been some great and marvelous figure, and that he possessed many resources and capacities for practical affairs?

Concerning these things the historian ought to relate to posterity not only what tends to slander and accusation, but also what redounds to the man’s praise; for this is the proper task of history.

But Timaeus, blinded by his own bitterness, has reported his faults to us spitefully and with exaggeration, while he has omitted his successes altogether, not realizing that falsehood is no less present in those who in their histories pass over in silence what actually happened than in those who misstate it.

As for me, I have refrained from adding further measure for the sake of dwelling on his hatefulness, but I have not left out what was germane to my own purpose. —

## Book 16 Chapter 3: Incidents of the Sea-Fight

The fighting began from the ship on which Attalus sailed, and at once all the nearby ships engaged each other without waiting for orders.

Attalus ran into an enemy eight-banked ship and struck it a well-placed blow below the waterline. After a long struggle among the men on deck, he finally sank it.

Philip’s ten-banked flagship, however, fell into the enemy’s hands in a most unexpected way.

A triemiolia slipped in beneath it and crashed violently into its hull amidships, below the upper bank of oars, where it became jammed fast. The helmsman could no longer recover control of the larger ship.

Because that smaller craft hung attached to it, the flagship became unwieldy and slow in every movement. At that moment two quinqueremes came up, struck it on both sides, and destroyed both the vessel and the marines on board, among them Democrates, Philip’s admiral.

At the same time Dionysodorus and Deinocrates, brothers who served as admirals under Attalus, charged enemy ships, the one a seven-banker and the other an eight-banker, and fought with remarkable daring.

Deinocrates, colliding with an eight-banked ship, received a blow above the waterline because the enemy vessel had a high-built prow, but he struck the enemy below the waterline. At first he could not break free, though he repeatedly tried to back away.

So, while the Macedonians fought bravely from the enemy ship, he was brought into extreme danger.

But Attalus came to his aid and, by driving into the enemy vessel, broke the two ships apart. Deinocrates was unexpectedly freed, while all the marines on the enemy ship, after a gallant resistance, were killed, and their now-empty vessel fell into Attalus’ hands.

Dionysodorus, on the other hand, made his attack with great force but missed the ramming blow. As he passed close by the enemy, he lost the whole right-side bank of oars, and the timbers that carried his towers were smashed together.

Once this happened, the enemy surrounded him on every side.

In the uproar and confusion that followed, all the rest of his marines were lost with the ship, but Dionysodorus himself, with only two others, managed to swim off to the triemiolia that was coming to help him.

## Book 21 Chapter 34: Moagetes of Cibyra

Moagetes was tyrant of Cibyra, a cruel and treacherous man, and he deserves remembrance not in passing, but with deliberate notice worthy of his character.

When the Roman consul Gnaeus approached Cibyra and Helvius had been sent ahead to discover what attitude he meant to take, Moagetes sent envoys begging that the countryside not be ravaged, since he was a friend of the Romans and would do whatever was ordered.

At the same time he also offered a crown worth fifteen talents.

On hearing this, Helvius said he would refrain from ravaging the territory, but told him to negotiate with the commander himself about the whole matter, since he was following close behind with the army.

After this Moagetes sent not only envoys but also his own brother along with them. When Gnaeus met them on the march, he spoke to the envoys in a strained and bitter tone, saying that Moagetes was not only the least friendly to Rome of all the dynasts in Asia, but that in his whole strength and conduct he deserved to be stripped of power and punished.

Terrified by this display of anger, the envoys gave up the rest of their instructions and merely asked him to grant an interview.

When he consented, they returned that day to Cibyra.

On the next day the tyrant came out with his friends, plainly and humbly dressed, without pomp, lamenting in his speech his own helplessness and the weakness of the cities he ruled, and asking Gnaeus to accept the fifteen talents.

For he ruled Cibyra, Syleium, and the city in the Marsh.

Gnaeus, astonished at his shamelessness, said nothing else to him except that if he did not gladly pay five hundred talents, he would not merely ravage the countryside, but would besiege and plunder the city itself.

Then Moagetes, terrified at what was coming, begged him not to do anything of the kind. Little by little he kept increasing the money offered, and in the end persuaded Gnaeus, after taking one hundred talents and ten thousand medimni of wheat, to admit him to friendship.

Aristaenus used such arguments as these before the Achaeans in defense of his own policy. He said that it was impossible, while holding out both the spear and the herald’s staff at once, to preserve friendship with Rome.

## Book 29 Chapter 12: On Proportion in History

[...] Again, other writers on the Syrian war. The reason for this is what I have already explained more than once.

For when men take a simple and straightforward subject and yet want not the facts but the bulk of their books to make them seem like historians, and want that sort of display to create the impression of importance, it becomes necessary for them to make small things big, and to dress up matters that were briefly stated, and in some cases to turn incidental happenings into full-scale actions and affairs—staging contests and announcing battle lines in which sometimes ten infantrymen fell, sometimes only a little more, and cavalry even fewer than that.

As for sieges, geography, and similar topics, no one could adequately describe how far they work these up, given their lack of real material.

But the opposite is true for those who write universal history: and so one should not blame us as if we were dragging out events when we sometimes pass over things that others have given great attention and embellishment, and sometimes mention them only briefly; rather, one should trust that we give each subject the treatment it deserves.

For when those writers describe, somewhere in the course of their whole work, the siege of Phanoteia or Coroneia or Haliartus, they are forced to set out every contrivance, every bold move, and every detail of the siege, and beyond that [...] to dwell on the capture of Tarentum, Corinth, Sardis, Gaza, Bactra, and, above all, Carthage, adding details of their own; yet they are not at all satisfied if we give a plain and truthful account of such matters.

The same judgment should apply to our treatment of battles and speeches, and likewise to the other parts of history: in all these matters we would fairly deserve much forgiveness, and the same is true of what I am about to narrate, for instance if [...] I should seem to use the same material, the same handling of events, or the same expressions; and also if I should anywhere make slips in the names of mountains or rivers or in the particular features of places. The size of the undertaking itself is excuse enough in all these matters, unless I am caught deliberately making false statements for gain; for that I ask no pardon, as I have repeatedly and expressly said in this work.
