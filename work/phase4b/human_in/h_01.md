## Book 5 Chapter 1: The Situation in the Summer of 218 BC

The year of office held by the younger Aratus had reached its end about the rising of the Pleiades; for that was how the Achaean people reckoned time in those days.

So he laid down his office, and Eperatus took over the leadership of the Achaeans. Dorimachus was general of the Aetolians.

At the same time, as summer was beginning, Hannibal, now openly committed to war against Rome, set out from New Carthage, crossed the Ebro River, and began his advance and march into Italy.

The Romans, meanwhile, were sending Tiberius Sempronius to Libya with a force, and Publius Cornelius to Iberia.

Antiochus and Ptolemy, having given up on embassies and on settling their dispute over Coele-Syria by negotiation, began making war on one another.

King Philip, being short of grain and money for his forces, summoned the Achaeans to an assembly through their magistrates.

When the people had gathered at Aegium according to law, he saw that Aratus and his supporters were holding back out of resentment because of the mischief worked against them by Apelles and his circle during the elections, while Eperatus was by nature ineffective and held in contempt by everyone.

Reflecting on what had already happened, and on the poor judgment of Apelles and Leontius, he decided once again to align himself with Aratus and his party.

So he persuaded the magistrates to transfer the assembly to Sicyon. Then he took both the elder Aratus and the younger Aratus aside, placed all the blame for what had happened on Apelles, and urged them to remain committed to the policy they had adopted from the beginning.

They readily agreed. Philip then entered the Achaean assembly and, with the help of the men just mentioned, secured every measure needed for his purpose.

It was decreed that the Achaeans should give him fifty talents at once for his first mobilization, provide three months’ pay for the army, and add ten thousand measures of grain.

After that, for as long as he remained in the Peloponnese and campaigned as their ally, he was to receive seventeen talents each month from the Achaeans.

## Book 8 Chapter 17: Bolis the Cretan Agrees to Rescue Achaeus

At the first opportunity Cambylus brought the proposal before the king.

Antiochus found the offer both agreeable and astonishing. Partly overjoyed, he promised everything; partly distrustful, he examined in detail the several plans and preparations.

Afterward, when he had become convinced and thought that the undertaking seemed to be advancing with divine favor, he urgently begged Cambylus again and again to carry it through.

Bolis acted in a similar way toward Nicomachus and Melancomas. Believing that the attempt rested on the strongest assurances, they at once prepared letters to Achaeus written in cipher, as was their custom, in such a way that anyone who got hold of the letter would be unable to understand any of what was written in it; and they sent them, urging him to trust Bolis and Cambylus.

Arianus, by means of Cambylus, entered the citadel and delivered what had been written to Achaeus and his circle. Since he had been present from the start, he gave an exact account of every particular, even under repeated and varied questioning about Sosibius and Bolis, about Nicomachus and Melancomas, and most of all about Cambylus.

Yet he endured the examination with natural confidence and resolution, chiefly because he did not know the essential point of the scheme that Bolis and Cambylus had actually agreed upon.

Achaeus, convinced both by Arianus’ answers and especially by the ciphers sent from Nicomachus and Melancomas, replied and immediately sent Arianus back again.

After this had happened several times between the two sides, Achaeus and his friends at last entrusted themselves to Nicomachus’ group, since absolutely no other hope of safety remained, and told them to send Bolis along with Arianus on a moonless night, as they were prepared to place themselves in their hands.

For Achaeus had formed a plan somewhat like this: first to escape the danger immediately around him, and afterward to make his move, by a circuitous route, toward Syria.

He had great hopes that if he appeared suddenly and unexpectedly among the people of Syria while Antiochus was still occupied around Sardis, he would stir up a great movement and receive a warm welcome from the people of Antioch, Coele-Syria, and Phoenicia.

The herdsmen cannot follow alongside their flocks at pasture, because the island is wooded, precipitous, and rugged; but whenever they wish to gather them, they take their stand at convenient spots and call the animals together with a trumpet, and every one of them runs unfailingly to its own trumpet.

But when the herdsman, seeing the men disembark, sounds his trumpet, the animals at once rush headlong and run together to the trumpet. This gives the impression of wildness—about which Timaeus, having inquired poorly and perfunctorily, made a slapdash report.

Their obedience to the trumpet is nothing to wonder at: for in Italy too those who raise swine manage the pasturing in the same way.

The swineherds do not follow at the heels of the beasts, as among the Greeks, but go on ahead sounding the horn at intervals, while the beasts follow behind and run together toward the sound; and so great a familiarity develops in the animals toward their own horn that those who hear of it for the first time are amazed and can scarcely believe it.

For owing to the abundance of labor and the other resources available, the droves of swine in Italy turn out to be enormous—especially along the coast, among the Etruscans and the Gauls—so that a single breeding sow rears a thousand pigs, and sometimes even more.

For this reason they make their drives out from the night-pens according to litters and ages.

Hence, when several droves are brought forward to the same place, they cannot keep them sorted by litter, but they get mixed up with one another both during the drives out and at pasture, and likewise during the drives home.

From these circumstances the device of the horn has been contrived by them for separating the animals, whenever they get mixed together, without toil or trouble.

For when one of the herdsmen goes off toward one quarter sounding his horn, and another turns aside toward another, the beasts separate of their own accord and follow their own horns with such eagerness that it is impossible by any means to force or to check their rush.

But among the Greeks, in the oak-forests, when the herds get mixed together while chasing the mast, the man who has more hands and seizes the opportunity gathers up his neighbor’s beasts along with his own and drives them off.

And sometimes a thief, lying in wait, drives them off, without the herdsman in charge even realizing how he lost them—because the animals stray far from their drivers in their scramble for the mast, just when it is beginning to fall. But enough of this.

## Book 21 Chapter 24: The Decision of the Senate

After this the senate introduced Antiochus’s envoys, Antipater and Zeuxis.

They spoke with dignity and earnest appeal, and the senate approved the agreements already made with Scipio in Asia.

A few days later the people ratified them as well, and oaths were sworn on these terms with Antipater and his colleagues.

After that they also introduced the rest of the envoys who were present from Asia; they listened only briefly, but gave them all the same answer: that they were sending ten commissioners to decide all disputed questions among the cities.

Having given this answer, they then appointed the ten commissioners, entrusting them with the details.

As for the main principles, they themselves decided that of the peoples dwelling on this side of Taurus, all who had formerly been subject to Antiochus should be given to Eumenes, except Lycia and Caria as far as the Maeander, which should belong to the Rhodians.

And among the Greek cities, those that had paid tribute to Attalus should continue to pay it to Eumenes, while only those that had paid tribute to Antiochus should have that tribute remitted.

Having laid down these outlines for the general settlement, they sent the ten commissioners out to Asia to join the consul Gnaeus.

After these arrangements had already been made, the Rhodians came again before the senate concerning Soli in Cilicia. Because of kinship, they said, it was fitting for them to care for that city.

For the people of Soli, like the Rhodians, were colonists from Argos, and on that basis they proved the kinship between the two peoples to be brotherly.

Therefore they said it was just that the people of Soli should receive freedom from the Romans through Rhodian good offices.

When the senate heard this, it summoned Antiochus’s envoys and at first ordered that Antiochus should give up all Cilicia. But when Antipater and his party refused, on the ground that this went beyond the treaty, the senate turned again to the case of Soli by itself.

Since the king’s envoys pressed their opposition strongly, the senate dismissed them, called in the Rhodians, explained the objections raised by Antipater’s party, and added that it would endure anything if the Rhodians had firmly resolved on this point.

But the Rhodian envoys, pleased by the zeal of the senate, said they would ask for nothing further; so the matter remained where it stood.

Then, just as the ten commissioners and the other envoys were about to set out, the Scipios and Lucius—the victors over Antiochus in the naval battle—sailed into Brundisium from Italy.

After some days they entered Rome and celebrated triumphs.

## Book 27 Chapter 7: Rhodes Debates Sending Ships to Rome

While Gaius was still lying at anchor off Cephallenia, he sent the Rhodians a letter about dispatching ships, entrusting the letter to a certain athletic trainer named Socrates.

When the letter reached Rhodes, Stratocles was serving as prytanis in the second half-year of his term.

When the matter was brought forward for debate, Agathagetes, Rhodophon, Astymedes, and many others thought the ships should be sent and that they should join the war at once from the beginning, without making any pretext.

But Deinon, Polyaratus, and their party, though already displeased by the friendly attitude that had been shown toward the Romans, now put forward King Eumenes as a pretext and began to corrupt the inclination of the masses.

For the Rhodians had long felt suspicion and hostility toward Eumenes, first dating from the war against Pharnaces, when Eumenes was blockading the mouth of the Hellespont to keep ships from sailing into the Black Sea, and the Rhodians intervened and stopped him, and then, in more recent times, the quarrel had been stirred up again by the Lycian disputes over certain forts and a tract of land that lay on the border of the Rhodian Peraea and was continually being ravaged by troops under Eumenes.

Because of all this they were ready enough to listen to anything said against the king.

So Deinon’s party seized on this opportunity and tried to discredit the letter, saying that it had come not from the Romans but from Eumenes, who wanted by every possible means to drag them into the war and saddle the people with needless expense and suffering.

As proof of what they said, they pointed to the fact that the man who had brought the letter was just such a person as this, whereas the Romans were not accustomed to act in that way, but sent communications of this kind with great seriousness and official protection.

They said this even though they knew perfectly well that the letter had in fact been written by Lucretius, because they wanted to teach the masses not to do anything readily for the Romans, but in every matter to be difficult and to provide occasions for suspicion and resentment.

For their purpose was to estrange the people from their goodwill toward Rome and, so far as they could, entangle them in friendship with Perseus.

These men were devoted to that course because Polyaratus, being more boastful and vain, had burdened his estate with debt, while Deinon, being greedy and reckless, had from the beginning been drawn to the profits that came from powerful lords and kings.

At this point Stratocles the prytanis rose and, after speaking at length against Perseus and much in favor of the Romans, urged the majority to ratify the decree for dispatching the ships.

They immediately made ready six quadriremes, sent five of them to Chalcis with Timagoras appointed to command them, and the remaining one to Tenedos under a commander named Timagoras.

When he reached Tenedos, he came upon Diophanes, who had been sent by Perseus to Antiochus; he did not get possession of Diophanes himself, but he did capture his crew.

Lucretius received all the allies who had come by sea with kindness and then released them from this service, saying that the situation did not require naval assistance.

## Book 29 Chapter 4: Gentius Joins Perseus

So all these men proceeded to Macedonia. But Pantauchus stayed at Gentius’ side and kept reminding and pressing the young king not to delay his preparations, but, while ready, to seize positions, cities, and allies in advance; above all he urged him to prepare for a struggle at sea.

For since the Romans were completely unprepared in this sphere, both on the coasts of Epirus and in Illyria, everything proposed would be accomplished without a fight by Gentius himself and the forces he sent out.

So Gentius, persuaded by these arguments, busied himself with preparations both by land and by sea.

Perseus, meanwhile, when the envoys and hostages from Gentius arrived in Macedonia, set out from the camp by the river Elpeius with all his cavalry and went to meet them at Dion, and when he met them he first gave the oaths for the alliance in the presence of all the cavalry. For he greatly wished the Macedonians to know clearly of Gentius’ cooperation, hoping that with this additional advantage they would be more confident.

Next he received the hostages and handed over his own to Olympion and his companions. The most distinguished among them were Limnaeus son of Polemocrates and Balacrus son of Pantauchus.

After this he sent those who had come for the money on to Pella to receive it there, while he dispatched the envoys bound for Rhodes to Thessalonica, to Metrodorus, instructing them to hold themselves ready for the voyage. That embassy also succeeded in persuading the Rhodians to join the war.

After arranging these matters, he sent Herophon as envoy to Eumenes, a man whom he had employed on such missions before, and Telemnastus the Cretan to Antiochus, telling him not to overlook the opportunity and not to suppose that Roman arrogance and harshness concerned Perseus alone, but to understand clearly that unless he too now took a hand, preferably by ending the war, but if not by helping Perseus, he would soon experience the same fortune himself.

## Book 38 Chapter 21: Fortune’s Instability and the Great Man

[...] The text here is badly broken in the witness, but the sense is that Polybius is completing Scipio’s remark about the danger of overconfidence and the importance of good judgment.

No clearer or more practical lesson than this is easy to state.

For to take account, in the midst of great achievements and the calamities of one’s enemies, of one’s own affairs and of the opposite condition, and in general to keep the instability of Fortune before one’s eyes amid success, is the mark of a great and complete man, and one worthy of remembrance. —
