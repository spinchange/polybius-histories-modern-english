## Book 13 Chapter 9: Antiochus in Arabia, 205–204 BC

Labae, like Sabae, is a city of Chattenia, as Polybius says in his thirteenth book. The ethnic is Labaean, just as from Sabae comes Sabaean. Both belong to the same region, for Chattenia is territory of the Gerrhaeans.

Chattenia is the third district of the Gerrhaeans.

Polybius, Book 13: “Chattenia is otherwise a bleak land, but because of the prosperity of the Gerrhaeans it has been furnished with villages and towers; for they are the ones who occupy it.”

It lies on the Erythraean Sea. The inhabitants are called Chattenians, as he himself says: “For to these men he gave orders to spare the land of the Chattenians.”

The Gerrhaeans asked the king not to destroy what the gods had given them—that is, perpetual peace and freedom. When their letter had been interpreted to him, he said that he granted what they requested.

When freedom had been confirmed for the Gerrhaeans, they immediately honored King Antiochus with gifts of five hundred talents of silver, a thousand of frankincense, and two hundred of the substance called stacte. He then sailed on to the island of Tylos and from there departed for Seleuceia. These aromatic products came from the Erythraean Sea.

## Book 26 Chapter 1: Antiochus Epiphanes

Antiochus, though called Epiphanes, was nicknamed Epimanes from his conduct. Polybius says of him that he would sometimes slip away from the court, eluding his attendants, and then be seen wandering about aimlessly in some quarter of the city with only one or two companions.

Most often he was found at the silversmiths’ and goldsmiths’ shops, chatting and showing his enthusiasm for craftsmanship with the metalworkers and other artisans.

At other times he would mix with ordinary townsmen and converse with anyone he happened to meet, and he would even drink with visiting foreigners of the humblest sort.

If he learned that some young men were feasting together, he would appear among them without any warning, bringing horns and musicians with him in reveling fashion, so that most people, startled by the oddity of it, would get up and run away.

He would often lay aside his royal dress, put on a toga-like robe, and walk about the marketplace canvassing for office, shaking hands with some men and even embracing others as he asked for their vote, sometimes as though he were seeking the aedileship and sometimes the tribunate.

And when he had won the office, he would sit down on the ivory curule chair in the Roman manner, hear the business cases conducted in the marketplace, and decide them with great seriousness and eagerness. ## Book 39 Chapter 8: Polybius Recapitulates His Whole Plan

Polybius says near the end of his work that after we had brought these matters to a conclusion, we returned from Rome, as though we had already completed the chief points of what went before, and were worthy of the gratitude due to Roman goodwill.
