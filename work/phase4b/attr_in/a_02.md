## Book 7 Chapter 11: Philip Dissuaded from Taking Messene

For the present, after bringing my narrative to a pause, I want to speak briefly about Philip, because this was the beginning of his reversal and of his movement and change for the worse.

It seems to me that for practical men who wish, even little by little, to improve themselves from the lessons of history, this offers a very vivid example.

For both because the beginning of his career was so conspicuous and because his natural gifts were so brilliant, the impulses of this king in either direction became especially clear and widely known to all the Greeks, and the consequences that followed each set of impulses likewise stood out plainly by comparison.

## Book 9 Chapter 27: Agrigentum

Let what has been said so far be enough on that subject. The city of the Agrigentines differs from most cities not only in the respects already mentioned, but also in strength, and especially in beauty and construction.

It is built eighteen stades from the sea, so that it lacks none of the advantages the sea provides.

Its circuit of walls is exceptionally secure both by nature and by art.

For the wall stands on a rock that is steep and broken all around, in some parts naturally so and in others made so by human labor, and the city is enclosed by rivers.

Along its southern side runs the river that bears the same name as the city, and along the side facing west and the southwest runs the river called Hypsas.

The citadel of the city lies above it toward the summer sunrise, enclosed on the outer side by an inaccessible ravine, and on the inner side having only one approach from the city.

On its summit stand a sanctuary of Athena and one of Zeus Atabyrius, as at Rhodes.

For since Acragas was colonized by Rhodians, it is natural that this god should bear the same title there as among the Rhodians.

The rest of the city too is splendidly adorned with temples and colonnades. And though the temple of Olympian Zeus has not been completely finished, in conception and size it seems inferior to none of those in Greece.

Polybius says that both the river and the city were called Acragas from the fertility of the land.

## Book 11 Chapter 18a: The Value of Analyzing Causes

*As Polybius says,* what use is it for readers to go through wars and battles, the enslavement of cities, and sieges, if they do not understand the causes by which in each case some succeeded and others failed?

For the outcomes of actions merely entertain those who hear of them, whereas the analysis of the plans formed beforehand, when properly examined, benefits those who love learning.

And above all, a detailed presentation of the handling of each event helps to correct the judgment of attentive readers.

The prytaneis, already distrustful of Philip because of his misconduct in Cretan affairs, also suspected that Heracleides was an agent planted among them.

But when he came before them, he explained the reasons for which he had fled from Philip.

For, he said, Philip would have been willing to undertake anything rather than let his intentions in these matters become apparent to the Rhodians. And by this means he cleared Heracleides of suspicion.

It seems to me that nature has shown truth to be the mightiest of divinities for mankind and has added to it the greatest power.

## Book 16 Chapter 14: Digression on the Merits of the Historians Zeno and Antisthenes of Rhodes

Polybius says that since some historians of particular episodes have written about this same period, in which the events at Messene and the sea-battles already mentioned took place, he wishes to discuss them briefly.

He will not speak of them all, but only of those whom he thinks worthy of remembrance and distinction, namely Zeno and Antisthenes of Rhodes.

I judge them worthy of notice for several reasons. They were contemporaries of the events, they had practical political experience, and in general they composed their histories not for profit but for reputation and from a sense of duty proper to public men.

Since they write about the same events that I do, I must not pass them over in silence, lest, because of the name of their country and the idea that naval matters especially belong to the Rhodians, readers eager to learn should follow them rather than me wherever I disagree with them.

Now to begin with, both of them describe the battle off Lade as no less serious than the battle off Chios, indeed as more active and more daring both in detail and in the overall conduct of the engagement, and they declare that the victory belonged to the Rhodians.

## Book 22 Chapter 19: Philopoemen Praises Archon’s Cunning

Philopoemen had some disagreement in argument with Archon the general. Philopoemen, however, being pleased at the moment by what had been said and changing his mind, praised Archon warmly as a man who had handled the occasion with both skill and cunning.

But for my own part, *says Polybius,* the remark did not please me then when I was present, since it amounted to praising a man while at the same time doing him moral harm; nor has it pleased me since, as my years have advanced.

For it seems to me that there is a very great difference in moral choice between a practical statesman and a mischievous intriguer, just as there is a similar difference between genuine dexterity and mere clever roguery. The one set of qualities is, so to speak, among the noblest of human things; the other is the very opposite.

But because of the lack of judgment now common among men, the things just mentioned, though they have very little in common, receive the same approval and the same eager admiration.
