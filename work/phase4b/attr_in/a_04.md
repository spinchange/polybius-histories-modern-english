## Book 8 Chapter 10: Philip Devastates Messene

Who would not reject such bitterness and such reckless abuse on the part of a historian?

For he deserves censure not only because he says things inconsistent with his own purpose, but also because he has slandered both the king and his friends, and most of all because he has framed the slander in language at once disgraceful and indecent.

If he had been speaking about Sardanapalus or his circle, he would scarcely have dared to use language so foul—and the kind of life and wantonness that man pursued we infer from the inscription on his tomb.

For the inscription says:

These are the things I possess: what I ate, what insolence I indulged in, and the pleasures I enjoyed in love.

But when speaking of Philip and his friends, one should be on guard not against accusing them of softness and cowardice, or even shamelessness, but rather lest, in trying to praise them, one should fail to speak worthily of their courage, hardiness, and, in a word, their excellence.

For it was plainly by their own hard work and daring that they turned the Macedonian kingdom from a very small one into the most renowned and greatest power of its day.

And quite apart from Philip’s own achievements, what they accomplished after his death under Alexander has handed down to all men a reputation for excellence that is universally acknowledged.

## Book 14 Chapter 11: Women at Alexandria

Polybius says in his fourteenth book that Philo became the flatterer of Agathocles, son of Oenanthe and companion of King Philopator.

Polybius also says in the fourteenth book of the Histories that many statues of Cleino, who served Ptolemy Philadelphus as his wine-pourer, were set up throughout Alexandria, dressed in a single tunic and holding a drinking horn in their hands.

When King Antiochus sacked the city of the Gazans, Polybius says:

“It seems to me both just and fitting to give the people of Gaza the testimony they deserve.

## Book 21 Chapter 38: A Galatian Woman Takes Vengeance

Chiomara, the wife of Ortiagon, was among the prisoners taken together with the other women when Gnaeus and the Romans defeated the Galatians in Asia.

The centurion who took her used his opportunity in a soldier’s fashion and violated her.

He was a man enslaved alike to pleasure and to money; yet greed finally got the better of him, and when a large amount of gold was agreed upon as ransom for the woman, he led her away to release her, with a river lying between the two sides.

When the Galatians had crossed, paid him the gold, and were taking back Chiomara, she signaled with a nod that one of them should strike down the Roman as he embraced her and was taking leave with affected courtesy.

The man obeyed, cut off his head, and she picked it up, wrapped it in her folds, and rode away.

Polybius says that he himself met and spoke with this woman at Sardis and admired both her spirit and her intelligence.
