## Book 7 Chapter 1: Capua and Petelia

*Quoted by Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae.*

Polybius says in the seventh book that the people of Capua in Campania, made wealthy by the excellence of their land, drifted into luxury and extravagance, surpassing even the famous reputation of Croton and Sybaris.

So, he says, being unable to bear their present prosperity, they called in Hannibal. For that reason they later suffered irreparable disasters at Roman hands.

The people of Petelia, on the other hand, kept faith with Rome and showed such endurance under Hannibal’s siege that, after eating all the leather in the city and consuming the bark and tender shoots of every tree in it, and after holding out for eleven months with no help arriving, they finally surrendered with the Romans’ consent.

Capua, after going over to the Carthaginians, used its influence to draw the other cities over as well.

## Book 15 Chapter 22: Philip Rouses the Enmity of the Greeks

When Philip had gained possession of the city, he was overjoyed, as if he had accomplished some noble and splendid deed, having zealously aided his brother-in-law, overawed all who resisted him, and gained an abundance of bodies and money by what he imagined was just warfare.

Affairs in Greece.

Polybius says that he rose and went on ahead, weighed down partly by sickness and partly by age, for he was in his seventieth year.

It seems to me that the common proverb is right: a human being may meet with success, but to be successful to the very end is impossible.

Therefore we should call the men of old fortunate, not as though any of them enjoyed unbroken good fortune—for why should we vainly flatter Fortune with a false phrase?— but rather those who have had her favorable for the longest part of life, and who, when she does change her mind, encounter only moderate disasters. —

## Book 30 Chapter 11: Aetolia Sinks into Lawless Violence

The Aetolians had long been accustomed to make their living from plunder and other such lawless practices.

And as long as they were free to prey upon and loot the Greeks, they drew their livelihood from that, regarding every land as enemy territory.

But afterward, when the Romans took control of affairs and they were cut off from support from outside, they turned upon one another.

Even before this, in their civil war, there was no sort of outrage they did not commit.

And not long before, after having tasted one another’s blood in the massacres at Arsinoe, they were ready for anything, their minds so brutalized that they would not even allow their leaders to offer advice.

That is why conditions in Aetolia were full of disorder, lawlessness, and murder, and nothing they did was carried out with calculation or settled purpose. Everything was done blindly and in confusion, as if some violent storm had burst upon them.

## Book 31 Chapter 18: Ptolemy Marches on Cyrene and Is Defeated

The party with Torquatus, after being conveyed to Alexandria, tried to persuade the elder Ptolemy to come to terms with his brother and yield Cyprus to him.

But Ptolemy partly made promises and partly ignored them, and by this method simply dragged out the time.

Meanwhile the younger Ptolemy was encamped with the Cretans in Libya near Apis, in accordance with the arrangement, and was thoroughly irritated that nothing had been made clear. At first he sent Gnaeus to Alexandria, hoping that through him he might at least get matters moving with Torquatus and his party.

But when he too was treated just like the others, and time kept being stretched out, and forty days passed without any result, Ptolemy fell into uncertainty about the whole situation.

For the elder king, by offering every kind of courtesy, had won over the envoys one by one and kept most of them there, not of their own choice but against their will.

At this same time news reached the younger Ptolemy that the Cyrenaeans had revolted, that the cities were siding with them, and that a certain Ptolemy called Sympetesis had also joined in the revolt.

He was an Egyptian by birth and had been entrusted by the king with general supervision of affairs at the time when Ptolemy sailed to Rome.
