For after proposing to write about a king exceptionally endowed by nature for virtue, he omits none of the shameful or dreadful charges he can find.

The result is that the historian must necessarily appear either a liar and flatterer in his opening and statement of purpose, or else childish and foolish in his treatment of particulars, if he thought that by irrational and borrowed abuse he would make himself seem more credible and his praises of Philip more acceptable.

Nor indeed would anyone approve even the broad design of this writer, since he set out to write Greek history from the point at which Thucydides ended, and when he had drawn near to the age of Leuctra and to the most brilliant achievements of Greece, he threw Greece and its deeds aside and instead proposed to write the acts of Philip.

## Book 12 Chapter 26c: Love of Paradox Brings Ridicule

The upshot is that, through his excessive love of paradox, he ends up bringing the very men and deeds he wants to champion not into favorable comparison but into ridicule; and he falls into almost the same plight as those students in the Academy who have trained themselves in the most facile kind of argument.

For some of these men, wanting to drive those they debate with into perplexity—both about things that seem plainly knowable and about things that are genuinely unknowable—resort to such paradoxes and have such a ready stock of plausibilities that they make one wonder whether it is possible for people in Athens to smell eggs being boiled in Ephesus, and to doubt whether perhaps, at the very moment they are discussing these matters in the Academy, they are not awake at all but lying at home asleep, framing these arguments in a dream.

As a result, through their excessive love of paradox, they have brought the whole school into disrepute, so that even the questions they raise legitimately have come to be disbelieved by people.

And quite apart from their own misjudgment, they have bred in the young such a passion for this sort of thing that it comes about that they give not even a passing thought to ethical and practical arguments—the very ones from which philosophers derive real benefit—but instead waste their lives chasing empty glory in useless and paradoxical feats of argumentation.

## Book 14 Chapter 1a: Preface

*Polybius says of himself and of the plan of his books:* perhaps, then, the summaries prefixed to each Olympiad bring readers to understanding both because of the number and because of the greatness of the events, since deeds from the whole inhabited world are being brought under one general view.

Yet I think the events of this Olympiad especially will make an impression on readers, first because in this period the wars in Italy and Libya reached their conclusion. And who would not wish to learn what sort of outcome and ending they had?

For by nature all people, even if they take in the particular actions and speeches only in outline, nevertheless long to know the end of each story.

Besides this, it happened that at the same time the intentions of the kings became especially plain. For what had previously been said about them as rumor was then clearly recognized by everyone, even by those who had no desire at all to pry into public affairs.

Therefore, wishing to make the exposition proportionate to the importance of the events, I have not arranged the actions of those two years into a single book, as I did in the earlier parts of the work.

## Book 20 Chapter 4: The Decline of Boeotia

The Boeotians had for a long time now been in a poor condition, one that stood in stark contrast to the former vigor and renown of their state.

For these people, having won great glory and great power in the days of the battle of Leuctra, somehow—I cannot say how—steadily kept losing both of the qualities just mentioned in the years that followed, under the leadership of Abaeocritus.

And from that time on they not only kept losing them, but, turning altogether to the opposite course, they obscured their earlier reputation as far as they were able.

For when the Achaeans had stirred them up to war against the Aetolians, they adopted the same policy as the Achaeans and formed an alliance with them, and thereafter waged continual war against the Aetolians.

But when the Aetolians invaded Boeotia with an army, the Boeotians marched out in full force, and—although the Achaeans had assembled and were about to come to their aid—they did not wait for their arrival but engaged the Aetolians.

Being defeated in the battle, they were so crushed in spirit that, from that campaign on, they no longer dared to lay claim to any honorable distinction at all, nor did they share with the other Greeks in any common undertaking or contest decided by joint resolution.

Instead, having given themselves over to feasting and drunkenness, they grew slack not only in their bodies but in their souls as well.

## Book 21 Chapter 6: Asia: Factions at Phocaea

Affairs in Asia.

At Phocaea there was civil strife, partly because the Romans left on board the ships were quartered there, and partly because the people resented the orders laid upon them.

## Book 30 Chapter 6: Greek States and the War With Perseus

Polybius says: I have already called my readers’ attention to the conduct of Deinon and Polyaratus.

For the crisis and the reversal were serious not only at Rhodes, but in almost every Greek state.

So it would be useful to examine the intentions of the politicians in each place and determine which of them acted rationally and which failed in their duty, so that those who come after us, with these cases set before them like models, may in similar emergencies truly pursue what is worth choosing and avoid what is to be shunned, and may not, by failing to see what is fitting at the final moment of life, suffer the same fate in their last hour as in the actions of their earlier career.

Now it turned out that there were three distinct classes among those who came under accusation because of the war with Perseus.

One class consisted of men who were not pleased to see the whole issue decided and the rule of the inhabited world falling under a single power, but who nevertheless neither helped nor opposed the Romans in any definite way, instead leaving the outcome to Fortune.

Another class consisted of those who were glad to see the struggle coming to a decision and wanted Perseus to win, but were unable to draw either their own fellow citizens or men of their own people over to their view.

A third class consisted of those who did draw their states with them and turned their constitutions over to the alliance of Perseus

## Book 36 Chapter 17: Fortune, Causes, and Greek Depopulation

Polybius says: in criticizing those who attribute events, whether public affairs or private reversals, to Fortune and Fate, I now wish to distinguish this matter as far as the method of factual history allows.

Where a human being cannot, God knows, discover the causes because they are either impossible or extremely difficult to grasp, one may perhaps have recourse to God and Fortune—for example, in the case of extraordinary and continuous rains or snowstorms, or, by contrast, droughts and frosts that ruin the crops, or similarly with recurring pestilences and other things like these, whose cause is not easy to discover.

Therefore, in such matters, since we cannot understand them, it is reasonable to follow the common opinion and, by prayer and sacrifice, seek to appease the divine and ask the gods what words or actions will be best for us and may bring an end to the evils that afflict us.

But where it is possible to discover the cause from which and through which the event arose, I do not think one should refer such matters to the divine.
