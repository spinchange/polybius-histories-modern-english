The floggers were standing there all but ready to bring down their whips, while others at his feet were preparing the instruments of compulsion.

Moeragenes said that what was chiefly needed was only someone to take the lead. When the Macedonians heard this, they were stirred to anger, and in the end they were persuaded by him. They immediately went first to the Macedonian tents and then to those of the other soldiers.

All these camps lay together, facing one side of the city.

Since the impulse had long been ready in the minds of the crowd and needed only someone to call it forth and dare the first move, the affair, once begun, blazed up like fire.

For before four hours had passed, every class, military and civilian alike, had agreed on the attack.

And at this crisis chance too contributed greatly toward the outcome.

For Agathocles received both a letter and the report of spies: the letter, written by Tlepolemus to the troops, declared that he would arrive soon, and the spies reported that he was already there.

This drove Agathocles so completely out of his senses that he gave up both action and even deliberation about the danger confronting him, but at his usual hour went off to drinking and there carried through the gathering in his customary fashion.

His mother Oenanthe, in deep distress, went to the Thesmophorium, the sanctuary being open because of an annual sacrifice.

At first she knelt and made magical appeals to the goddesses; then she sat down by the altar and kept still.

Most of the women, enjoying the sight of her distress and misery, kept silent. But some female relatives of Polycrates and certain other noblewomen, still unaware of the full crisis, approached and tried to comfort her.

She cried out in a loud voice, “Do not come near me, you beasts! I know you well: your hearts are against us, and you pray to the goddesses for the worst things against us.

But I still trust that, if the gods will it, you shall taste your own children yet.”

After saying this she ordered the women carrying rods to drive them off and beat any who refused to obey.

Taking this as their excuse, all the women withdrew, lifting up their hands to the gods and praying that she herself might experience those very things she was threatening to inflict on others.

### English (current)

The detailed provisions were as follows: Antiochus and the Romans were to remain friends for all time, provided he carried out the treaty.

King Antiochus and those under his authority were not to allow enemies of the Romans and their allies to pass through their territory or to supply them in any way.

The same obligation applied in reverse to the Romans and their allies with regard to Antiochus and those under him.

Antiochus was not to make war on the islanders or on peoples in Europe.

He was to evacuate the cities and territory [...].

His soldiers were to take nothing out except the weapons they were carrying; if they had removed anything else, they were to restore it to the same cities.

They were not to receive from the realm of King Eumenes either soldiers or anyone else.

If any of Antiochus’s troops were in the cities being taken over by the Romans, they were to be returned to Apamea.

But any persons from Antiochus’s kingdom who were with the Romans and their allies were to be free either to remain where they were, if they wished, or to run away.

Antiochus and those under him were to restore the slaves of the Romans and their allies, as well as prisoners and deserters, and any captives they might have received from any source.

Antiochus was also to hand over, if it was in his power, Hannibal son of Hamilcar the Carthaginian, Mnasilochus the Acarnanian, Thoas the Aetolian, the Chalcidians Eubulidas and Philo, and any Aetolians who had held federal office.

He was to surrender all the elephants at Apamea and to keep no others in the future.

He was likewise to surrender his warships and all their tackle and equipment, and thereafter keep no more than ten decked ships; he was not to possess any vessel rowed by more than thirty oars, nor any single-banked warship for wars begun by himself.

Nor were his ships to sail west of the Calycadnus and the Sarpedonian promontory, except when carrying tribute, envoys, or hostages.

Antiochus was not to be allowed to levy mercenaries from territory subject to Rome, nor to receive fugitives from it.

Any houses belonging to Rhodians or their allies in territory subject to King Antiochus were to remain Rhodian property, as they had been before the outbreak of war.

Any debts owed to them were still to be legally recoverable, and any property taken from them was to be returned once traced. The Rhodians were also to continue, as before the war, exempt from dues.

If Antiochus had given to others any of the cities he was now bound to restore, he was to withdraw his garrisons and men from those places too. If people later wished to desert to him, he was not to receive them.

Antiochus was to pay the Romans twelve thousand talents of the best Attic silver over twelve years, one thousand each year, each talent weighing not less than eighty Roman pounds, and also five hundred and forty thousand modii of grain.

He was also to pay King Eumenes three hundred and fifty talents over the first five years, seventy each year at the season when he also paid the Romans, and, in place of the grain as valued by Antiochus himself, one hundred and twenty-seven talents and one thousand two hundred and eight drachmas, which Eumenes agreed to accept in satisfaction.

Antiochus was to give twenty hostages, replacing them every three years with others, none younger than eighteen and none older than forty-five.

If any payment fell short, the deficit was to be made good in the following year.

If any of the cities or peoples whom Antiochus was forbidden to attack should themselves first make war on him, he might lawfully fight them.

But he was not to exercise sovereignty over such peoples or cities, nor attach them to himself as friends.

Any injuries committed by either side against the other were to be submitted to arbitration.

If both parties by common agreement wished to add anything to the treaty or remove anything from it, they were free to do so.

### English (current)

Because Perseus intended to come into Thessaly with an army and decide the whole issue by force of arms, Archon and his colleagues thought it best to answer the suspicions and slanders against them once again by their actions themselves.

So they introduced before the Achaeans a decree that they should make a general levy and march into Thessaly, taking their full part in the struggle alongside the Romans.

When this decree had been ratified, the Achaeans decided that Archon should take charge of assembling the army and making preparations for the expedition, and that envoys should be sent to the consul in Thessaly to explain the Achaean decision and ask when and where they should join their army to his.

They immediately appointed Polybius and others as envoys, and gave Polybius special instructions: if the general approved the arrival of the army, he was to send his fellow envoys off at once to report this, so that they might not miss the right moment, while he himself was to see to it that the whole force found markets in the cities through which it marched and that the soldiers lacked none of their necessities.

With these instructions the men just mentioned set out.

They also appointed Telocritus and his colleagues as envoys to Attalus, carrying the decree concerning the restoration of the honors of Eumenes.

At the same time word reached the Achaeans concerning King Ptolemy that the customary rites performed for kings when they come of age, the Anacleteria, had now taken place in his case.

Judging that it was proper for them to mark the occasion, they voted to send envoys to renew the goodwill already existing between the League and the kingdom, and they at once appointed Alcithus and Pasiadas.

### English (current)

At the time when Antiochus occupied Egypt, those of the envoys from Greece who had been sent to arrange a settlement came to meet him. He received the men graciously and made their first reception notably splendid.

On the following day he granted them an audience and told them to speak on the matters for which they had been instructed.

The Achaean envoys spoke first, then Demaratus on behalf of the Athenians, and after him Eudemus of Miletus.

Since they were all speaking at the same moment of the same issue, the substance of their individual speeches was naturally much alike.

All of them laid the blame for what had happened on Eulaeus, and by appealing to Ptolemy’s kinship with Antiochus and to the king’s youth, they begged Antiochus to put aside his anger.

Antiochus agreed with all this and even strengthened their case further, but then began to speak in defense of his original rights, by which he tried to establish that the regions of Coele-Syria properly belonged to the kings of Syria, supporting his position by referring to the dominion of Antigonus, the first ruler who held the kingdom in Syria, and citing the concessions granted to Seleucus by the kings of Macedonia after Antigonus’s death.

Next, building on these points, he appealed to the final acquisition made by his father Antiochus in war, and, above all, he denied the agreement which the men in Alexandria said had been made between the recently deceased Ptolemy and Antiochus’s father: namely, that Coele-Syria was to be given to him as a dowry when he married Cleopatra, the mother of the present king.

After speaking on this case, and convincing not only himself but also those who had come to meet him that his claims were just, he then sailed to Naucratis.

There too he treated the people kindly, gave a gold coin to each of the Greeks living there, and then advanced against Alexandria.

To the envoys he promised that he would give his answer when Aristides and Theris returned to him.

For, he said, he had sent them to Ptolemy, and he wanted the envoys from Greece all to be fully informed and to serve as witnesses.
