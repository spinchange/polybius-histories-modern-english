### English (current)

The more eagerly the Senate took up the war, the stranger the events became for them.

For Q. Fulvius Nobilior, who had commanded in Iberia the previous year, and the men who had served with him, reported in Rome the endless series of pitched battles, the great number of the dead, and the courage of the Celtiberians.

And since Marcellus was plainly shrinking from the war, a completely irrational panic seized the young men—one, the older men said, such as had never occurred before.

So far did this fear go that there were not enough men willing to come forward for the office of military tribune, and the posts were not completely filled, though in former times more men than were needed used to volunteer for them.

Nor would the men nominated by the consuls as legates to accompany the commanders serve.

And worst of all, the young men tried to evade the levies and came up with excuses that were shameful to make, improper to examine, and impossible to refute.

At last, when the Senate and the magistrates were at a loss to know what the end of this shamelessness among the young men would be—for they had no other name for it—Publius Cornelius Scipio Aemilianus, who, though still young, had already been regarded as a leader in the war and had won a settled reputation for moral excellence and self-restraint, but still lacked a reputation for personal bravery, saw the Senate’s difficulty and stood up to say that he was willing to go to Iberia with the consuls either as a military tribune or as a legate, since he was ready for either role.

He added, however, that for his own part a mission to Macedonia would be both safer and more fitting, for at that very time the Macedonians had in fact invited Scipio by name to come and settle the disputes raging among them.

But, he said, the needs of his own country were more urgent, and they called those who truly loved honour to Iberia.

This offer, unexpected from every point of view because of Scipio’s age and his general caution, immediately won him great approval, and still more in the days that followed; the men who had earlier shrunk from danger, in order to avoid the disgrace of being compared with him, now began volunteering for service, some offering to serve as legates to the commanders and others joining the military register in groups and customary circles.

### English (current)

Some may perhaps ask why I have not made use of dramatic speeches in narrating a subject and events of such importance.

That is in fact what most historians do: they arrange the arguments on both sides and present the speeches that could have been delivered.

As for myself, I have shown in many places in my history that I do not reject this practice altogether, since I have often reported speeches and political statements made by statesmen.

But that I do not choose to do this in every case will now be clear: for it would not be easy to find a subject more striking than this one, or material more abundant and better suited to comparison.

Indeed, no form of composition could be more convenient for me.

Yet I do not think it right for statesmen, on every question brought before them, to be ready with arguments and long elaborations, but rather to speak in the way appropriate to the particular occasion; and in the same way I do not think it right for historians to display their skill for the amusement of their readers, but rather to devote all their effort to discovering and recording what was truly said, and, among such words, only those that are most timely and most essential.
