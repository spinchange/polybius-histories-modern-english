### English (current)

And about this same time the Rhodians declared war on the Byzantines for certain such reasons. As far as the sea is concerned, the Byzantines inhabit a site that is the most favorable, both for security and for prosperity, of any in the whole of our inhabited world; but as regards the land, it is in both respects the most unfavorable of all.

For by sea they so command the mouth of the Pontus that no merchant can sail either in or out without their consent.

And since the Pontus holds many things useful for the livelihood of the rest of mankind, the Byzantines are masters of all of them.

For toward the necessary needs of life, the regions of the Pontus furnish us with livestock and with the great number of persons brought into slavery, supplying them, by common admission, in the most abundant and most serviceable quantity; while toward our comforts they provide us unstintingly with honey, wax, and salt-fish.

They also receive what is surplus in our own regions—olive oil and every kind of wine—and they exchange these for grain, sometimes giving it when it is opportune, sometimes taking it.

From all these things the Greeks would necessarily have been either wholly cut off, or else have found the exchange of them entirely unprofitable, had the Byzantines chosen to be obstructive and to make common cause now with the Gauls, now still more with the Thracians—or had they not inhabited the place at all.

For owing to the narrowness of the passage and the multitude of barbarians lying alongside it, the Pontus would, by common admission, have been closed to our ships.

Now perhaps the greatest advantages for their own livelihood accrue to the Byzantines themselves, because of the special features of their location.

For everything that is surplus among them finds a ready and profitable export, and whatever they lack a ready and profitable import, without any hardship or danger.

Yet, as we have said, many useful things also come to other peoples through them. Therefore, being in a way common benefactors of all, they would reasonably deserve from the Greeks not only gratitude but also a shared defense in times of crisis brought on by the barbarians.

But since it has come about that the special character and natural advantage of the place are unknown to most people—because it lies a little outside the parts of the inhabited world that are commonly surveyed— and since we all wish to know such things, and above all to become eyewitnesses of places that have something exceptional or distinctive, or, if that is not possible, at least to hold in our minds notions and impressions of them as close to the truth as may be, it would be right to state what the circumstance is, and what it is that produces such great and such remarkable abundance for the aforementioned city.

### English (current)

For supposing that getting inside the gates was the whole point of taking an enemy’s city, in this manner they handled their affairs. And so, after staying together only a very short time around the marketplace, they then, in their passion for plunder, drifted apart, broke into the houses, and began carrying off the people’s property—and by now it was broad daylight.

The Aegiratans, the affair having befallen them utterly unhoped-for and unexpected, were thrown into panic and terror: those upon whose houses the enemy had descended all turned to flight out of the city, supposing that it was already firmly in the enemy’s hands.

But all those who, hearing the shouting, came out to help from houses that had not yet been touched, ran together to the citadel.

These kept growing more numerous and more confident, while the body of Aetolians, on the contrary, grew smaller and more disordered, for the reasons already given.

Nevertheless Dorimachus and his men, seeing the danger now closing around them, rallied together and charged the men holding the citadel, supposing that by their boldness and daring they would strike panic into those who had assembled for the defense and put them to flight.

But the Aegiratans, cheering one another on, fought back and grappled with the Aetolians nobly.

Since the citadel was unwalled, and the fighting was hand to hand and man against man, the contest was at first what one would expect, the one side fighting for fatherland and children, the other for their own survival. In the end, however, the Aetolians who had broken in were routed.

The Aegiratans, seizing the opening of their enemies’ wavering, pressed upon them vigorously and terrifyingly. As a result it came about that most of the Aetolians, in their panic, fleeing of their own accord, were trampled together at the gates.

Alexander fell in hand-to-hand fighting in the thick of the action, while Archidamus perished in the crush and suffocation at the gates.

Of the rest of the Aetolian host, part was trampled underfoot, and part, fleeing over the cliffs by the trackless ways, broke their necks.

What part of them did get through safely to the ships threw away their arms in utter disgrace and made their voyage back, as unexpectedly as they had come.

Thus the Aegiratans, having lost their fatherland through negligence, recovered it again, against all expectation, through their courage and gallantry.

### English (current)

After traversing the places just mentioned and reaching the village called Pamphia, he likewise secured this place with a garrison and advanced toward Thermus by a road that was not only steep and exceptionally rough, but also hemmed in on both sides by deep precipices, so that in some places the path was very dangerous and narrow, the whole ascent being nearly thirty stades.

Yet he covered this stretch too in a short time, because the Macedonians made the march with great energy.

He reached Thermus late in the day, and after pitching camp he let the army plunder the surrounding villages and sweep over the plain of Thermus; likewise they looted the houses in Thermus itself, which were full not only of grain and similar provisions, but also of the finest equipment and possessions belonging to the Aetolians.

For since every year their most notable markets and festivals, and also the appointments of magistrates, were held in this place, each person stored there the most splendid of his possessions for entertaining guests and for the preparations connected with those occasions.

Quite apart from their practical use, they also believed this place gave them the greatest security, because no enemy had ever yet dared to invade these districts, and because the natural character of the site made it, as it were, the acropolis of all Aetolia.

When the Carthaginians seized the Roman transport ships, along with a vast quantity of supplies, Scipio was deeply angered, not only because those provisions had been taken from his own side, but because the enemy had been furnished with an abundant store of necessities.

He was even more indignant because the Carthaginians had broken their oaths and their treaty, and had started the war again from the beginning.

So he immediately chose Lucius Sergius, Lucius Baebius, and Lucius Fabius as envoys and sent them to speak to the Carthaginians about what had happened, while also announcing that the Roman people had ratified the treaty.

For letters had just reached Scipio making this clear.

When the envoys arrived at Carthage, they were brought first before the senate and then before the people, and in both settings they spoke openly about the situation.

They first reminded them that the Carthaginian ambassadors, when they had come to Tunes and entered the Roman council, had not merely appealed to the gods and kissed the ground, as men commonly do, but had actually thrown themselves flat on the earth in a shameful way and kissed the feet of those sitting in council. After that, standing up again, they had accused themselves, admitting that they themselves had broken the treaties first made between Romans and Carthaginians.

Therefore, they said, they were not unaware that any punishment the Romans chose to inflict would be deserved; yet, for the sake of the changes of human fortune, they begged not to suffer anything beyond remedy, since their own folly would provide proof of Roman nobility and generosity.

Remembering all this, the envoys said, their general and all who had been present in that council were astonished that the Carthaginians could forget what they themselves had said then, and now dare to violate their oaths and treaty.

This, they said, could only mean that they were trusting in Hannibal and the forces with him, and in that they were badly mistaken.

For everyone knew perfectly well that for two years already he and his army had been driven from all Italy and shut up near the Lacinian promontory, almost under siege, and had now barely escaped with their lives.

And even if he had come as a conqueror, they said, and even if he were about to fight men who had already beaten the Carthaginians in two successive battles, they still ought to hold uncertain hopes about the future, and think not only about victory but also about defeat.

If that happened, what gods would they call upon?

With what words would they try to draw pity from the victors for their disasters? By their faithlessness and folly, they would cut themselves off from every hope, both from gods and from men.
