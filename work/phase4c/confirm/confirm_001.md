### English (current)

As soon as Xanthippus gave the order to the men on the elephants to advance and break up the enemy’s line, and to the cavalry on each wing to wheel around and attack the enemy, then the Roman army too, according to their custom, clashed their weapons together, raised their battle cry, and charged the enemy.

The Roman cavalry on both wings quickly fled, because the Carthaginian cavalry were so much more numerous.

Of the infantry, those posted on the left wing, partly avoiding the elephants’ charge and partly despising the mercenaries, attacked the Carthaginian right. After routing them, they pressed on and pursued them as far as the camp rampart.

But among those stationed opposite the elephants, the front ranks, once engaged, were forced back and trampled underfoot by the sheer power of the animals, and perished in heaps where they stood. Still, because of the depth of the formation behind them, the main body remained unbroken for some time.

But when the men in the rear ranks found themselves surrounded on every side by cavalry and were forced to turn and fight them, while those who had forced their way through the elephants to the front, with the beasts now behind them, ran into the Carthaginian phalanx still intact and in good order, they were cut down.

Then, hard pressed from every side, most of the Romans were trampled to death by the overwhelming force of the animals, while the rest were shot down by the mass of cavalry on the very ground of the battle line. Only a very few broke away and tried to flee.

But since the routes of retreat lay over open plain, some of these too were killed by the elephants and cavalry, and perhaps five hundred who fled with the general Marcus were soon overtaken and captured alive together with him.

On the Carthaginian side about eight hundred mercenaries fell, namely those posted opposite the Roman left.

Of the Romans, about two thousand survived—those who, by pursuing the troops mentioned above, had stayed outside the main danger. All the rest perished except the general Marcus and those who fled with him.

The Roman units that escaped made their way with difficulty back to Aspis.

The Carthaginians stripped the dead, took the general along with the prisoners, and returned to the city overjoyed at the turn events had taken.

### English (current)

Now, since among those who have written about the same period as Aratus, Phylarchus is, in some quarters, held worthy of acceptance—though on many points he holds opposing views and writes the contrary of Aratus— it would be useful, or rather necessary for us, since we have chosen to follow Aratus concerning the Cleomenic affairs, not to leave this matter unexamined, lest we allow falsehood in written works to carry weight equal to the truth.

In general, then, this writer has throughout his whole work said much at random and as chance dictated.

But while it is perhaps not necessary to censure him on the other matters at present, or to scrutinize them closely, those of his statements that bear on the period I am now writing about—that is, the affairs of the Cleomenic war—on these it is necessary for us to give a precise account.

These will be quite sufficient to enable us to understand his whole purpose and ability in his work.

Wishing, then, to set forth the cruelty of Antigonus and the Macedonians, and along with theirs that of Aratus and the Achaeans, he says that the Mantineans, when they fell into their hands, met with great misfortunes, and that the most ancient and greatest city of Arcadia struggled with such great calamities as to move all the Greeks to amazement and tears.

And in his eagerness to call forth the pity of his readers and to make them feel for what he describes, he brings in women clinging together, hair flung loose, breasts bared, and besides these the tears and laments of men and women being led off into captivity together with their children and aged parents. And he does this throughout his whole history, always trying in each instance to set the horrors before our eyes.

Now let the ignoble and womanish character of his approach be passed over; let us examine instead what is proper to history and at the same time useful.

A historian, then, must not try to astonish his readers by sensational writing, nor seek out the speeches that might have been delivered, nor reckon up the circumstances that might attend his subject—as the writers of tragedy do—but must record above all only what was actually done and said, even if it happens to be quite ordinary.

For the aim of history and of tragedy is not the same, but the opposite. In the latter the object is to astound and beguile the hearers for the moment by the most plausible words; in the former it is, through true deeds and words, to instruct and convince for all time those who love learning.

In the one, what governs is plausibility, even if it be false, because the object is the deception of the spectators; in the other it is the truth, because the object is the benefit of those who love learning.

Apart from this, he relates most of his catastrophes without setting out the cause and manner of what happened—without which it is impossible reasonably to feel pity or properly to feel anger at anything that occurs.

For who among men does not think it a dreadful thing that free men should be struck? Yet, nonetheless, if a man suffers this because he began with unjust violence, he is judged to have suffered justly; whereas if the same thing is done for correction and instruction, those who strike free men are thought worthy even of honor and gratitude.

And again, the killing of fellow citizens is held to be the gravest impiety, deserving the heaviest penalties—and yet plainly the man who kills a thief or an adulterer goes unpunished, while the man who kills a traitor or a tyrant receives honor and precedence among all.

So in every case the final judgment about such things rests not in the deeds themselves, but in the causes and purposes of those who act, and in the differences between them.

### English (current)

Besides this, he recounted the misfortunes of the Mantineans with exaggeration and dramatic effect, evidently supposing that it is the duty of historians to call attention to lawless deeds; yet of the nobility of the Megalopolitans, which they displayed at the very same period, he made not even the slightest mention— as though it were more proper to history to enumerate the offenses of the wicked than to mark out the fine and just things that men do, or as though readers of such records were less improved by serious and admirable deeds than by lawless and despicable ones.

How Cleomenes took the city, and how, having kept it intact, he at once sent letter-carriers to the Megalopolitans in Messene, inviting them to recover their homeland undamaged and to share in his own cause—this he did relate to us, wishing to display the magnanimity and moderation of Cleomenes toward his enemies.

And further, how the Megalopolitans, while the letter was being read aloud, would not let it be read to the end and nearly stoned the letter-carriers—this much he made clear. But the natural sequel, the very thing proper to history, he left out: the praise and the honorable record of their admirable resolve.

And yet it lay ready at hand. For if we count as good men those who endure war on behalf of friends and allies merely by their words and votes, while to those who go so far as to take upon themselves the devastation of their land and a siege we award not only praise but also gratitude and the greatest rewards, what estimate must we form of the Megalopolitans? Surely the most honorable and the highest?

First they surrendered their land to Cleomenes; then again they suffered utter ruin for their homeland because of their loyalty to the Achaeans; and finally, when an unhoped-for and unexpected opportunity was given them to recover it undamaged, they chose instead to lose their land, the tombs of their ancestors, their temples, their homeland, their possessions—everything, in a word, that men hold most necessary—rather than betray their faith to their allies.

What nobler deed has ever been done, or could be done? To what could a historian better draw the attention of his audience? By what deed could he more strongly urge men toward keeping faith and toward genuine and lasting partnership? Yet of none of this did Phylarchus make any mention, being blind, as it seems to me, to the finest things and those most fitting for a historian to treat.

### English (current)

The Saguntines kept sending embassies to Rome, partly because they were anxious for themselves and could see what was coming, and partly because they wanted the Romans to be aware of the steady success the Carthaginians were having in their Iberian affairs.

For a long while the Romans paid no attention to them, but at this point they dispatched envoys to investigate the situation.

At about the same time Hannibal, having subdued the peoples he had set out to subdue, came back with his forces to winter at New Carthage, which was in a sense the showpiece and royal seat of the Carthaginians in the regions of Iberia.

There he found the embassy from Rome, granted it an audience, and heard out what it had to say about the present circumstances.

The Romans, for their part, solemnly warned him to keep his hands off the Saguntines—since they were under Roman protection—and not to cross the river Ebro, in keeping with the agreement made in Hasdrubal’s time.

But Hannibal—being young, full of warlike energy, successful in his ventures, and long since driven by his hatred of Rome— answered the envoys by posing as the protector of the Saguntines. He charged the Romans with having, a short time before, when civil strife broke out in the city, taken on the role of arbitrators and unjustly put some of the leading men to death; he said the Carthaginians would not stand by while the Saguntines were treated treacherously like this, for it was Carthage’s ancestral policy to overlook no one who was being wronged.

At the same time he sent word to Carthage asking what he should do, on the grounds that the Saguntines, trusting in their alliance with Rome, were committing injustices against some of the peoples subject to Carthage.

In short, he was thoroughly governed by unreason and violent passion. For this reason he did not make use of the genuine grounds for complaint, but took refuge in irrational pretexts—just as men typically do when, because of the impulses already lodged in them, they lose all regard for what is right.

How much better it would have been for him to take the view that the Romans ought to give back Sardinia to the Carthaginians, along with the tribute they had imposed at the same time—both of which the Romans had unjustly seized earlier when they took advantage of Carthage’s difficulties—and that, failing that, he would go to war.

Instead, by saying nothing of the real, existing grievance and fabricating a non-existent one concerning the Saguntines, he gave the impression of starting the war not only without reason but, still worse, unjustly.

As for the Roman envoys, since they now clearly understood that war was inevitable, they sailed off to Carthage, intending to lodge the same protest there as well.

They expected, however, that they would be fighting the war not in Italy but in Iberia, and that they would use the city of Saguntum as their base of operations.

### English (current)

Furthermore, the Romans concluded yet another treaty, the last of them, at the time of Pyrrhus’s crossing, before the Carthaginians had embarked on the war over Sicily.

In it they preserve all the other provisions in accordance with the existing agreements, but the following clauses are added to them.

“If they make a written alliance with Pyrrhus, both parties shall make it on such terms that they remain free to come to each other’s aid in the territory of whichever is at war.

Whichever of the two has need of the assistance, the Carthaginians shall furnish the ships both for the voyage out and for the return, but each side shall provide the pay for its own men.

The Carthaginians shall also aid the Romans by sea, if there is need; but no one shall compel the crews to disembark against their will.”

The oath that had to be sworn was as follows.

In the case of the first treaties, the Carthaginians swore by the gods of their ancestors and the Romans by Jupiter the Stone, in accordance with a certain ancient custom; but in the case of these later treaties, by Mars and Enyalius.

The swearing by Jupiter the Stone went like this: the man taking the oath over the treaty takes a stone in his hand, and once he has sworn in the name of the public faith, he says these words:

“If I keep my oath, may good things be mine; but if I think or do otherwise, then, while all others are kept safe in their own homelands, under their own laws, in possession of their own livelihoods, their sacred things, and their tombs, may I alone be cast out, just as this stone is now.”

And having said this, he throws the stone from his hand.

### English (current)

At this time the general of the Aetolians was Ariston. Because of certain physical infirmities he was unfit for active military service, and since he was moreover a kinsman of Dorimachus and Scopas, he had in effect handed over the whole of his command to Dorimachus.

Now Dorimachus did not dare to urge the Aetolians publicly to take up the war against the Messenians, because he had no pretext worth mentioning. Everyone knew that his impulse arose from his own lawless conduct and from the insult he had suffered.

So he gave up that idea and instead set about privately persuading Scopas to join him in his design against the Messenians. He pointed out that there was no danger from Macedonia, on account of the youth of its ruler—for Philip was then no more than seventeen years old—and he set before him the hostility of the Spartans toward the Messenians, and reminded him of the goodwill and alliance of the Eleans toward themselves. From all this he argued that their invasion of Messenia would be perfectly safe.

But the heart of his Aetolian appeal was that he held before their eyes the profits to be had from the Messenian territory, which was unguarded and had remained the only part of the Peloponnese untouched throughout the Cleomenic war.

On top of all this he urged the popularity they would win among the mass of the Aetolians as a result.

As for the Achaeans, he argued that if they tried to block the passage through their land, the Aetolians could not be blamed for defending themselves; and if the Achaeans kept quiet, they would be no hindrance to the enterprise.

Against the Messenians, he said, they would not be short of a pretext, since the Messenians had long been guilty of wrongdoing by promising the Achaeans and Macedonians that they would join their alliance.

By saying this, and other things to the same effect, he stirred up such eagerness in Scopas and his friends that, without waiting for a general assembly of the Aetolians, without consulting the Apocleti, and without taking any of the proper constitutional steps—acting entirely on their own impulses and decisions—they launched a war simultaneously against the Messenians, Epirotes, Achaeans, Acarnanians, and Macedonians.
