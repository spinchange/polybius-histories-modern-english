### English (current)

[...] Again, other writers on the Syrian war. The reason for this is what I have already explained more than once.

For when men take a simple and straightforward subject and yet want not the facts but the bulk of their books to make them seem like historians, and want that sort of display to create the impression of importance, it becomes necessary for them to make small things big, and to dress up matters that were briefly stated, and in some cases to turn incidental happenings into full-scale actions and affairs—staging contests and announcing battle lines in which sometimes ten infantrymen fell, sometimes only a little more, and cavalry even fewer than that.

As for sieges, geography, and similar topics, no one could adequately describe how far they work these up, given their lack of real material.

But the opposite is true for those who write universal history: and so one should not blame us as if we were dragging out events when we sometimes pass over things that others have given great attention and embellishment, and sometimes mention them only briefly; rather, one should trust that we give each subject the treatment it deserves.

For when those writers describe, somewhere in the course of their whole work, the siege of Phanoteia and Coroneia and [...], they are forced to set out every contrivance, every bold move, and every detail of the siege, and beyond that [...] to dwell on the capture of Tarentum, Corinth, Sardis, Gaza, Bactra, and, above all, Carthage, adding details of their own; yet they are not at all satisfied if we give a plain and truthful account of such matters.

The same judgment should apply to our treatment of battles and speeches, and likewise to the other parts of history: in all these matters we would fairly deserve much forgiveness, and the same is true of what I am about to narrate, for instance if [...] I should seem to use the same material, the same handling of events, or the same expressions; and also if I should anywhere make slips in the names of mountains or rivers or in the particular features of places. The size of the undertaking itself is excuse enough in all these matters, unless I am caught deliberately making false statements for gain; for that I ask no pardon, as I have repeatedly and expressly said in this work.

### English (current)

At the time when Perseus, after his defeat, was making his escape, the senate decided to summon the Rhodian ambassadors, who had come to ask for an end to the war.

Fortune seemed, as if by design, to put the Rhodians’ ignorance on display before the stage—if one should call it ignorance of the Rhodians, and not rather of the men who were then dominant at Rhodes.

When the party led by Hegesipolis entered, they said that they had come to end the war. For the Rhodian people, seeing that the war had dragged on for a long time, and seeing that it was harmful to all the Greeks and also to the Romans themselves because of the enormous expense, had come to that view.

But now that the war had ended according to the Rhodians’ wishes, they had come simply to congratulate the Romans. Such, briefly, was what the men around Hegesipolis said.

The senate, taking advantage of the moment and wanting to make an example of the Rhodians, delivered a reply whose main point was this:

They did not think that this embassy had been sent by the Rhodians for the sake either of the Greeks or of themselves, but for Perseus.

For if they had been sent on behalf of the Greeks, the more fitting time would have been when Perseus was ravaging Greek territory and cities, while encamped in Thessaly for almost two years [...].

But to let that time pass by, and now to come eagerly seeking an end to the war, when our armies had entered Macedonia and Perseus was shut in with only the faintest hope of survival, made it plain to anyone who judged matters rightly that they had sent their embassy not to end the war, but to rescue Perseus and save him as far as they could.

For that reason, they said, the Romans were under no obligation at present either to show them favor or to answer them kindly.

That was the senate’s decision for the Rhodian ambassadors.

### English (current)

That is why one is often reminded of the words of Demetrius of Phalerum.

In his treatise on Fortune, wishing to show people clearly how changeable Fortune is, he began with the age of Alexander, when Alexander brought down the Persian empire, and said this:

“If you were to keep in mind not an endless time or many generations, but only the fifty years immediately before our own day, you would recognize the harshness of Fortune here.

For would you have believed, fifty years earlier, if any god had foretold it, that by this time not even the name of the Persians would remain at all—those who had been masters of almost the whole inhabited world—while the Macedonians, whose name was scarcely known before, would become masters of it all?

And yet Fortune, whose action in our lives is unpredictable, who is always making new things out of what lies beyond our reckoning and showing her power through the unexpected, is even now, in my view, making this plain to all men: she has brought the Macedonians into the prosperity once enjoyed by the Persians, because she has lent these blessings to them too, until she decides something else about them.

“And that is what has now happened in the case of Perseus. So Demetrius, as though inspired by some divine mouth, has spoken prophetically about the future.

As for myself, when I came in my narrative to the time at which the kingdom of Macedon was destroyed, I did not think it right to pass the moment by without comment, since I had myself witnessed the event; rather, I wanted both to speak fittingly on the matter and to recall Demetrius’s words.

For it seems to me that he made a statement more divine than merely human, since he had foretold what would happen almost a hundred and fifty years beforehand.”

Astymedes thought that he had spoken well on behalf of his country, but he pleased neither the Greeks then staying at Rome nor those living at home.

These are the men we accept; these are the men we consider noble. But the man who, because of some vague fear, lays all other people’s errors before those in power and revives matters that time was carrying off into forgetfulness among the rulers—how could he fail to offend all who hear of it?

### English (current)

Now just when he had received his answers, it happened that Eumenes arrived.

This put the senate in considerable difficulty; for they were prejudiced against him and firmly fixed in their opinions about him, yet did not wish in any way to make their feelings obvious.

For since they had previously proclaimed this king to all the world as their foremost and greatest friend, if they now admitted him to an audience and allowed him to make his defense, then, if they answered in accordance with their real opinions, they would expose themselves for having valued such a man so highly in earlier times; but if, out of deference to appearances, they gave him a gracious reply, they would be overlooking the truth and the interests of their country.

So, since something unwelcome was likely to follow from either course, they found this solution to the dilemma.

As though they were generally displeased by visits from kings, they issued a decree that no king should come to them.

Afterwards, learning that Eumenes had landed in Italy at Brundisium, they sent the quaestor with the decree and instructed him to ask whether he had any business with the senate; and if he needed nothing, to tell him to leave Italy as quickly as possible.

When the quaestor met him, the king, recognizing the senate’s intention, in the end said nothing more than that he had no need of anything.

And so Eumenes was prevented from going up to Rome.

This policy also had another practical consequence.

For as a great danger from the Gauls hung over his kingdom, it was obvious that because of this humiliation all the king’s allies would be disheartened, while the Gauls would be doubly encouraged for the war.

And so, being absolutely determined to humble him by every means, they adopted this course.

While these events were still taking place at the beginning of winter, the senate dealt with all who had arrived on embassies— for there was neither city nor dynast nor king that had not sent an embassy at that time to offer congratulations on what had happened— and answered them all in a fitting and gracious manner, except the Rhodians.

These it dismissed while giving various hints about what was to come. It was still postponing a decision about the Athenians.

### English (current)

After this the senate summoned the Rhodians and gave them a hearing.

Astymedes came in and spoke with restraint, and much better than on the previous embassy.

For he gave up accusing others and instead turned to pleading for pardon, like men under the lash begging to be spared the blows, saying that his country had already suffered penalties enough, greater than its offense deserved.

He then went on to explain its losses, summarizing them point by point. First, he said, they had lost Lycia and Caria, regions on which they had spent a great deal of money from the beginning, having been compelled to wage three wars over them, and from which they had once received considerable revenues.

“But perhaps,” he said, “that can still be justified. You yourselves gave those districts to our people as a favor because of our goodwill, and when suspicion and estrangement arose between us, you could plausibly think it reasonable to take them away again.

But Caunus, surely, we bought for two hundred talents from the generals of Ptolemy, and Stratoniceia we received as a great favor from Antiochus, the son of Seleucus.

From these two cities together a yearly revenue of one hundred and twenty talents came to our people. Of all this income we have been deprived, because we wished to obey your commands.

By doing this you have laid a heavier burden on the Rhodians for a single mistake than on the Macedonians, who were your enemies at all times.

And the greatest blow of all to our city is this: your making Delos a free port has ruined our harbor revenues, and you have taken away the people’s political voice, by which both the business of the harbor and every other concern of the city had previously enjoyed the protection suited to them.

That this is true is not hard to learn.

In earlier times the harbor dues produced a million drachmas, but now you have taken away a hundred and fifty thousand. So, Romans, your anger has indeed struck at the principal resources of the city.

If the offense and estrangement had really been shared by the whole people, then perhaps even you might reasonably seem justified in maintaining an unrelenting anger that was hard to appease.

But if you know clearly that only a few men were responsible for this folly, and that all of them have perished at the hands of the people itself, why do you remain irreconcilable toward those who bear no blame, when to everyone else you seem the gentlest and most magnanimous of all men?

Therefore, gentlemen, the people, having lost its revenues, its political voice, and its equal standing, the very things for which in earlier times it had been ready to endure every risk, asks and entreats all of you, now that it has suffered blows enough, to let your anger cease and to restore the alliance, so that this may become plain to everyone: that you have laid aside your anger toward the Rhodians and returned to your original policy and friendship.

For that is what the people needs now, not an alliance of arms and soldiers.”

After speaking in this way, and to much the same effect besides, Astymedes was thought to have adapted his speech well to the circumstances.

Still, what helped the Rhodians most in obtaining the alliance was the recent arrival of the commissioners with Tiberius.

For they testified, first, that the Rhodians had obeyed every decree of the senate, and second, that all those responsible for the estrangement had been condemned to death among them. In this way they overcame those who spoke against the Rhodians and secured the alliance with Rome.
