# Book 2 Chapter 36: Hasdrubal Dies and Hannibal Succeeds Him

Now Hasdrubal, the Carthaginian general — for it was from his affairs that we digressed in our narrative — died after governing the affairs of Iberia for eight years. He was treacherously murdered at night in his own quarters by a certain man of Celtic stock, in revenge for private wrongs.

He had brought about no small but a great advance in the fortunes of Carthage, not so much through his military exploits as through his dealings with the native princes.

The Carthaginians then conferred the command in Iberia on Hannibal, despite his youth, on account of the shrewdness and daring that his conduct had already begun to display.

As soon as he took up the command, it was clear from his designs that he intended to make war on Rome — and this in fact he soon did, after waiting only a very short time.

From this period onward, then, relations between the Carthaginians and the Romans were marked by mutual suspicion and friction.

For the Carthaginians were plotting, eager to take their revenge for their losses around Sicily, while the Romans were distrustful, observing what the Carthaginians were planning.

From all this it was clear to anyone who judged the situation correctly that the two peoples would be at war with each other before long.

# Book 2 Chapter 37: Greece At This Time

At the same period the Achaeans and King Philip, together with their other allies, were embarking on the war against the Aetolians known as the Social War.

Now, having carried our account of the affairs of Sicily and Libya, and of what followed, continuously through the preliminary portion of the work down to the beginning of the Social War and of the second war between Rome and Carthage — the one most people call the Hannibalic War — and since from the outset it was my declared intention to make this period the starting point of my own composition,

it would be fitting to set those matters aside and turn to events in Greece, so that, having brought my preliminary survey from every quarter to the same level and the same point in time, I may then at last begin the demonstrative history proper.

For I have not undertaken, as my predecessors did, to record particular sets of events — Greek history, say, or Persian — but rather the events in all the known parts of the world at once, because the times we live in have contributed something distinctive toward this aspect of my plan, of which I shall give a clearer account elsewhere.

It would therefore be proper, even before the main construction of the work, to touch briefly on the most prominent and best-known nations and regions of the inhabited world.

Now as for the affairs of Asia and Egypt, it will be enough to begin our account of them from the period just mentioned, since the earlier history of those countries has been published by many writers and is familiar to everyone, and since in our own day nothing so unusual or unexpected has befallen them at the hands of fortune as to require any reference back to their past.

But as for the Achaean nation and the royal house of Macedonia, it will be appropriate to go back briefly in time — since the latter has now been utterly extinguished,

while in the case of the Achaeans, as I said above, there has occurred in our own time an extraordinary growth and a coming-together in unity.

For although many men in past times had set out to bring the Peloponnesians together for their common advantage, and not one had succeeded — because each was exerting himself not for the freedom of all but for his own personal power —

yet in our own day this enterprise has reached such progress and completion that there is among them not merely an alliance and friendly partnership in their affairs, but they also use the same laws, weights, measures, and currency, and in addition the same magistrates, councillors,

and judges. In general, the only respect in which the whole Peloponnese falls short of being a single city is that its inhabitants are not enclosed within one and the same wall; in all other respects, both collectively and city by city, their institutions are nearly identical.

# Book 2 Chapter 38: Origin of the Name "Achaean"

First, it is worth learning how and in what way the name of the Achaeans came to prevail over all the Peloponnesians.

For those who from the beginning bore this name as their ancestral title are not distinguished by any greater extent of territory and cities, nor by greater wealth, nor by the valor of their men.

The Arcadian people, and likewise the Laconian, far surpass them in numbers of men and in extent of land; and indeed neither of these would ever consent to yield the first place in courage to any of the Greeks.

How then, and for what reason, are these peoples and the rest of the Peloponnesians now content to share both the constitution and the name of the Achaeans?

It would clearly be quite inappropriate to call it the work of chance — that is a feeble answer; rather, one must seek a cause, for without a cause nothing, whether it seems to follow reason or to defy it, can be brought about. The cause, in my opinion, is something like this.

Nowhere could one find a system and a principle of equality before the law, of free speech, and in a word of genuine democracy, more pure than that which exists among the Achaeans.

This constitution found some of the Peloponnesians willing converts of their own accord, and won over many by persuasion and argument; and those whom it constrained at the right moment it presently brought, even so, to acquiesce in it.

For since it reserved no special advantage for the original members, but granted equal rights to all newcomers alike, it swiftly attained its purpose, using the two most powerful aids: equality and humane treatment.

This, then, must be regarded as the originating and contributing cause of the Peloponnesians' coming to share one purpose and establishing the prosperity they now enjoy.

This principle and this distinctive character of their constitution, as just described, had existed among the Achaeans in earlier times as well.

This could be shown by many other proofs, but for the present it will suffice, for the sake of confidence, to bring forward one or two pieces of evidence.

# Book 2 Chapter 39: The First Achaean League

At the time when, in the regions of Italy then called Magna Graecia, the meeting-houses of the Pythagoreans were burned down,

there followed, as was natural, a complete upheaval of the political order, since the leading men of each city had thus been destroyed in so unexpected a fashion;

and the Greek cities in those parts came to be filled with slaughter, factional strife, and confusion of every kind.

At that time, when embassies came from most parts of Greece to try to settle these disorders, the cities turned to the Achaeans and to their good faith for deliverance from the evils that beset them.

And it was not only on this occasion that they showed their approval of the Achaean way; some time later they set out wholeheartedly to become imitators of their constitution.

For the people of Croton, Sybaris, and Caulonia, after inviting one another and reaching a common understanding, first established a shared temple and precinct of Zeus Homarios, in which they held their meetings and deliberations, and then, adopting the customs and laws of the Achaeans, undertook to use them and to govern their state accordingly. But under the pressure of the despotic power of Dionysius of Syracuse,

and likewise of the domination of the neighboring barbarians, they were hindered, and abandoned these arrangements — not willingly, but under compulsion.

Later, when the Lacedaemonians had suffered their unexpected defeat at the battle of Leuctra, and the Thebans had unexpectedly laid claim to the leadership of Greece, there was great uncertainty among all the Greeks, and especially among the two peoples just named, since the one would not admit it had been beaten, and the other did not believe it had won.

Nevertheless, in their dispute the Thebans and the Lacedaemonians entrusted the points at issue to the Achaeans alone of all the Greeks — not with any regard to their power,

for at that time they had perhaps the smallest power of any people in Greece, but chiefly out of regard for their good faith and their general integrity. For that was, by common consent, the reputation everyone then held of them.

At that period, then, these qualities of principle existed among them only in bare form; but no achievement or notable action tending to the growth of their own power came about,

because they were unable to produce a leader worthy of their principles. Whenever someone gave promise of it, he was overshadowed and held back, at one time by the dominance of the Lacedaemonians, at another, still more, by

that of the Macedonians. But when at last, in due time, the nation found leaders of sufficient worth, it quickly made its own power manifest by accomplishing that most glorious of works — the concord of the Peloponnesians.

As the originator and prime mover of the whole undertaking we must reckon Aratus of Sicyon; as the champion who carried the work through to completion, Philopoemen of Megalopolis; and as the man who secured its lasting permanence for a time, Lycortas and those who shared his policy.

What each of these men did, and how, and at what times, I shall try to make clear, always giving my attention to it in the manner appropriate to the narrative.

As for the measures carried out by Aratus, I shall mention them in summary both now and again later on, because he himself composed thoroughly truthful and lucid memoirs of his own deeds;

but for the others I shall give a more detailed and discriminating account. I suppose the easiest course for me, and the one that will make the subject easiest for my readers to follow, will be to take my starting point from the period when, the Achaean nation having been broken up city by city by the kings of Macedon, a new beginning was made and the cities once more drew together toward one another.

From that time the nation, growing continuously, advanced to the state of completion in which it stood in our own day, about which I have just spoken in some detail.

# Book 2 Chapter 41: The Second League

It was the 124th Olympiad when the people of Patrae and Dyme first began to act in concert,

the period in which Ptolemy son of Lagus, Lysimachus, and also Seleucus and Ptolemy Ceraunus departed this life — for all these died around the Olympiad just mentioned.

Now in the times before this, the situation of the aforesaid nation was roughly as follows.

They had been ruled by kings ever since Tisamenus, who was the son of Orestes and who, having been expelled from Sparta on the return of the Heraclidae, took possession of the regions around Achaea;

from him they were ruled by kings of the same line in unbroken succession down to Ogyges. After that, becoming dissatisfied with the sons of Ogyges because they ruled them not constitutionally but despotically, they changed their government into a democracy.

Thereafter, throughout the following times down to the supremacy of Alexander and Philip, their fortunes varied with circumstances, now one way, now another; but their common government, as we have said, they tried to maintain as a democracy.

This league consisted of twelve cities, which still survive today, except for Olenus and for Helice, which was swallowed up by the sea before the events at Leuctra.

These cities are: Patrae, Dyme, Pharae, Tritaea, Leontium, Aegium, Aegeira, Pellene, Bura, and Caryneia.

But in the period after the time of Alexander and before the Olympiad just mentioned, they fell into such discord and ill condition — chiefly through the agency of the kings of Macedon — that all the cities became separated from one another and pursued interests opposed to each other.

As a result, some of them came to have garrisons imposed on them by Demetrius and Cassander, and afterwards by Antigonus Gonatas, while others fell under the rule of tyrants; for this last king seems to have planted more absolute rulers among the Greeks than anyone else.

But around the 124th Olympiad, as I said above, they changed their minds and began once again to act in concert. This was at the time of Pyrrhus's crossing into Italy.

The first to form the union were the peoples of Dyme, Patrae, Tritaea, and Pharae; and for this reason there happens to be no monument recording the federation of these cities.

After this, in about the fifth year, the people of Aegium expelled their garrison and joined the federation; next to them the people of Bura, after killing their tyrant.

At the same time the people of Caryneia were restored to it. For Iseas, then tyrant of Caryneia, seeing that the garrison had been driven out of Aegium and that the despot in Bura had been destroyed by Margos and the Achaeans, and seeing himself on the point of being attacked from every side,

laid down his power and, after receiving guarantees from the Achaeans for his safety, attached his city to the Achaean confederation.

# Book 2 Chapter 42: Policy of the Achaean League

For what reason, then, did I go back to these times? First, so that it might become clear how, at what periods, and which of the original Achaeans were the first to undertake again the formation of the present league;

and second, so that their guiding policy might be confirmed not merely by my own assertion but by the facts themselves — namely, that the Achaeans always held to one and the same principle.

In accordance with this principle, holding out the equality before the law and the freedom of speech that prevailed among them, and continually making war on and crushing those who, whether on their own account or with the help of the kings, were enslaving their respective homelands, they accomplished this work in this manner and with this aim — partly by their own efforts, partly with the help of their allies.

For indeed whatever was achieved toward this end through those allies in later times must be credited to the deliberate policy of the Achaeans.

For though they took part in many enterprises with others, and in very many and very fine ones with the Romans, they never at any time sought from their successes any private advantage for themselves;

rather, in exchange for all the zeal they offered their allies, they bargained for nothing but the freedom of each people and the common concord of the Peloponnesians.

But it will be possible to grasp these things more clearly from the actual record of their deeds.
