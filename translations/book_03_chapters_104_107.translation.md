# Book 3 Chapter 104: Minucius In Difficulties

Hannibal, partly from what he heard from the prisoners taken and partly from what he observed of what was being done, came to understand the rivalry between the two commanders, as well as the impetuosity and ambition of Marcus.

For this reason, judging that what was happening among his opponents told not against him but in his favor, he turned his attention to Marcus, eager to strip away his boldness and to forestall his rash impulse.

There was a certain rise of ground between his own camp and that of Marcus, capable of harming either side, and he set out to seize it. Knowing clearly, from the success that had gone before, that Marcus would come to oppose this move at once, hand to hand, he devised the following scheme.

Since the ground around the hill was bare of trees but full of many folds and hollows of every kind, he sent out during the night, into the most suitable hiding places, parties of two and three hundred at a time — five hundred horsemen in all, and light-armed and other foot soldiers numbering in all about five thousand.

And so that they should not be spotted early by the men going out to forage, at the first light of dawn he seized the hill with his light troops.

When Marcus saw what was happening, he thought it a piece of luck and immediately sent out his light-armed troops, ordering them to fight and contend for the position; after them he sent the cavalry.

# Book 3 Chapter 105: Cautious Fabius Saves the Day

in much the same way. Just as day was breaking, and everyone's thoughts and eyes were wholly absorbed by the men fighting on the hill, the planting of the ambush went unsuspected.

As Hannibal kept sending up fresh reinforcements to his men on the hill, and followed close on their heels himself with his cavalry and his main force, it soon came about that the cavalry of the two sides also clashed.

When this happened, and the Roman light troops were being hard pressed by the mass of the cavalry, they fell back upon the heavy infantry and threw it into confusion;

and at the same moment the signal was given to the men in the ambush, who appeared on every side and charged in. So now not only the light troops but the whole army faced grave danger for the Romans.

At this critical moment Fabius, seeing what was happening and gravely afraid that they would suffer a complete disaster, led out his forces and hurried to the aid of the men in danger.

When he quickly drew near, the Romans took heart again; and although they had by now lost all order, they gathered once more around their standards and withdrew, taking refuge under the protection of his army — having lost many of their light troops and still more of the men from the legions, including their best soldiers.

Hannibal's men, struck by the unbroken strength and good order of the relieving army, broke off the pursuit and the fighting.

To those who had been in the thick of the danger it was plain that everything had been all but lost through the rashness of Marcus, and had been saved — both now and before — through the caution of Fabius.

And to those at Rome it now became openly clear what a difference there is between a soldier's recklessness and vainglory on the one hand, and a general's foresight and steady, sensible calculation on the other.

In any case, the Romans, taught by events, threw up a single palisade and all encamped together in one place, and from then on gave their attention to Fabius and to the orders he issued.

The Carthaginians, for their part, dug a trench across the ground between the hill and their own camp, and threw a palisade around the crest of the captured hill and set a guard upon it; and from then on they made their preparations for wintering in safety.

# Book 3 Chapter 106: New Consuls Elected

As the season for elections drew near, the Romans chose as their commanders Lucius Aemilius and Gaius Terentius. Once these had been appointed, the dictators laid down their office.

The consuls of the previous year, Gnaeus Servilius and Marcus Regulus — who had been appointed in succession after the death of Flaminius — were then commissioned as proconsuls by Aemilius and his colleagues; and, taking over command in the field, they managed the affairs of the army according to their own judgment.

Aemilius and his colleagues, after consulting with the Senate, at once enrolled the number of soldiers still lacking for the whole undertaking and dispatched them.

They instructed Gnaeus and his men by no means to risk a general engagement of any kind, but to carry out partial skirmishes as vigorously and as continuously as possible, in order to train the young men and prepare them, full of confidence, for the decisive battles —

since they reckoned that their earlier reverses had come about, in no small part, from their having relied on troops that were newly levied and wholly untrained.

They themselves sent Lucius Postumius, a praetor with six lictors, to Gaul with an army, wishing to create a diversion against the Celts who were serving with Hannibal.

They also took thought for bringing back the fleet wintering at Lilybaeum, and sent across to the commanders in Iberia everything that was urgently needed for the campaign.

So these men busied themselves carefully with these and the rest of their preparations.

Gnaeus and his officers, having received their instructions from the consuls, handled every detail according to their judgment.

For this reason I will refrain from writing more about them. Nothing decisive or worth recording was done at all, because of these instructions and because of the circumstances of the season;

there were only more frequent skirmishes and partial engagements, in which the Roman commanders won a good name — for they were thought to handle each matter both bravely and sensibly.

# Book 3 Chapter 107: Hannibal Occupies Cannae

So through the winter and the spring season the two armies remained encamped facing each other. But when the time was already at hand for drawing supplies from the year's crops, Hannibal moved his army out of the camp near Geronium.

Judging it to his advantage to force the enemy by every possible means to give battle, he seized the citadel of the town called Cannae.

For it was here that the Romans had been gathering their grain and other supplies from the region around Canusium, and from here these were continually brought up to the camp as need required.

The town itself had in fact already been razed earlier; but now that the stores and the citadel had been captured, no small disturbance arose among the Roman forces —

for they were in difficulty not only on account of the supplies, because the aforesaid place had been taken, but also because of its commanding position over the surrounding country.

So they kept sending to Rome and asking what they should do, since, if they drew near the enemy, they would not be able to avoid battle, with the country being laid waste and all their allies hanging in suspense.

The Senate resolved that they should fight and engage the enemy. They told Gnaeus and his men to hold back still, while they themselves sent out the consuls.

It happened that everyone looked to Aemilius and rested most of their hopes on him, both because of the nobility of his life in general and because, a short time before, he was thought to have conducted the war against the Illyrians both bravely and with advantage to the state.

They resolved to risk the venture with eight legions, something that had never before happened among the Romans, each legion containing about five thousand men apart from the allies.

For the Romans, as we have said somewhere before, always levy four legions. Each legion takes about four thousand foot and two hundred horse.

But whenever some more pressing need arises, they make the foot in each legion about five thousand and the horse three hundred.

Of the allies they make the number of foot equal to that of the Roman legions, but the horse, as a rule, three times as many.

Half of these allies, together with two legions, they assign to each of the consuls and send them out on campaign.

Most of their battles they decide with a single consul and two legions and the aforesaid number of allies, and only rarely do they employ them all at one moment and for a single venture.

On this occasion, however, they were so dismayed and fearful of what was to come that they chose to risk the venture not with four but with eight Roman legions together.
