# Book 3 Chapter 8: The Credibility of Fabius Pictor

Fabius, the Roman historian, claims that the cause of the war with Hannibal was, alongside the wrong done to the Saguntines, the greed and lust for power of Hasdrubal.

For Hasdrubal, he says, had built up great personal authority in the regions of Iberia, and afterward came to Libya with the intention of abolishing the laws and transforming the Carthaginian government into a monarchy.

But the leading men of the state foresaw his design, joined together against him, and resisted him.

Hasdrubal, suspecting their hostility, withdrew from Libya and from then on managed affairs in Iberia entirely according to his own judgment, paying no attention to the Carthaginian senate.

Hannibal, who from boyhood had shared and admired Hasdrubal's outlook, and who then succeeded to the command in Iberia, conducted affairs in the same manner as Hasdrubal.

And so, in this present case too, he launched this war against the Romans on his own authority, contrary to the will of the Carthaginians;

for none of the men of standing in Carthage approved of what Hannibal had done against the city of the Saguntines.

After saying this, Fabius goes on to claim that, following the capture of the aforementioned city, the Romans came forward and demanded that the Carthaginians either hand Hannibal over to them or take on the war.

Now, suppose someone were to ask this historian: what occasion could have suited the Carthaginians better, and what course could have been more just or more advantageous than this one—seeing that, as he himself says, they had been displeased from the very beginning with Hannibal's actions?

What better than to yield then to the Romans' demands, hand over the man responsible for the wrongs, and so reasonably rid themselves, through others' hands, of the common enemy of the state, while securing safety for their territory and warding off the threatened war—all by merely passing a decree? What answer could Fabius give to this? Clearly none.

For so far were the Carthaginians from doing any of the things just described that they fought continuously for seventeen years in accordance with Hannibal's policy, and did not lay down the war until, having exhausted every hope, they came at last to the danger that threatened their homeland and the lives of all in it.

# Book 3 Chapter 9: First Cause of the Second Punic War

For what reason, then, have I brought up Fabius and what he has written?

Not out of any worry that his account is so plausible it might win belief among some readers—for the absurdity of his claims can be seen on its own, even without any explanation from me, by those who come across it—

but rather for the sake of warning those who take up his books, so that they may look not to the author's reputation but to the facts.

For there are some who do not fix their attention on what is said but on the man who says it, and who, bearing in mind that the writer lived through those times and was a member of the Roman senate, at once assume that everything he says is to be trusted.

My own view is that we should not, on the one hand, take the historian's authority lightly, but neither should we treat it as conclusive; rather, readers should base their judgments for the most part on the facts themselves.

But to return to the war between the Romans and the Carthaginians—for it was from this point that we made our digression—we must consider its first cause to have been the resentment of Hamilcar, surnamed Barcas, the natural father of Hannibal.

For that man's spirit had not been broken by the war over Sicily, since he believed that he himself had kept intact the forces at Eryx, where he was stationed, with their fighting spirit undiminished, and that it was only because of the Carthaginians' defeat in the naval battle that he had yielded to circumstances and made the treaty. He therefore held fast to his anger, always watching for a chance to strike.

Now, had the upheaval over the mercenaries not broken out among the Carthaginians, he would at once have sought another pretext and begun preparations for action, so far as it lay in his power.

But he was forestalled by the civil disturbances, and in these...

# Book 3 Chapter 10: Second Cause of the War

...he was occupied, taken up with those events. When the Romans, after the Carthaginians had put down the aforementioned disturbance, declared war on them, the Carthaginians at first were ready to face any extremity, supposing they would prevail on the justice of their cause, as we have explained concerning these matters in the earlier books—

without which it would be impossible to follow properly either what is now being said or what we shall say hereafter.

But since the Romans would not give way, the Carthaginians yielded to the circumstances, and though deeply aggrieved, being unable to do anything, they withdrew from Sardinia and agreed to pay another twelve hundred talents on top of the earlier sum, on condition of not having to take on the war at that time.

This, then, must be reckoned the second—and the most important—cause of the war that later broke out.

For Hamilcar, adding to his own private resentment the anger of his fellow citizens over these matters, as soon as he had subdued the rebellious mercenaries and secured his country's safety, immediately set his energies on affairs in Iberia, eager to use it as a base of preparation for the war against the Romans. This too must be counted a cause—the third—

I mean the prosperous course of Carthaginian affairs in Iberia. For it was by relying on the strength gained there that they confidently entered upon the aforementioned war.

That Hamilcar contributed most to bringing about the second war—even though he died ten years before its outbreak—one could find many proofs; but what follows will be just about...

# Book 3 Chapter 11: Hannibal's Oath

...enough to establish the point. For at the time when Hannibal, after being defeated by the Romans, finally left his homeland and was staying at the court of Antiochus, the Romans, now observing the scheming of the Aetolians, sent envoys to Antiochus, wishing not to be left in the dark about the king's intentions.

These envoys, seeing that Antiochus was inclined toward the Aetolians and eager to make war on the Romans, courted Hannibal, intent on bringing him under suspicion with Antiochus.

And this is just what happened. As time went on, and the king grew ever more suspicious of Hannibal, an occasion arose to bring out into open discussion the estrangement that had been quietly festering between them.

In the course of this, Hannibal offered several explanations, but at last, finding his arguments unavailing, he came out with the following.

He said that at the time when his father was about to set out on his expedition into Iberia with his forces, he himself was nine years old, and that as his father was sacrificing to Zeus he stood beside the altar.

When the omens proved favorable, his father poured the libation to the gods and performed the customary rites, then bade the others present at the sacrifice withdraw a little, and calling Hannibal to him, asked him kindly whether he wished to set out with him on the campaign.

When the boy eagerly assented and even begged, as children will, to be taken along, his father took him by the right hand, led him up to the altar, and bade him lay his hand on the sacrificial victims and swear that he would never be a friend to the Romans.

Knowing this clearly, then, Hannibal urged Antiochus that, so long as he was planning anything hostile against the Romans, he should take heart and trust him, considering that he had in Hannibal a most genuine ally;

but that if ever Antiochus should make a settlement or friendship with them, then he need wait for no slander, but should distrust Hannibal and be on his guard against him—

# Book 3 Chapter 12: Statesmen Must Pay Attention to Motives

—for there was nothing Hannibal would not do against the Romans, if it lay in his power. Antiochus, hearing this and judging that it had been spoken with real feeling and sincerity, set aside all the suspicion he had held before.

As for the hostility of Hamilcar and his whole purpose, this must be regarded as undeniable evidence, as the facts themselves also made plain.

For he prepared such enemies of the Romans in Hasdrubal, his daughter's husband, and in his own natural son Hannibal, that no greater degree of hatred could be left to imagine.

Hasdrubal, dying before his time, did not make his purpose evident to everyone; but circumstances gave Hannibal abundant opportunity to display, all too fully, the hatred of Rome he had inherited from his father.

For this reason, those placed in charge of public affairs ought to attend to nothing more than discerning the true intentions of those who lay aside their enmities or form friendships—whether they are making their agreements as a concession to circumstances or because their spirit has truly been defeated—

so that they may be constantly on guard against the former, regarding them as men lying in wait for an opportunity, while trusting the latter as genuine subjects or friends and giving them, without hesitation, whatever orders the occasion may require.

These, then, are to be considered the causes of the war with Hannibal. As for the beginnings, which I am about to relate...

# Book 3 Chapter 13: Hannibal Succeeds to Command in Spain

...The Carthaginians had taken hard their defeat over Sicily, but their anger was intensified still further, as I said above, by the affair of Sardinia and by the size of the sum of money imposed on them at the end.

For this reason, as soon as they had brought most of Iberia under their control, they were ready to seize on anything that offered a chance against the Romans.

So when the death of Hasdrubal occurred—the man to whom they had entrusted affairs in Iberia after the death of Hamilcar—they at first waited to see which way the armies would lean.

But when news came from the camps that the forces had with one accord chosen Hannibal as their commander, the Carthaginians at once convened the popular assembly and by a unanimous vote ratified the army's choice.

As soon as Hannibal had taken over the command, he set out at once to subdue the tribe of the Olcades; and arriving before Althaea, their most important city, he made camp.

After this, by means of vigorous and terrifying assaults, he quickly took the city. When this happened, the rest of the tribe, struck with terror, surrendered themselves to the Carthaginians.

Having exacted money from the cities and gained control of large funds, he came to New Carthage to winter.

Treating those under him generously—paying part of the wages owed to the men serving with him and promising the rest—he instilled in his forces great goodwill and high hopes...

# Book 3 Chapter 14: Hannibal Attacks the Vaccaei

...for the future. In the following summer he set out again, this time against the Vaccaei. He took Helmantica (Salmantica) by storm at the first assault, but Arbucala—because of the city's size, its large population, and the bravery of its inhabitants—he captured by force only after a laborious siege.

After this, on his march back, he unexpectedly found himself in the gravest danger, when the Carpesii—who are just about the strongest tribe in those regions—rushed upon him.

Their neighbors gathered together with them as well, stirred up chiefly by the exiles of the Olcades, but further inflamed by those who had escaped from Helmantica.

Had the Carthaginians been forced to risk a pitched battle against these foes, they would beyond question have been defeated.

As it was, Hannibal acted with skill and good sense: he wheeled about and withdrew, placing the river called the Tagus as a barrier in front of him, and arranged the engagement around the crossing of the river, using as his allies both the river itself and the elephants he had, about forty in number. As a result, the whole affair turned out, against expectation, just as he had reasoned it would.

For when the barbarians attempted to force a crossing of the river at several points, the greater part of them were destroyed at the places where they emerged, as the elephants moved along the bank and caught those coming out of the water one after another;

and many were killed in the river itself by the cavalry, since the horses were better able to hold their ground against the current, and the horsemen had the advantage of higher ground in fighting against the foot soldiers.

Finally, Hannibal's men crossed over to the other side in their turn, fell upon the barbarians, and routed more than a hundred thousand of them.

After their defeat, no one south of the river Iber any longer dared readily to look him in the face—except the Saguntines.

From this city Hannibal tried his best to keep his distance, wishing to give the Romans no acknowledged pretext for war until he had securely brought everything else under his control, in keeping with the instructions and counsels of his father Hamilcar.
