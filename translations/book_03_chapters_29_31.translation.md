# Book 3 Chapter 29: The Roman Case For War

I have now set out what the Carthaginians said on that occasion; next I will give the arguments put forward by the Romans. They did not in fact make use of these at the time, because of their anger at the destruction of Saguntum, but the arguments were voiced often and by many men among them.

First, they said that the agreement made with Hasdrubal could not be set aside, as the Carthaginians presumed to claim it could. For it did not include the clause—

—that the treaty of Lutatius had included, namely that "these terms shall be binding only if the Roman people also approve them." Instead, Hasdrubal made the agreement on his own full authority, and in it stood the provision that "the Carthaginians shall not cross the river Iber for the purpose of war."

Furthermore, in the treaty concerning Sicily there was a written clause—as the Carthaginians themselves admit—that "the allies of each party shall enjoy security from the other." This did not apply only to those who were allies at that time, as the Carthaginians construed it.

For if it had, there would have been an added clause either forbidding each side to take on additional allies beyond those they already had, or excluding from these terms any allies acquired later.

But since neither of these was written in, it was plain that all the allies of both parties—both those existing at the time and those to be taken on afterward—were always to enjoy security from both sides.

And this surely seems entirely reasonable. For they were hardly going to make a treaty that stripped them of their own freedom to take on, as occasions arose, any who might prove to be suitable friends and allies;

nor, having taken such people under their protection, would they then stand by and watch them be wronged by anyone.

Rather, the gist of what both parties had in mind in the treaty was this: with regard to the allies that both sides already had, each would keep its hands off them and in no way receive any of the other's allies into its own alliance;

and with regard to those who would be taken on later, the point was precisely the same—that neither side should recruit mercenaries or levy contributions in the other's provinces and allied territories, and that all should enjoy security from both sides.

# Book 3 Chapter 30: Both Sides Are in the Wrong

Given that this was the situation, it was also a matter of agreement that the Saguntines had placed themselves under Roman protection many years before the time of Hannibal.

The clearest proof of this—and one acknowledged even by the Carthaginians themselves—was that when the Saguntines fell into civil strife among themselves, they entrusted the matter not to the Carthaginians, although these were close at hand and were already active in Iberia, but to the Romans, and through them they set their political affairs right.

Therefore, if one regards the destruction of Saguntum as the cause of the war, it must be granted that the Carthaginians began the war unjustly—both in light of the treaty of Lutatius, under which the allies of each party were to have security from the other, and in light of the treaty of Hasdrubal, under which the Carthaginians were not to cross the river Iber for the purpose of war.

But if one regards the seizure of Sardinia and the money exacted along with it as the cause, then it must be fully admitted that the Carthaginians had good reason for waging the Hannibalian war: for having yielded to circumstance at the time, they took the opportunity, when circumstance favored them, to defend themselves against those who had injured them.

# Book 3 Chapter 31: The Value of Such Discussions

Some who consider such matters without discernment might perhaps say that there is no real need for us to go into such precise detail about arguments of this kind.

For my part, if anyone supposed himself to be self-sufficient in the face of every circumstance, I would say that knowledge of the past, while a fine thing, is perhaps not essential.

But since no human being would dare to make such a claim about either his private or his public affairs—because even if he is prosperous in the present, no one of sound mind can reasonably guarantee anything about the future on the basis of the present—then for these reasons I say that knowledge of the past is not only a fine thing,

but all the more essential.

For how could a man—whether wronged himself or with his country being wronged—find helpers and allies, or, if he were eager to gain something and take the initiative, rouse others to join him in his undertakings?

And how, if he were content with the present state of affairs, could he rightly spur on those who would secure his policy and preserve the established order, if he knew nothing from the record of what each party had done in the past?

For with an eye always to the present, everyone adapts and plays a part, saying and doing such things that each man's true purpose is hard to discern, and in many cases the truth is heavily obscured.

But the deeds of the past, which are tested by the facts themselves, truly reveal the choices and judgments of each person, and show us among whom we have gratitude, kindness, and aid to expect, and among whom the opposite.

From this record one can often find, in many situations, the man who will show pity, the man who will share our indignation, and the man who will see justice done.

These things offer the greatest help, both to communities and to individuals, in human life.

This is why neither those who write history nor those who read it should concern themselves so much with the bare narrative of the events themselves as with what preceded, accompanied, and followed them.

For if you take away from history the why, the how, and the purpose for which something was done, and whether its outcome was reasonable, what is left of it becomes a mere display rather than a lesson;

and though it gives pleasure for the moment, it is of no use whatever for the future.
