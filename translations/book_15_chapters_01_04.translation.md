# Book 15 Chapter 1: Speech of Roman Envoys At Carthage

I. Affairs in Italy and Africa

When the Carthaginians seized the Roman transport ships, along with a vast quantity of supplies, Scipio was deeply angered, not only because those provisions had been taken from his own side, but because the enemy had been furnished with an abundant store of necessities.

He was even more indignant because the Carthaginians had broken their oaths and their treaty, and had started the war again from the beginning.

So he immediately chose Lucius Sergius, Lucius Baebius, and Lucius Fabius as envoys and sent them to speak to the Carthaginians about what had happened, while also announcing that the Roman people had ratified the treaty.

For letters had just reached Scipio making this clear.

When the envoys arrived at Carthage, they were brought first before the senate and then before the people, and in both settings they spoke openly about the situation.

They first reminded them that the Carthaginian ambassadors, when they had come to Tunes and entered the Roman council, had not merely appealed to the gods and kissed the ground, as men commonly do,

but had actually thrown themselves flat on the earth in a shameful way and kissed the feet of those sitting in council. After that, standing up again, they had accused themselves, admitting that they themselves had broken the treaties first made between Romans and Carthaginians.

Therefore, they said, they were not unaware that any punishment the Romans chose to inflict would be deserved; yet, for the sake of the changes of human fortune, they begged not to suffer anything beyond remedy, since their own folly would provide proof of Roman nobility and generosity.

Remembering all this, the envoys said, their general and all who had been present in that council were astonished that the Carthaginians could forget what they themselves had said then, and now dare to violate their oaths and treaty.

This, they said, could only mean that they were trusting in Hannibal and the forces with him, and in that they were badly mistaken.

For everyone knew perfectly well that for two years already he and his army had been driven from all Italy and shut up near the Lacinian promontory, almost under siege, and had now barely escaped with their lives.

And even if he had come as a conqueror, they said, and even if he were about to fight men who had already beaten the Carthaginians in two successive battles, they still ought to hold uncertain hopes about the future, and think not only about victory but also about defeat.

If that happened, what gods would they call upon?

With what words would they try to draw pity from the victors for their disasters? By their faithlessness and folly, they would cut themselves off from every hope, both from gods and from men.

# Book 15 Chapter 2: Treacherous Attempt on the Lives of the Roman Envoys

After speaking in this way, the envoys withdrew.

Among the Carthaginians only a few were willing to keep the agreement. Most of those active in politics and in the senate resented the terms of the treaty, were offended by the frankness of the envoys, and were unwilling to give back the captured ships and the provisions taken from them.

What chiefly sustained them was not a small hope but a great one: they believed they could still win by means of Hannibal.

So the general vote was to send the envoys away without any answer. But the political leaders who were determined, by any means at all, to throw the war back into confusion met together and devised the following treachery.

They said that proper care should be taken for the envoys, so that they might return safely to their own camp.

They therefore at once prepared two triremes to escort them, but also sent instructions to Hasdrubal the admiral to keep ships ready not far from the Roman camp, so that once the escort vessels had left the Romans, he could swoop down and sink them.

For the Carthaginian fleet was lying off the coast near Utica.

After arranging this with Hasdrubal, they sent the Romans off and instructed the officers of the triremes to abandon them and sail away as soon as they had passed the river Macara.

From that point the Roman camp could already be seen.

So, once they had gone beyond the river as agreed, the escorting crews saluted the Romans and turned back.

Lucius and his companions suspected nothing serious. They were merely annoyed, thinking the escorts had left them early out of carelessness or disrespect.

But as soon as they were sailing alone, three Carthaginian triremes came darting out from ambush and attacked. They could not ram and disable the Roman quinquereme, because it kept backing water and avoiding the blow, nor could they board it, because the men defended themselves bravely.

Still, by coming alongside and fighting all around the ship, they wounded the marines and killed many of them.

At last the Romans saw men from their own camp, who had been foraging along the coast, rushing down to help from the shore, and so they ran their ship aground.

Most of those aboard were killed, but the envoys themselves were saved in a most unexpected way.

# Book 15 Chapter 3: Last Year of the Second Punic War

These events gave the war a new beginning, harsher and more bitter than before.

The Romans, believing that the treaty had been treacherously violated, were fiercely determined to overcome the Carthaginians; and the Carthaginians, conscious of what they had done, were ready for anything rather than fall into enemy hands.

With both sides so disposed, it was obvious that the present crisis would have to be settled by battle.

As a result, not only everyone in Italy and Libya but also those in Iberia, Sicily, and Sardinia were left hanging in suspense, anxiously waiting to see what would happen.

At this time Hannibal, being short of cavalry, sent messages to a Numidian named Tychaeus, a friend of Syphax who was said to command some of the finest mounted troops in Libya.

He urged him to come to his aid and seize the opportunity, since, as he clearly knew, if the Carthaginians won he would be able to preserve his own power, but if the Romans prevailed he would put even his life in danger because of Massanissa’s ambition.

Tychaeus was persuaded by this appeal and came to Hannibal with two thousand cavalry.

# Book 15 Chapter 4: Scipio Sends the Envoys Home

After securing his naval force and leaving Baebius as his lieutenant, Scipio himself moved through the cities.

This time he no longer accepted into protection those who willingly surrendered themselves, but took the cities by force, sold their inhabitants into slavery, and made his anger visible because the Carthaginians had broken faith.

He kept sending messages to Massanissa, making clear how the Phoenicians had violated the truce and urging him to gather as large a force as possible and join him quickly.

For Massanissa, as was said earlier, had set out as soon as the treaty was made, taking his own troops along with ten Roman standards of cavalry and infantry and certain envoys from Scipio, in order not only to recover his ancestral kingdom but, with Roman help, to add Syphax’s realm as well. And this in fact came to pass.

It happened at the same time that the envoys sent from Rome sailed into the Roman naval camp.

Baebius immediately sent the Roman envoys on to Scipio, but kept the Carthaginian envoys under guard. They were thoroughly dispirited and thought themselves in extreme danger.

For when they learned of the outrage their countrymen had committed against the Roman ambassadors, they expected that the punishment for it would fall on them.

But Scipio, when he heard from the men just arrived that both senate and people at Rome had warmly approved the treaty he had made with the Carthaginians and were ready to grant all he requested, was greatly pleased.

He then ordered Baebius to send the Carthaginian envoys home with every mark of humanity, a decision that seems to me both admirable and prudent.

For knowing how highly his own country valued good faith toward envoys, he considered not so much what the Carthaginians deserved to suffer as what the Romans ought to do.

So, restraining his own anger and the bitterness caused by what had happened, he tried, as the proverb says, to preserve the fine traditions handed down by his fathers.

In this way he conquered all the Carthaginians in spirit, and Hannibal himself as well, by setting Roman nobility above their folly.
