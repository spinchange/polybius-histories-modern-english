# Book 2 Chapter 29: A Peculiar and Surprising Battle

This was the way the engagement unfolded. In the first place, since the battle was made up of three separate forces, it is clear that the spectacle, as well as the actual fighting of the troops drawn up in this fashion, must have looked strange and out of the ordinary.

In the second place, one might well be uncertain — both now and at the time, for anyone present at the very moment — whether the Celts held the most perilous position of all, with the enemy pressing in on them from both directions at once,

or, on the contrary, the most advantageous one: fighting against both forces simultaneously, yet securing their own safety from either side by means of the men at their backs, and, most important of all, having every avenue of retreat to the rear cut off, along with any hope of survival should they give way. For this is precisely the special quality of a formation that faces two ways.

Such, indeed, is the advantage of a double-fronted line of battle.

As for the Romans, on the one hand they were emboldened by having caught the enemy in the middle and surrounded them on every side; on the other hand they were unnerved by the splendor and the din of the Celtic army.

For the number of horn-blowers and trumpeters was beyond counting, and when, along with these, the whole army joined in raising its war cry together, the clamor that arose was so great and of such a character that not only the trumpets and the men, but even the surrounding country, seemed to echo back and to be giving off a voice of its own.

Terrifying too were the appearance and the gestures of the naked warriors who stood in the front line, men in the very prime of their strength and conspicuous for their fine physique.

And all those who held the foremost companies were adorned with gold torques and armlets.

At the sight of all this the Romans were on the one hand dismayed, but on the other, drawn on by the hope of profit, they were spurred on twice as keenly toward the

danger. But as soon as the javelin-throwers advanced in front of the Roman legions, in keeping with their custom, and let fly with vigorous and densely massed volleys of missiles, the Celts in the rear ranks found their cloaks and trousers of great use to them;

but for the naked men standing in front, the outcome turned out contrary to their expectation, and what happened caused them great difficulty and helplessness.

For since the Gallic shield could not cover the whole man, the more the bodies were exposed and the larger they were, the more readily the missiles found their mark.

In the end, unable to ward off the men hurling the javelins because of the distance, and overwhelmed by the multitude of falling missiles, they were utterly distressed and at a loss in their situation. Some of them, out of rage and recklessness, flung themselves blindly upon the enemy and, giving themselves up willingly, perished; others, drawing back step by step toward their own friends and plainly showing their cowardice, threw the men behind them into disorder.

In this way the spirit of the Gaesatae was broken down by the javelin-throwers.

But the main body of the Insubres, Boii, and Taurisci — once the Romans had taken back their own javelin-throwers and the companies charged at them — clashed with the enemy and put up a stubborn hand-to-hand fight.

For though they were being cut down, they held firm in spirit on equal terms, inferior only in this one respect — both as a whole and man for man — namely in the construction of their weapons.

The Roman shields, that is, offered far greater protection, and their swords were far more effective in action, while the Gallic sword could only deliver a downward cut.

And when the Roman cavalry charged in from higher ground against the flank, riding down from the hill and pressing the attack vigorously, then the Celtic infantry were cut to pieces right where they stood in their line, while their cavalry took to flight.
# Book 2 Chapter 31: Aemilius Returns Victorious

About forty thousand of the Celts were killed, and no fewer than ten thousand taken prisoner, among them one of their kings, Concolitanus.

The other king, Aneroestes, fled with a few men to a certain place and there laid hands on himself and on his close companions.

The Roman general gathered the spoils and sent them to Rome, and restored the plunder to its rightful owners.

Then, taking up his legions and marching along the very edge of Liguria, he invaded the territory of the Boii. After satisfying the legions' appetite for plunder, within a few days he returned with his forces to Rome.

He adorned the Capitol with the standards and the torques — a torque being the gold collar that the Gauls wear around their necks —

while the rest of the spoils and the captives he used for his own entry into the city and for the decoration of his triumph.

So this, the most formidable Celtic onslaught, was destroyed in this manner — an invasion that had hung over all the Italians, and especially the Romans, as a great and fearful danger.

Emboldened by this success, the Romans came to hope that they would be able to drive the Celts out of the regions around the Padus altogether. They therefore sent out both of the consuls appointed for the following year, Quintus Fulvius and Titus Manlius, together with their forces and large-scale preparations, against the Celts.

These consuls, by terrifying the Boii in a sudden assault, forced them to put themselves under Rome's protection; but for the remainder of the campaign,

with extraordinary rainstorms setting in, and on top of that an outbreak of plague befalling them,

they accomplished nothing in the end. After these men, Publius Furius and Gaius Flaminius were appointed and once again invaded Celtic territory, marching through the country of the Anares, who happen to live not far from Massalia.

Having won this people over to friendship, they crossed into the land of the Insubres at the confluence of the rivers Adua and Padus.

After suffering some losses both during the crossing and around their camp, they stayed put for the moment; but afterward they made a truce and withdrew from the region by agreement.

Then, after marching around for several days and crossing the river Clusius, they came into the country of the Cenomani, and taking these people along as allies, they invaded once more — descending from the Alpine regions into the plains of the Insubres — and laid waste the land and plundered their settlements.

The chiefs of the Insubres, seeing that the Romans' purpose was unshakable, decided to put their fortune to the test and to risk a full, decisive battle against them.

So they gathered all their available forces in one place, took down the golden standards known as "the immovables" from the temple of Athena, made the rest of their preparations as needed, and then, with confidence and in formidable array, encamped opposite the enemy, numbering some fifty thousand men.

The Romans, on the one hand, seeing that they were far outnumbered by their opponents, wished to make use of the forces of the Celts allied with them;

but on the other hand, reflecting on Gallic faithlessness and on the fact that they were about to fight against men of the same race as these auxiliaries, they were wary of sharing such a moment and such an undertaking with men of that sort.

In the end, then, they themselves remained on their own side of the river, while the Celts who were with them they sent across to the far bank, and then tore up the bridges over the stream —

at once securing themselves against those allies, and at the same time leaving themselves only one hope of survival, namely in victory, since the aforementioned river lay impassable at their backs.

Having done this, they were ready to risk the battle.
# Book 2 Chapter 33: Tactics Against the Gauls

The Romans are thought to have handled this battle intelligently, for the military tribunes showed how the fighting ought to be conducted, both collectively and by each man individually.

For they had observed from earlier engagements that the whole Gallic race is most fearsome in its first charge, in its fury, so long as that fury is still fresh;

and that their swords, by their construction, as has been said before, have only one effective downward stroke — the first — but after that they immediately lose their edge, bending both lengthwise and crosswise so badly that, unless the men are given time to straighten them by setting them against the ground with the foot, their second blow is utterly useless.

So the tribunes distributed the spears of the triarii, who stood in reserve behind, to the men in the front companies, and ordered them to use their swords only after switching weapons; and they engaged the Celts in pitched battle, facing them head-on.

As soon as the Gauls made their swords useless by their first downward strokes against the spears, the Romans closed to hand-to-hand range and rendered the Celts helpless, robbing them of their slashing style of fighting — which is peculiar to Gallic warfare — since their sword has no point for thrusting at all.

The Romans, by contrast, used their swords not for cutting but for thrusting, holding them straight, since the point on them is effective; and striking at the chests and faces and landing blow upon blow, they killed the greater part of those drawn up against them, thanks to the foresight of the tribunes.

For the consul Flaminius is thought not to have made good use of the engagement described above. By forming his line right along the very brow of the river, he ruined what is distinctive about Roman tactics, leaving no room for the companies to fall back step by step.

For if the men had been pressed back even slightly from their ground during the battle, they would have had to throw themselves into the river, all because of their commander's blunder.

Nevertheless, by their own valor they won a decisive victory, as I have said, and after taking abundant plunder and no small amount of spoils, they returned to Rome.
# Book 2 Chapter 34: Capture of Mediolanum and End of the War

In the following year, when the Celts sent an embassy seeking peace and promising to do anything, the newly appointed consuls Marcus Claudius and Gnaeus Cornelius pressed hard that no peace be granted them.

The Celts, having failed in this and resolving to put their last hopes to the test, once more set about hiring about thirty thousand of the Gaesatae Gauls who live around the Rhone. Having taken these into service, they kept them at the ready and awaited the enemy's attack.

The Roman generals, when the season came, took up their forces and led them into the country of the Insubres.

Arriving there and encamping around the city of Acerrae, which lies between the Padus and the Alpine mountains, they laid siege to it.

The Insubres, unable to bring relief because the favorable positions had already been seized, but eager to break the siege of Acerrae, sent a portion of their force across the Padus into the country of the Anares and laid siege to the place called Clastidium. When news of what was happening reached the generals,

Marcus Claudius took the cavalry and some of the infantry and hurried off, eager to relieve the besieged.

The Celts, learning of the enemy's arrival, raised the siege, came out to meet them, and drew up for battle.

When the Romans, with the cavalry alone, boldly charged them in a sudden assault, they held out at first; but afterward, with the enemy surrounding them both at the rear and on the flank, they were hard-pressed in the fighting and were finally routed by the cavalry alone.

Many of them fell into the river and were destroyed by the current, while the greater number were cut down by the enemy.

The Romans also captured Acerrae, full of grain, after the Gauls withdrew to Mediolanum, which is the principal stronghold of the Insubres' territory.

Gnaeus followed close on their heels and made a sudden assault on Mediolanum; at first the Gauls kept quiet,

but when he started back again toward Acerrae, they sallied out, boldly fell upon his rearguard, killed many men, and even forced a part of his force to flee,

until Gnaeus called back the men from the vanguard and urged them to stand and engage the enemy.

The Romans, obeying their general, fought stoutly against those pressing on them.

The Celts, on account of their present success, held their ground confidently for a while, but before long they were routed and fled to the foothills. Gnaeus pursued them, ravaged the countryside, and
# Book 2 Chapter 35: Character of the Gauls

took Mediolanum by storm. When this happened, the chiefs of the Insubres, despairing of any hope of safety, surrendered everything they had to the Romans.

Such, then, was the outcome of the war against the Celts. In terms of the desperate fury and daring of the men who fought it, and further in terms of the battles and the number of those killed and engaged in them, it was inferior to none recorded in history;

but in terms of strategic planning and the lack of judgment in the conduct of its individual stages, it was utterly contemptible — because not the greater part, but absolutely everything the Gauls did was governed by passion rather than by reason.

Having observed for myself how these people were driven out of the plains around the Padus not long afterward — except for a few localities lying right at the foot of the Alps — I decided that I ought not to leave unrecorded either their original invasion, or their subsequent actions, or their final expulsion,

believing it proper to the work of history to bring such episodes of Fortune into memory and to hand them down to those who come after,

so that our descendants may not be utterly ignorant of these things and thus be terrified by the sudden and unexpected attacks of the barbarians, but rather, bearing in mind to some degree how short-lived and how easily broken the barbarian host is, may withstand their onslaught — those who face danger with judgment — and may exhaust all their own resources before yielding any of what is essential.

For I believe that those who recorded for us, and handed down to memory, the Persians' invasion of Greece and the Gauls' invasion of Delphi contributed in no small way — indeed greatly — to the struggles waged for the common freedom of the Greeks.

For no one, dismayed by an abundance of supplies or arms or men, would abandon his last hope — that of fighting to the end for his own land and homeland — if he kept before his eyes the astonishing nature of what happened then, and remembered how many myriads of men, what daring, and how vast the preparations were that the resolve and power of those who faced danger with judgment and calculation brought to nothing.

And since the dread of the Gauls has dismayed the Greeks repeatedly, not only in ancient times but even down to our own day,

I was all the more moved to give an account of these matters — summary in form, yet reaching back to the beginning.
