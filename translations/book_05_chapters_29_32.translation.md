# Book 5 Chapter 29: Disturbed State of Achaia

There was therefore open insolence toward Aratus among them. The Aetolians, meanwhile, were eager on the one hand to make peace, being hard pressed by the war and finding that events were turning out quite differently from what they had expected. For they had assumed that, because of his youth and inexperience, they would be dealing with a mere child in Philip; instead they found him a fully developed man, both in the breadth of his plans and in the execution of them, while they themselves showed themselves contemptible and childish alike in the details and in the general conduct of affairs.

Then, when they were also thrown into confusion by the uproar among the peltasts and by the destruction of the men around Apelles and Leontius, they hoped that there must be some great and serious disturbance at court, and so they kept putting off the day appointed for the meeting at Rhium.

Philip, gladly seizing this excuse because he was confident about the war and had already decided to reject any settlement, then urged the allies who had come to him not to deal with peace but with war, and after putting out to sea he sailed back to Corinth.

He dismissed all the Macedonians through Thessaly to winter at home, and himself, after setting sail from Cenchreae and being carried along the coast of Attica through the Euripus, sailed into Demetrias; and there he had Ptolemy, the last remaining man from the circle around Leontius, tried before a court of Macedonians and put to death.

At this same time Hannibal, having invaded Italy, was facing the Roman forces near the river called the Padus; Antiochus, after subduing most of Coele-Syria, had once more disbanded his army for the winter; and King Lycurgus of the Lacedaemonians fled to Aetolia in terror of the ephors.

For the ephors, on receiving a false accusation that he was planning some revolutionary move, gathered the young men and came by night to his house. He, having learned of it in advance, slipped away with his household servants.

# Book 5 Chapter 30: Aratus Elected Strategus

When winter had come on and King Philip had withdrawn to Macedonia, Eperatus, the Achaean strategus, was despised by the politically active young men and completely disregarded by the mercenaries. No one obeyed orders, and nothing was ready for the defense of the country.

Seeing this, Pyrrhias, whom the Aetolians had sent as commander to the Eleans, had with him 1,300 Aetolians, the Eleans’ mercenaries, and also 1,000 citizen infantry and 200 cavalry, making a total of 3,000.

With this force he not only kept plundering the land of Dyme and Pharae, but also that of Patrae. At last he encamped on what is called Mount Panachaean, which stands above the city of Patrae, and laid waste the whole district sloping toward Rhium and toward Aegium.

As a result, the cities, suffering badly and getting no help, found it difficult to meet their contributions; and the soldiers, since their pay was constantly being delayed and held back, acted in much the same way when called upon for relief.

Because the two sides kept retaliating in this way, affairs went from bad to worse, and in the end the mercenary force broke up altogether. All this happened because of the incompetence of the man in charge.

When the Achaean situation had reached this state and the time for the elections had arrived, Eperatus laid down his office, and the Achaeans chose Aratus the elder as their strategus at the beginning of summer.

Such was the situation in Greece. Since we have reached the proper point, both in time and in the order of events, we shall now move on to the actions in Asia that were completed in the same Olympiad, and speak of them in turn.

# Book 5 Chapter 31: Return to Narrative of the War in Coele-Syria

I shall now resume the narrative, and first I shall try, in accordance with my original plan, to set forth the war between Antiochus and Ptolemy over Coele-Syria.

I do so even though I am well aware that at the point in our Greek narrative where we have now arrived, that war was on the verge of being decided and brought to its conclusion. But I have deliberately chosen this kind of pause and division in the account.

My reason is that I am convinced I can help readers avoid mistakes about the sequence of events if, as I tell the story, I indicate the years of this Olympiad in which events in each part of the world, and in Greece, began and ended.

For I think nothing is more necessary for the clarity of the history of this Olympiad than not to mix the various narratives together, but to separate and divide them as far as possible, until we come to the following Olympiads and begin recording the events year by year in proper sequence.

For since I have chosen not to write a local history but a universal one, and have embarked on a scale of history, as I have already shown, greater than that of my predecessors, I ought also to take greater care than they did in handling and arranging my material, so that my work may be clear both in detail and as a whole.

For that reason, even now, after making a brief return to the reigns of Antiochus and Ptolemy, I shall try to establish starting points that are agreed upon and recognized for what I am about to say—something that is necessary above all else.

# Book 5 Chapter 32: Dimidium Facti Qui Coepit Habet, Well Begun is Half Done

The ancients used to say that the beginning is half of the whole, and they urged that in every case one should take the greatest care to begin well.

They may have seemed to be speaking extravagantly, but in my view they have said less than the truth. One might confidently say not that the beginning is half of the whole, but that it stretches all the way to the end.

For how can anyone begin something well without first grasping in thought the completion of the whole plan, or without knowing where he is setting out, toward what goal, and for what purpose he is undertaking it?

And how can anyone properly summarize events without taking account of their beginning, showing from where, how, and why he has arrived at the present situation?

That is why, instead of thinking that beginnings extend only to the middle, we ought to think that they extend to the end, and to take the greatest care about them, both as speakers and as listeners, in matters of the whole. And that is what we shall now try to do.
