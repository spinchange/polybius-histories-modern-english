# Book 35 Chapter 1: The "Fiery War"

The war between the Romans and the Celtiberians was called the "fiery war," for it was unusually fierce and remarkable for the frequency of its battles.

As a rule, the wars in Greece and Asia are decided by a single battle, or at most by a second one, and the battle itself is usually settled by the first charge and clash of the armies.

In this war, however, the opposite happened.

As a rule, the dangers were brought to an end by nightfall, because the men would neither lose heart nor give way bodily to fatigue, but would again and again return with renewed resolve after a withdrawal and a change of mind.

Indeed, the whole war and the sequence of pitched battles were interrupted for a time by the winter.

In general, if anyone were to imagine a "fiery war," he could hardly think of any other than this one.

# Book 35 Chapter 2: The Celtiberian Wars

When the Celtiberians, after making terms with Marcus Claudius, the Roman general, sent embassies to Rome, they themselves remained quiet and waited for the Senate's decision.

Meanwhile Marcus campaigned against the Lusitanians, captured the city of Nercobrica by storm, and then wintered at Corduba.

When the envoys arrived in Rome, the Senate admitted those from the Belli and Titthi, who were on the Roman side, to the city;

but it ordered the envoys from the Arevacae to camp across the Tiber, because they were enemies, until the Senate should decide the whole matter.

When the time for the audience arrived, the praetor introduced the allied envoys first.

Though they were barbarians, they spoke at length and tried to make every point of dispute clear.

They pointed out that if the tribes who had gone to war were not brought under control and punished as they deserved, then as soon as the Roman armies returned from Iberia they would fall upon the Belli and Titthi at once, treating them as traitors;

and that if they escaped punishment for their first act of hostility, they would quickly set the whole of Iberia in motion, because everyone there would think them capable of standing up to Rome.

So they asked either that the legions remain in Iberia and that each year a consul be sent there to support Rome's allies and punish the Arevacae's wrongdoing,

or, if the armies were to be withdrawn, that the Senate first make the outbreak of those men a warning example, so that no one else would dare to do the same.

That, or something like it, was what the envoys of the Belli and Titthi, Rome's allies, said.

After that the envoys from the enemy were brought in.

The Arevacae came forward and, in their speech, affected submission and humility;

but their actual intentions clearly showed no surrender and no defeat.

For they were always hinting at the uncertainty of fortune, and by describing the battles that had already been fought as indecisive, they left the impression that in every case they had had the better of the contest.

The end of their speech was this: if they had to endure some fixed penalty for their ignorance, they were ready to accept it;

but once that was done, they asked to be restored to the terms of their agreement with the Senate under Tiberius Gracchus.

# Book 35 Chapter 3: The Senate's Instructions to Marcellus

The senators, after hearing both sides, then introduced the envoys from Marcellus.

But when they saw that these men too were inclined toward a settlement, and that the general was siding more with the enemy than with the allies,

they replied to the Arevacae and their allies that Marcellus would explain the Senate's decision to both parties in Iberia.

Privately, however, they were convinced that the allies were speaking truly and in their own interest, that the Arevacae were still full of pride, and that the commander was afraid to fight them;

so they gave the envoys from Marcellus secret instructions to carry on the war bravely and in a manner worthy of their country.

After they had thus resolved on war, they first decided to send another commander to Iberia, in place of Marcellus;

for by then the new consuls, Aulus Postumius and Lucius Licinius Lucullus, had taken office.

They then set about their preparations with enthusiasm and on a grand scale, thinking that the fate of Iberia would be decided by this campaign:

if the enemy were beaten, they believed that everyone would do what Rome ordered; but if the Arevacae succeeded in escaping the punishment hanging over them, not only would their own spirit revive, but so would that of all the other tribes as well.

# Book 35 Chapter 4: Scipio Volunteers For Spain

The more eagerly the Senate took up the war, the stranger the events became for them.

For Q. Fulvius Nobilior, who had commanded in Iberia the previous year, and the men who had served with him, reported in Rome the endless series of pitched battles, the great number of the dead, and the courage of the Celtiberians.

And since Marcellus was plainly shrinking from the war, a completely irrational panic seized the young men — one, the older men said, such as had never occurred before.

So far did this fear go that there were not enough men willing to come forward for the office of military tribune, and the posts were not completely filled, though in former times more men than were needed used to volunteer for them.

Nor would the men nominated by the consuls as legates to accompany the commanders serve.

And worst of all, the young men tried to evade the levies and came up with excuses that were shameful to make, improper to examine, and impossible to refute.

At last, when the Senate and the magistrates were at a loss to know what the end of this shamelessness among the young men would be — for they had no other name for it — Publius Cornelius Scipio Africanus,

who, though still young, had already been regarded as a leader in the war and had won a settled reputation for moral excellence and self-restraint,

but still lacked a reputation for personal bravery, saw the Senate's difficulty and stood up to say that he was willing to go to Iberia with the consuls either as a military tribune or as a legate, since he was ready for either role.

He added, however, that for his own part a mission to Macedonia would be both safer and more fitting,

for at that very time the Macedonians had in fact invited Scipio by name to come and settle the disputes raging among them.

But, he said, the needs of his own country were more urgent, and they called those who truly loved honour to Iberia.

This offer, unexpected from every point of view because of Scipio's age and his general caution, immediately won him great approval, and still more in the days that followed;

the men who had earlier shrunk from danger, in order to avoid the disgrace of being compared with him, now began volunteering for service, some offering to serve as legates to the commanders and others joining the military register in groups and customary circles.
