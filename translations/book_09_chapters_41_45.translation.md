# Book 9 Chapter 41: Investment of Echinus by Philip

Having decided to make his approach to the city opposite two towers, Philip constructed in front of them tortoise-shelters for earthworks and battering rams, and opposite the space between the towers he built a covered gallery between the rams running parallel to the wall.

When this design was completed, the outward appearance of the works came to resemble the arrangement of the city wall itself.

For the structures built on top of the tortoises took on the form and appearance of towers because of the way the wicker screens were fitted together, while the space between them looked like a wall because the upper wickerwork of the gallery had been woven into battlements.

In the lower part of the towers, the men who were filling in the uneven ground so that the ram-frames could advance kept throwing in earth, and the battering ram itself was pushed forward.

The second level contained jars of water, the equipment needed to fight fires, and the artillery besides.

On the third stood a large number of men posted to fight those who tried to damage the ram; and these were level in height with the towers of the city.

From the gallery between the towers double trenches were carried forward toward the space between the city towers.

And there were three artillery positions for stone-throwers, one of which discharged stones of a talent's weight, while the other two threw stones of thirty minas.

From the camp to the earthwork-tortoises roofed tunnels had been made, so that neither those coming from the camp to the works nor those returning from the works would suffer any harm from missiles shot from the city.

In very few days the entire work was completed, because the region had abundant supplies for everything needed in the operation.

For the Echinaians dwell on the Melian Gulf, facing south, opposite the territory of the Thronians, and they cultivate exceptionally fertile land. For this reason Philip lacked nothing needed for his assault.

And so, as I said, once the works were complete, they immediately brought up both the trenchworks and the siege engines.

# Book 9 Chapter 42: Asia and Egypt

Publius, the Roman commander, and Dorimachus, the Aetolian leader, arrived while Philip was besieging the city of Echinus, after he had secured his approaches to the wall well and fortified the outer face of his camp with trench and wall.

Publius came with a fleet and Dorimachus with infantry and cavalry, and when they attacked the entrenchment they were repulsed, since Philip fought the more vigorously.

The people of Echinus therefore despaired and surrendered themselves to Philip.

For Dorimachus and his force were unable, through lack of supplies, to reduce Philip, who was provisioning himself by sea.

When Aegina was taken by the Romans, the Aeginetans who had not escaped gathered by the ships and begged the commander to permit them to send envoys to kindred cities to arrange ransom.

At first Publius answered harshly, saying that when they were still masters of themselves, then was the time to send embassies to their betters about their safety—not now, when they had become slaves.

And if, a little before, they had not thought ambassadors sent by him worthy even of a hearing, how was it not absurd that now, when they were captives, they should demand to send envoys to their kinsmen?

So on that occasion he sent them away with those words. But on the following day he called all the prisoners together and said that he owed the Aeginetans no kindness; nevertheless, for the sake of the rest of the Greeks, he granted them leave to send envoys about ransom, since that was their custom.

# Book 9 Chapter 43: Nature of the Euphrates River

For the Euphrates takes its beginning in Armenia and flows through Syria and the lands beyond toward Babylonia.

It seems to discharge itself into the Red Sea, but that is not in fact so. Its waters are spent first on the channels led out into the countryside before it can make an outlet into the sea.

Thus it turns out that this river has a nature contrary to that of most rivers. Most streams grow larger the farther they pass through more regions, and they are highest in winter and lowest at the height of summer.

But the Euphrates is fullest at the rising of the Dog Star and greatest in the Syrian regions, and then continually grows smaller as it advances.

The reason is that its increase does not come from the gathering of winter rains, but from the melting of snow, while its decrease comes from the diversions made into the country and from its division for irrigation.

That is why on that occasion the transport of the army was slow: the boats were heavily loaded, the river was at its lowest, and the force of the current contributed only very little to the voyage.
