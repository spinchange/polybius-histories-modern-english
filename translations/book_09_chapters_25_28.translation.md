# Book 9 Chapter 25: Hannibal's Greed

He does seem, however, to have been markedly fond of money, and to have employed a money-loving associate, Mago, the man in charge of operations in Bruttium.

This account I received from the Carthaginians themselves.

For natives know best, as the proverb says, not only which way the winds are blowing, but also the character of the people among whom they live.

And I heard it even more precisely from Masannasus, who brought charges in general against all Carthaginians, but especially against Hannibal and Mago the so-called Samnite, for greed.

Among other things, he said that from early youth these men had divided their operations between themselves in a very grand fashion, and although each had taken many cities both in Iberia and in Italy—some by force and some by surrender—they never shared in the same action together.

Instead, they were always scheming more against each other than against the enemy, so that one might not be present when the other captured a city, and they might neither quarrel over such matters nor have to divide the profit, since their rank and standing were equal.

# Book 9 Chapter 26: Hannibal Treats Different Cities in Different Ways

That Hannibal's nature was often constrained and altered not only by the influence of his friends, but still more by the force of circumstances, is clear both from what has already been said and from what is about to be told.

For as soon as Capua came into Roman hands, the cities at once, and quite naturally, became unsettled and began looking for opportunities and pretexts to change sides back to Rome.

It was then, it seems, that Hannibal, under the greatest pressure, fell into deep perplexity about the situation before him.

For he could neither remain in one place and keep watch over all the cities, since they lay so far apart and the enemy were moving against him with several armies,

nor could he divide his own force into many parts, because he would thereby become easy prey for the enemy both from inferiority in numbers and from his inability to be personally present everywhere.

Accordingly he was compelled to abandon some cities openly and to withdraw garrisons from others, fearing that in the shifting course of events he might lose his own soldiers along with them.

Some cities he even treated with treacherous violence, transferring their inhabitants elsewhere and exposing their property to plunder.

Because of this, some men condemned him for impiety and others for cruelty.

For together with these measures there came robberies of money, murders, and acts of violence, on one pretext or another, committed both by soldiers going out of cities and by those entering them, since each side assumed that the inhabitants left behind were on the very point of going over to the enemy.

For these reasons it is extremely difficult to pronounce on Hannibal's true character, because of both the influence of his friends and the pressure of events.

The common report, however, was that among the Carthaginians he had the reputation of being greedy for money, and among the Romans of being cruel.

# Book 9 Chapter 27: Agrigentum

Let what has been said so far be enough on that subject. The city of the Agrigentines differs from most cities not only in the respects already mentioned, but also in strength, and especially in beauty and construction.

It is built eighteen stades from the sea, so that it lacks none of the advantages the sea provides.

Its circuit of walls is exceptionally secure both by nature and by art.

For the wall stands on a rock that is steep and broken all around, in some parts naturally so and in others made so by human labor, and the city is enclosed by rivers.

Along its southern side runs the river that bears the same name as the city, and along the side facing west and the southwest runs the river called Hypsas.

The citadel of the city lies above it toward the summer sunrise, enclosed on the outer side by an inaccessible ravine, and on the inner side having only one approach from the city.

On its summit stand a sanctuary of Athena and one of Zeus Atabyrius, as at Rhodes.

For since Acragas was colonized by Rhodians, it is natural that this god should bear the same title there as among the Rhodians.

The rest of the city too is splendidly adorned with temples and colonnades. And though the temple of Olympian Zeus has not been completely finished, in conception and size it seems inferior to none of those in Greece.

Polybius says that both the river and the city were called Acragas from the fertility of the land.

Marcus, after giving guarantees of safety, persuaded them to withdraw to Italy, on the condition that they should receive rations from the people of Rhegium while they ravaged Bruttium, and that they should keep whatever profit they won from enemy territory.

# Book 9 Chapter 28: Greece: Philip Reduces Thessaly

That the Macedonian domination, men of Lacedaemon, proved to be the beginning of slavery for the Greeks, I am persuaded no one would dare to deny; and you may consider the matter in this way.

There was a federation of Greeks in the Thracian region, colonized by Athenians and Chalcidians, among whom the city of Olynthus possessed the greatest standing and power.

When Philip enslaved it and made it an example, he became master not only of the cities in Thrace but also brought the Thessalians under his control through fear.

Not long afterward he defeated the Athenians in battle and behaved magnanimously in his success—not because he wished to benefit the Athenians, far from it, but in order that by his kindness toward them he might induce the rest to do willingly what he commanded.

At that time the prestige of your own city still remained, and it looked likely that in a suitable moment Sparta would place herself once more at the head of Greece.

Therefore Philip seized on every plausible pretext, came with his forces, and ravaged your land by cutting it down and by burning your houses.

In the end, after stripping you of both cities and countryside, he assigned part of your territory to the Argives, part to the Tegeans and Megalopolitans, and part to the Messenians, wishing to confer benefits on all of them, even against justice itself, on one condition only—that he should injure you.

Alexander succeeded him in power. And how he in turn destroyed Thebes, thinking that some small spark of Greek spirit still remained there, I suppose you all know well enough.
