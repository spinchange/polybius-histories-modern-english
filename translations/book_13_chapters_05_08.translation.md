# Book 13 Chapter 6: Nabis, Tyrant of Sparta, B.C. 207-192

Nabis, the tyrant of the Lacedaemonians, now in the third year of his rule, did not yet venture to undertake or attempt anything on a grand scale, because the defeat of Machanidas by the Achaeans was still recent,

but he was laying the groundwork and setting the foundation for a tyranny that would be long-lasting and oppressive.

He destroyed outright what remained in Sparta, drove into exile those who stood out most in wealth or ancestral distinction, and distributed their property and wives among the most prominent of the others and among his mercenaries.

These men were murderers, brigands, robbers, and burglars. In general, this was the class of people he carefully gathered to himself from the whole inhabited world—men for whom the land that had reared them was closed because of impiety and lawlessness.

By setting himself up as the patron and king of such men, and by using them as his spear-bearers and bodyguards, he plainly ensured that his rule and his reputation for impiety would endure for a long time.

Nor, apart from what has already been mentioned, was he satisfied with merely banishing citizens; there was no place safe for the exiles, no refuge they could rely on.

Some he had murdered by sending men after them on the roads; others he killed by dragging them back from the places to which they had fled.

In the end, in whatever cities one of the exiles happened to be living, he secretly rented the neighboring houses through men unlikely to arouse suspicion, and sent Cretans into them; these made openings in the walls and, shooting through existing windows,

destroyed some of the exiles while they were standing and others while they were lying in their own houses, so that there was no place of escape and no moment of safety for the wretched Lacedaemonians.

# Book 13 Chapter 7: Nabis's Wife

By this method he did away with most of them. He also had a certain device constructed—if indeed one ought to call it a device.

It was a female figure clothed in expensive garments and fashioned with extraordinary accuracy to resemble the wife of Nabis.

Whenever he summoned one of the leading citizens because he wanted to extract money from him, he began with lengthy and courteous speeches,

pointing to the danger hanging over the countryside and the city from the Achaeans, explaining the number of mercenaries maintained for their protection, and adding the expenses owed to the gods and to the public needs of the city.

If by such arguments they gave way, that was enough for his purpose.

But if any of them refused and tried to put off his demand, he would make a speech like this: “Perhaps I am not able to persuade you, but I think this Apéga here will persuade you.” Apéga was the name of Nabis’s wife.

When he had said this, the figure I mentioned a little earlier was brought in.

After greeting the man, the figure, once he had raised the woman from her seat and embraced her with his arms, would gradually draw him toward her breasts.

Its forearms and hands were full of iron spikes beneath the garments, and so were its breasts.

When Nabis pressed the hands against the back of the figure and then, by means of mechanical devices, tightened the embrace and little by little forced the victim against the breasts, he compelled the man under torture to utter anything at all.

In this way he destroyed many who refused his demands.

# Book 13 Chapter 8: Nabis Finds a Pretext for War

In all his other actions as ruler he was of the same sort and character as in these beginnings.

He joined with the Cretans in piracy at sea, and throughout the whole Peloponnese he maintained temple-robbers, highwaymen, and murderers; taking a share of the profits from their crimes, he offered Sparta to them as a base and refuge.

Now at this time some visitors from Boeotia who were staying in Lacedaemon persuaded one of Nabis’s grooms to leave with them, taking a white horse that was thought to be the finest in the tyrant’s stables.

When the man agreed and did as they asked, Nabis’s agents pursued them as far as Megalopolis and, finding them there, at once led away the horse and the groom—no one interfering—but after that they laid hands on the foreign visitors as well.

At first the Boeotians demanded to be taken before the magistrates; but when no one paid attention, one of the visitors shouted,

“Help!” The local people came running up and protested that the men should be brought before the authorities, so Nabis’s agents were forced to let the men go and depart.

Nabis, who had long been looking for grounds of accusation and a plausible pretext for conflict, seized on this occasion at once and raided the livestock of Proagoras and certain others. From this came the beginning of the war.
