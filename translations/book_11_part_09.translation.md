# Book 11 Chapter 30: Execution of the Ringleaders

Just as he was saying these things, the soldiers standing in arms around the assembly, acting on orders, clashed their swords against their shields, and at the same moment the men responsible for the mutiny were brought in naked and in chains.

Such terror seized the crowd from the fear all around them and the horrors before their eyes that, while some were being scourged and others beheaded, not one man changed expression or uttered a sound, but all stood gaping and stunned by what was happening.

So the ringleaders of the evil, after suffering torture, were dragged dead through the middle. The rest then received from the general and the other officers a public pledge

that no one would bear a grudge against anyone, and they themselves came forward one by one and swore before the tribunes that they would obey the commands of their officers and harbor no thought hostile to Rome.

Thus Publius, having rightly corrected the beginnings of a great danger, restored his forces once more to their original condition.

# Book 11 Chapter 31: Scipio Proposes to Fight Andobales

Publius at once assembled the troops at Carthage into a meeting and spoke about the boldness of Andobales and his treachery toward them.

By developing these points at length, he stirred the mass of the soldiers to eagerness for an attack on the dynasts mentioned.

Then he reviewed their earlier campaigns against Iberians and Carthaginians fighting together under Carthaginian commanders,

and said that, since they had always defeated them, it was not fitting for them now to hesitate at the thought of fighting Iberians alone under Andobales.

Accordingly he said he would not even accept any Iberian ally at all, but would bring the struggle on by means of Romans alone,

so that it might become plain to all that it was not by using Iberians against the Carthaginians, as some people say, that they had driven the Carthaginians out of Iberia, but that Roman valor and their own courage had defeated both Carthaginians and Celtiberians.

Having said this, he urged them to be of one mind and of good courage, and to go into this struggle as boldly as they would go against anyone else. As for victory, he said that with the gods' help he had taken all proper thought for securing it.

This inspired such eagerness and confidence in the troops that, simply from their appearance, they looked like men who already saw the enemy before them and were just about to engage.

# Book 11 Chapter 32: Scipio Defeats Andobales

After saying this, he dismissed the assembly. On the next day he broke camp and advanced; reaching the Ebro on the tenth day and crossing it, he encamped on the fourth day after that near the enemy, with a certain valley lying between his camp and theirs.

On the next day he drove some of the livestock that followed the army into the valley, after giving Gaius instructions to have the cavalry ready and ordering certain tribunes to prepare the javelin-fighters.

The Iberians quickly rushed upon the animals, and Scipio released some of the javelin-men. As these engaged, and more men came to the support of either side, a large skirmish of infantry developed in the valley.

When the moment supplied a good opening for attack, Gaius, having the cavalry ready as ordered, charged the skirmishers and cut them off from the foothills, so that most of them were scattered through the valley and destroyed by the cavalry.

This enraged the barbarians; and fearing that, because of their earlier defeat, they might seem utterly demoralized, they marched out at daybreak and drew up their whole force for battle.

Publius was ready for the engagement, but when he saw that the Iberians were descending irrationally into the valley and posting not only their cavalry but even their infantry on the level ground, he waited,

wanting as many of them as possible to make use of this position. He trusted his own cavalry, and still more his infantry, because in regular, close hand-to-hand fighting both the equipment and the men on his side were far superior to the Iberians.

# Book 11 Chapter 33: Scipio's Return To Rome

When it seemed to him that the proper moment had come, he drew up a counterforce against the enemy stationed on the foothills, but against those who had descended into the valley he led out the main body from the camp and attacked the enemy infantry with four cohorts.

At the same time Gaius Laelius, with the cavalry, moved forward over the hills that slope from the camp toward the valley, charged the Iberian horse in the rear, and occupied them in fighting him.

The enemy infantry, therefore, deprived of the support of the cavalry in reliance on which they had come down into the valley, were pressed and overwhelmed in the battle; and the cavalry suffered much the same.

Hemmed in within a narrow space and at a disadvantage, they were destroyed more by each other than by the enemy, as their own infantry pressed on them from the flank, the hostile infantry faced them in front, and the cavalry surrounded them from the rear.

In a battle that developed in this way, nearly all who had descended into the valley were destroyed, while those on the foothills escaped.

These were the light-armed troops, amounting to a third of the whole army, and with them Andobales too escaped alive and fled to some fortified place.

Publius, having brought his work in Iberia to completion, arrived at Tarraco in the highest spirits, bringing to his country a most splendid victory and the material for a splendid triumph.

Then, eager not to miss the settlement of the consuls at Rome, he put all Iberian affairs in order, handed the army over to Junius and Marcius, and himself sailed with Gaius and his other friends for Rome.
