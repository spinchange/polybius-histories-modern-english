# Book 6 Chapter 45: The Cretan Constitution Compared to the Spartan

Turning now to the Cretan constitution, two questions deserve attention: first, how the most learned ancient writers—Ephorus, Xenophon, Callisthenes, and Plato—could say that it was the same as the Spartan constitution; and second, how they could also praise it.

In my judgment neither claim is true.

This can be seen from the following points, and I will first explain how unlike the Spartan system it is. The first distinctive feature of the Spartan constitution, they say, concerns landed property: no citizen may possess more than another, but all must have an equal share in the civic land.

The second concerns wealth in money. Because money-making is held in disrepute there, the rivalry that grows out of having more or less is removed altogether from the state.

A third point is that among the Spartans the kings hold office for life by hereditary right, while the men called elders likewise hold office for life, and through them and with them the whole state is governed.

# Book 6 Chapter 46: Greed Among the Cretans

Among the Cretans the exact opposite of all this is true. Their laws allow them, as the saying goes, to acquire land without limit according to their power.

Money is held in such honor among them that possessing it seems not only necessary but positively admirable.

Indeed greed for base profit and grasping selfishness are so deeply rooted there that among the Cretans alone of all mankind no gain of any kind is considered shameful.

Their magistracies too are annual, and their system has a democratic character.

So I often wonder how anyone could describe constitutions that are so opposite in nature as if they were kindred and closely related.

And besides overlooking differences of such importance, these writers go on at length in praise of Lycurgus, saying that he alone among lawgivers grasped the essential point.

For since there are two things by which every constitution is preserved—courage against enemies and concord among citizens—they say that by abolishing greed he abolished at the same time all civil strife and faction.

That is why the Spartans, being free from such evils, became the best-governed and most united people in Greece in their relations with one another.

Yet though these same writers can see, by direct comparison, that the Cretans because of their inborn greed are constantly entangled in private and public seditions, murders, and civil wars, they still think this does not affect their argument and boldly say the two constitutions are alike.

Ephorus even uses almost the same language for both systems, so that if someone paid no attention to the proper names, he could hardly tell which constitution was being described.

So much, then, for the ways in which the two differ. I must now explain why the Cretan constitution is neither admirable nor worthy of imitation.

# Book 6 Chapter 47: What Makes a Constitution Good

I think there are two fundamental principles in every constitution, and by these its powers and structure become either desirable or objectionable: customs and laws.

Good customs and laws make men's private lives reverent and self-controlled, and make the public character of the city gentle and just; bad ones do the opposite.

So whenever we see excellent customs and laws among a people, we confidently conclude that the men themselves and their constitution will also be good.

And when we see private lives marked by greed and public conduct marked by injustice, it is plainly reasonable to say that their laws, customs, and whole political order are bad.

Now apart from a very few exceptions, one could hardly find private conduct more treacherous than that of the Cretans, or public measures more unjust.

Therefore, since their constitution is neither like the Spartan nor in any way worthy of choice or imitation, I reject it from this comparison.

Nor is it right to introduce Plato's republic either, even though some philosophers speak of it with admiration.

For just as we do not admit to athletic contests those performers or athletes who have neither won recognition nor undergone proper training, so we should not admit Plato's constitution to this contest for first place unless it can first demonstrate some real achievement in practice.

As things stand, to compare it with the constitutions of Sparta, Rome, and Carthage would be like setting up a statue beside living, breathing men and inviting comparison.

Even if the statue were flawless as a work of art, comparing the lifeless with the living would still strike the spectators as inadequate and entirely out of place.

# Book 6 Chapter 48: Institutions of Lycurgus

Leaving those aside, I return to the Spartan constitution.

It seems to me that Lycurgus legislated and planned so well for securing harmony among the citizens, preserving Laconia safely, and maintaining Sparta's freedom that one might think his insight something more than human.

Equality in property, simplicity and commonality in daily life were likely to make private conduct disciplined and the public order free from faction, while training men to endure labor and danger would make them brave and high-minded.

When courage and self-control come together in one soul or one city, vice can hardly grow from them, nor can such men easily be overcome by their neighbors.

By constructing his constitution on these principles, Lycurgus secured lasting safety for all Laconia and left the Spartans themselves a freedom that endured for a very long time.

But as for conquering neighboring peoples, gaining supremacy, and in general competing for wider power, he seems to me to have made no provision at all, either in particular measures or in the system as a whole.

What still remained was to create in the citizens some necessity or settled purpose that would make the common spirit of the city, as well as private life, self-sufficient and moderate.

In fact, although he made them utterly disinterested and sober-minded in their own personal lives and in the institutions of their own city, he left them toward the rest of Greece excessively ambitious, hungry for rule, and greedy for advantage.
