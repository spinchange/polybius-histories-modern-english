# Book 4 Chapter 81: Chilon's Fruitless Attempts In Sparta

At about this same time, while Philip was occupied with affairs in Triphylia, Chilon the Lacedaemonian—holding that the kingship belonged to him by right of birth, and bitterly resenting the slight that the Ephors had shown him in the decision regarding the kingship, when they awarded it to Lycurgus—set out to overturn the established order.

He reasoned that if he followed the same path as Cleomenes and held out to the common people the hope of land allotments and a redistribution of property, the masses would quickly fall in behind him; and so he launched into the enterprise.

Having conferred about the matter with his friends and won as many as two hundred men to share in the venture, he set about carrying his plan to completion.

But seeing that the greatest obstacle to his design was Lycurgus and the Ephors who had conferred the crown upon him, he directed his first attack against these men.

He caught the Ephors at dinner and slaughtered every one of them on the spot—Fortune thus exacting from them the penalty they had earned; for whether one considers the man at whose hands, or the man on whose account, they suffered this fate, one would have to admit that they suffered it justly.

After finishing with these men, Chilon went to the house of Lycurgus and found him inside, but was unable to lay hold of him.

For Lycurgus was smuggled out by some of his neighbors and slipped away unnoticed. He then withdrew by back-country tracks to the place in Tripolis called Pellene.

Chilon, having been foiled in the most crucial point of his attempt, was thoroughly dispirited, yet he was forced nonetheless to press on with what he had begun.

So he burst into the marketplace, laid violent hands on his enemies, appealed to his kinsmen and friends, and held out to the rest the hopes just described.

But when no one paid him any heed, and the people instead began to band together against him, he grasped what was happening, slipped away in secret, and making his way across the country arrived alone in Achaia, an exile. As for the Lacedaemonians,

terrified at the approach of Philip, they cleared away everything from the countryside, and after demolishing the Athenaeum of the Megalopolitans, abandoned it.

So then the Lacedaemonians—

who from the time of Lycurgus's legislation had enjoyed a most admirable constitution and held very great power down to the battle of Leuctra, but after whom Fortune turned against them, their political condition steadily and ever more sliding toward the worse—

in the end experienced a great many hardships and civil factions, struggled through countless redistributions of land and banishments, and tasted the most bitter servitude down to the tyranny of Nabis; they who in former days could scarcely bear to hear even the word "tyrant."

Now the ancient history of Sparta, and most of its later history, has been recounted by many writers on both sides of the question. But the clearest part of it begins from the point at which Cleomenes utterly abolished the ancestral constitution; and this I shall relate at the appropriate moments as they arise.

# Book 4 Chapter 82: Intrigue of Apelles Against Aratus

Philip, breaking camp from Megalopolis and marching by way of Tegea, arrived at Argos, where he spent the remainder of the winter, having won admiration beyond his years both for his general conduct and for his achievements in the campaigns described above.

Apelles, however, even so did not abandon his scheme, but was bent on bringing the Achaeans gradually under the yoke.

Seeing that Aratus and his circle stood in the way of such a design, and that Philip listened to them—and especially to the elder Aratus, both because of his earlier connection with Antigonus and because he wielded the greatest influence among the Achaeans, and above all because of the man's resourcefulness and good sense—he set about working against them and scheming in something like the following way.

Investigating who the political opponents of Aratus were, he drew each of them out from their cities, and once he had them in his grasp he flattered them and urged them into friendship with himself.

He also presented them to Philip, pointing out to him at every turn that, if he followed Aratus, he would have to deal with the Achaeans according to the written terms of the alliance; but that if he listened to Apelles and took on friends of this sort, he would be able to handle all the Peloponnesians as he pleased.

He also at once threw himself into the matter of the elections, wanting to secure the office of Strategus for one of his own party and to drive Aratus and his people out of their position of power.

For these reasons he persuaded Philip to come to the Achaean elections at Aegium, on the pretext that he was at the same time making his way toward Elis.

The king having agreed, Apelles was present himself at the critical moment, and partly by entreaty, partly by intimidation, he barely managed it—but in the end he prevailed in having Eperatus of Pharae made Strategus, and in having Timoxenus, the candidate put forward by Aratus and his circle, rejected.

# Book 4 Chapter 83: Philip Captures the Wall

After this the king broke camp and, marching by way of Patrae and Dyme, came to the fortress called the Wall, which lies on the frontier of the territory of Dyme; this had been seized a short time before, as I mentioned earlier, by Euripidas and his men.

Eager to recover it for the Dymaeans by any means, he encamped before it with his whole army.

The Elean garrison, struck with alarm, surrendered the fortress to Philip—a place not large, but exceptionally well fortified:

for its circumference was no more than a stade and a half, while the height of its wall was nowhere less than thirty cubits.

Having handed it over to the Dymaeans, he advanced and ravaged the territory of the Eleans; and after devastating it and carrying off a great quantity of plunder, he returned with his army to Dyme.

# Book 4 Chapter 84: Aratus Defends Himself

Meanwhile Apelles, supposing that he had accomplished part of his purpose now that the Achaean Strategus had been installed through his own efforts, again set to work against Aratus and his circle, wishing to detach Philip entirely from his friendship with them. He set about fabricating his slander by means of a contrivance of the following kind.

Amphidamus, the Strategus of the Eleans, who had been captured at Thalamae together with those who had taken refuge there—as we related earlier concerning these events—when he arrived at Olympia, brought along with the other prisoners, made urgent efforts through certain intermediaries to obtain an interview with the king.

Having secured this, he stated that he was able to bring the Eleans over into friendship and alliance with the king. Philip, convinced, sent Amphidamus off without ransom,

instructing him to promise the Eleans that, if they chose friendship with him, he would return all their captives without ransom, would himself guarantee the security of their territory against every outside attack,

and besides this would preserve them free, ungarrisoned, exempt from tribute, and governed by their own institutions.

The Eleans, however, when they had heard these terms, paid them no heed, attractive and substantial though the offers seemed to be.

Apelles then, fashioning his slander out of this affair, brought it before Philip, claiming that Aratus and his circle did not bear genuine friendship toward the Macedonians, nor were they truly well disposed toward the king; for it was they who had been responsible even now for the estrangement of the Eleans.

He alleged that at the very time when the king dispatched Amphidamus from Olympia to Elis, these men had taken the fellow aside privately and worked on him, telling him that it was in no way to the advantage of the Peloponnesians for Philip to become master of the Eleans;

and that for this reason the Eleans had spurned all the offers made to them, choosing instead to keep up their friendship with the Aetolians and to persist in their war against the Macedonians.
