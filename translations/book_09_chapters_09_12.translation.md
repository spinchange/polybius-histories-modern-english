I do not say this so much for the sake of praising either Romans or Carthaginians—for I have already noted their qualities more than once—as for the sake of those who hold command among either people, and of all who in later times will have charge of public affairs.

By remembering such actions, or by having them set before their eyes, they may become eager to imitate them. # Book 9 Chapter 10: The Spoils of Syracuse: Works of Art Taken To Rome

A city is not adorned by things brought in from outside, but by the virtue of its inhabitants.

For this reason the Romans decided to transfer the objects just mentioned to their own country and leave nothing behind.

Whether they acted rightly and to their own advantage, or the reverse, would require a long discussion. But the stronger argument, I think, is that they did not act properly then, and are not acting properly now when they do such things.

If it had been by means of such objects that they had first advanced their own country, then it would plainly have made sense to bring home the things through which they had grown great.

But if, while living in the utmost simplicity and keeping themselves far removed from the extravagance and luxury connected with such things, they nevertheless conquered people who possessed them in greatest abundance and of finest quality, how are we not to regard what they did as a mistake?

For to abandon the customs of the conquerors and take up admiration for those of the conquered, while at the same time taking on the jealousy that always follows such things—and that is the most dangerous thing for those in positions of superiority—must surely be acknowledged to be an error.

A spectator never praises those who possess what belongs to others so purely that pity for the original losers does not at once mingle with envy.

And when power continues to grow, and one people gathers everything belonging to others into its own hands, and in a sense invites the dispossessed to come and look at it all, the evil becomes double.

For then the spectators no longer pity their neighbors, but themselves, as the sight recalls their own disasters.

From this there springs not only envy, but a kind of anger against the prosperous, because the memory of their private misfortunes acts like a spur driving men to hatred of those who caused them.

There may perhaps be some reasonable argument for bringing gold and silver together into one's own possession; for no one can aspire to universal power without at once weakening the resources of others and preparing that kind of strength for himself.

But the things that lie outside this kind of power ought to have been left in their original places. In that way they would both have avoided stirring up envy and would have made their own country more glorious, adorning it not with paintings and statues, but with dignity and greatness of soul.

Let me say this for the sake of those who continually inherit positions of power, so that when they plunder cities they may not suppose that other people's disasters are an ornament to their own fatherlands. The Romans, however, after transferring these objects, used them to decorate private life in individual households and to embellish the public spaces of the city.

The Carthaginian commanders, though they had mastered their enemies, could not master themselves.

Thinking that they had already brought the war with Rome to an end, they fell into internal quarrels, continually led into conflict by the Phoenicians' inborn greed and love of power.

# Book 9 Chapter 12: On the Art of Commanding Armies

Military enterprises are attended by many circumstances that require careful thought; yet in each case it is possible to hit upon the right course, if a man carries out his plan with intelligence.

That operations in war openly and forcibly executed are fewer than those accomplished by stratagem and by the timely use of opportunity is easy for anyone to learn from past events.

And that even among those operations that do depend on seizing the right moment, failures are more numerous than successes is also not hard to see from experience.

Nor would anyone doubt that most such failures come about through the ignorance or carelessness of commanders.

So we may now examine what method governs this sort of undertaking.

Events in war that occur unexpectedly ought not really to be called operations at all, but rather accidents and chance occurrences.

For that reason we may leave aside anything that cannot be treated methodically or reduced to a fixed principle. What should be explained is what is done according to deliberate design; and it is of this that the present discussion speaks.

Every action has its definite opportunity, duration, and place, and requires secrecy, fixed signals, and also clear knowledge of through whom, with whom, and in what manner it is to be carried out.

It is therefore plain that the man who gets each of these points right will not fail in his enterprise, whereas one who neglects even a single point will ruin the whole design.
