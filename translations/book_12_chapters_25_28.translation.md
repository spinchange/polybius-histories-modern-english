# Book 12 Chapter 25: General Remarks on Timaeus as an Historian

Concerning the bronze bull that was made by Phalaris at Acragas, into which he used to put men and then, by kindling a fire beneath, exact

such a punishment from his subjects that, as the bronze was heated white-hot, the man, roasted and scorched on every side, was destroyed; and in the extremity of his agony, whenever he cried out, the sound that issued from the device struck the ears of those listening like the bellowing of a bull.

This bull, when the Carthaginians gained the upper hand, was carried off from Acragas to Carthage, and the little door between the shoulders—through which those condemned to punishment used to be let down—still remained; and although no other reason why such a bull should have been built at Carthage could be discovered at all,

Timaeus nevertheless set out both to demolish the common report and to brand as false the statements of the poets and historians, asserting that the bull at Carthage had not come from Acragas and that no such thing had ever existed in that city;

and he has devoted a great many arguments to this point. — What term and what word must one apply to Timaeus? It seems to me that his kind deserves all the most bitter names, the very ones he himself has used against those around him.

That he is quarrelsome, a liar, and reckless has been shown well enough, I think, from what has already been said; but that he is unphilosophical and, in a word, an ill-bred writer will become clear from what is about to be said.

For in his twenty-first book, and at the very end of it, in the exhortation of Timoleon he says this: that the earth, lying beneath the heavens, is divided into three parts, one called Asia, another Libya, and another Europe.

That Timaeus could have said such a thing no one would believe—no, not even of that proverbial Margites.

# Book 12 Chapter 26: Sophistical Commonplaces

...any chance youth? For he, first of all, thinks he ought to remind the delegates that those who sleep at dawn are roused in war by trumpets, but in peacetime by the birds.

After this he says that Heracles established the Olympic games and the truce that goes with them as a demonstration of his own principle, and that, although he harmed all those against whom he made war, he did so under necessity and by another's command, and was never willingly the cause of harm to any man.

Next to these, he brings in from the poet (Homer) Zeus, displeased with Ares and saying:

"Most hateful to me are you of the gods who hold Olympus; for strife is ever dear to you, and wars and battles."

And likewise the wisest of the heroes saying:

"Clanless, lawless, hearthless is that man who longs for chilling civil war."

And that Euripides agrees with the poet,

in the lines where he says:

"O Peace, rich in deep wealth, fairest of the blessed gods, I long for you—how long you delay! I fear that old age may overtake me before I look upon your lovely season, your songs of beautiful dances, and your garland-loving revels."

And in addition to these he says that war is most like disease and peace like health: for the one restores even the sick, while in the other even the healthy perish.

And that in peace the older are buried by the younger, as nature requires, whereas in war the opposite happens;

and, most important of all, that in war there is no safety even as far as the city walls, while in peace it extends to the very borders of the country—and other things much like these.

I wonder, then, what other words or expressions could have been used by a youth who has only just taken up the schools and the busywork drawn from written notes, and who wishes, by the rules of the art, to frame his composition from the attributes appropriate to the characters; for it seems to me he would use no others than precisely those which Timaeus says Hermocrates used.

# Book 12 Chapter 27: An Historian Needs Practical Experience

For there being by nature two instruments, as it were, by which we learn and investigate everything—hearing and sight—and sight being not a little the truer, according to Heraclitus (for the eyes are more accurate witnesses than the ears)—of these Timaeus set out to pursue his inquiry by the pleasanter,

but the inferior, of the two roads.

For he abstained entirely from learning through sight, and laid claim to learning through hearing. And this latter being itself of two kinds—one through written records *** —he handled the part concerning personal interrogation carelessly, as we have shown above.

The reason he made this choice is easy to grasp: matters drawn from books can be investigated without danger or hardship, if one only takes the single precaution of securing either a city that possesses an abundance of records or a library somewhere close at hand.

After that, one need only recline and search out the question at issue and compare the errors of earlier writers without any hardship at all.

Personal inquiry, on the other hand, demands much toil and expense, yet it contributes greatly and is the most important part of history.

This is clear from the very writers who compose histories. For Ephorus says that, if it were possible for them to be present at all the events, that would be far the best of all kinds of experience;

and Theopompus says that the best in military matters is the man who has taken part in the most battles, and the most capable in speech is the one who has shared in the most political contests.

And the same thing holds in the case of medicine and of steering a ship.

And even more vividly than these writers the poet (Homer) has spoken about this point. For he, wishing to show us what kind of man the man of action ought to be, takes up the figure of Odysseus and speaks somehow as follows:

"Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns, who wandered very far,"

and then continuing,

"and of many men he saw the cities and learned their mind, and many sorrows he suffered in his heart upon the sea,"

and again,

"passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves."

# Book 12 Chapter 28: Mere Inquiry Is Insufficient

It seems to me that the very dignity of history calls for such a man.

Now Plato says that human affairs will go well only when either philosophers become kings or kings take up philosophy;

and I too would say that history will be in a good state only when either men of action undertake to write histories—

not, as they do now, as a secondary matter, but considering this too to be among their most necessary and finest pursuits, and devoting themselves undistracted *** to this branch of work throughout their lives—

or when those who set out to write come to regard the experience gained from affairs themselves as indispensable for history. Until that happens there will be no end to the ignorance of historians.

Of all this Timaeus took not the slightest forethought,

but having lived out his life in a single place as a resident alien, and having, almost as if on purpose, renounced both active engagement in military and political affairs and the firsthand experience that comes from travel and observation, he somehow or other carries off the reputation of one who lays claim to the foremost rank among writers.

And that he is of this character it is easy to demonstrate from Timaeus' own admissions.

For in the preface to his sixth book he says that some people suppose that the genre of display oratory requires greater natural talent, industry, and preparation than that of history, and that these opinions had earlier been brought against Ephorus;

and since that writer had been unable to answer adequately those who said such things, he himself attempts to set history side by side with display oratory in a comparison—doing the most absurd thing of all,

first in misrepresenting that writer. For Ephorus, admirable throughout his whole work in his style, in his treatment, and in the conception of his material, is at his most masterly in his digressions and in the maxims he contributes of his own—in short, whenever he sets out some supplementary observation;

and, as it happens, it is on this very subject—the comparison of historians with speech-writers—that he has spoken most agreeably and most persuasively.
