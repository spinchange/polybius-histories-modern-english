# Book 3 Chapter 46: The Crossing of the Elephants

The way the animals were ferried across was roughly this. They built a number of rafts, sturdily put together, and lashed two of these firmly to one another, then anchored both against the ground at the point where the river was to be entered. Together the two had a width of about fifty feet.

To these they joined still others on the outer side and fitted them on, extending the structure of the joined platform out into the channel.

The side facing the current they secured with cables run to the land, fastening them to the trees growing along the bank, so that the whole work would hold together and not be pushed downstream by the river.

When they had made the entire projecting platform about two hundred feet long, they then attached to the outermost rafts two others that had been built especially large. These were bound tightly to the platform's end but connected to the rest only by ties that could be easily cut through. To these last rafts they also fastened several towlines,

with which the boats, towing them, were meant to keep them from being carried off downstream, and instead, holding them by force against the current, were to bring them along and ferry the animals across on them.

After this they heaped a great deal of earth onto all the rafts, piling it on until they had made the surface match, level and the same color as the road that led over dry land to the crossing.

The animals were trained to obey their Indian handlers right up to the water's edge, but would no longer dare in any way to step into the water. So they led them across over the bank of earth, putting two females in front, with the animals following them obediently.

Once the elephants had been brought onto the outermost rafts, the men cut through the ties by which these were attached to the rest, hauled in the towlines with the boats, and quickly pulled both the animals and the rafts beneath them away from the earthen ramp.

When this happened, the beasts, thrown into a panic, at first turned about and rushed in every direction; but, hemmed in on all sides by the current, they grew too frightened to act and were forced to stay where they were.

And it was in just this way, by always attaching a fresh pair of rafts, that most of the animals were carried across.

A few, however, in the middle of the crossing flung themselves into the river out of fear. Of these, all the Indians happened to perish, but the elephants were saved.

For thanks to the strength and length of their trunks, they raised them above the water and breathed through them, while at the same time blowing out everything that got in, and so held out, making most of their way upright along the river bottom.

# Book 3 Chapter 47: Previous Histories of this March either False or Inconsistent

Once the animals had been ferried over, Hannibal took up the elephants and the cavalry and advanced with them as a rearguard along the river, away from the sea and toward the east, making his march as though heading for the interior of Europe.

The Rhone has its sources above the head of the Adriatic Gulf, inclining toward the west, in the parts of the Alps that slope toward the north; it flows toward the winter sunset, and empties into the Sardinian Sea.

It runs for a long way through a valley, to the north of which dwell the Ardyan Celts, while the entire southern side of it is bounded by the foothills of the Alps that incline toward the north. As for the plains around the Po,

about which I have already spoken at some length, the ridges of the aforementioned mountains separate them from the valley of the Rhone; these ridges take their beginning at Massalia and run as far as the head of the whole Adriatic.

It was these that Hannibal then crossed over, passing from the region of the Rhone into Italy.

Some of those who have written about this crossing, in their wish to astonish their readers with marvelous tales about the places mentioned, fall without realizing it into two errors that are more foreign than anything else to all sound history: for they are driven both to tell falsehoods and to write things that contradict themselves.

For on the one hand they bring Hannibal on stage as an inimitable general, both in daring and in foresight, while at the same time they demonstrate to us, by their own account, that he was utterly devoid of judgment;

and on the other hand, being unable to find any resolution or way out of their falsehood, they bring gods and the sons of gods into what is supposed to be factual history.

For having laid it down that the strongholds and ruggedness of the Alpine mountains were such that not only horses and armies, and with them elephants, but not even lightly armed infantry could easily get through; and likewise having sketched the wilderness of those regions as so complete that, unless some god or hero had met Hannibal's men and shown them the roads, they would all have been at a total loss and perished — on both these counts they manifestly fall into the two errors mentioned above.

# Book 3 Chapter 48: Absurd Premises of Other Historians

[They fall into both of these errors.] For, in the first place, what general could be shown to be more devoid of judgment than Hannibal, what commander more inept,

if, leading forces so large and resting his greatest hopes of overall success on them, he knew, as these writers claim, neither the roads nor the country, neither where he was going at all nor against whom,

and, to crown it, not even whether what he was attempting was generally possible?

What men who have utterly failed and are at their wits' end in every way will not bring themselves to do — namely, to plunge into unscouted country with an army — this the writers attribute to Hannibal, who held the greatest hopes for his cause entirely unimpaired.

Likewise their account of the desolation, and further of the ruggedness and difficulty of the terrain, makes their falsehood plain.

For they failed to inquire into the fact that the Celts living along the Rhone, not once nor twice before Hannibal's arrival — and not in the distant past but quite recently — had crossed the Alps with great armies, drawn up against the Romans and fought alongside the Celts inhabiting the plains around the Po, as I have shown in the earlier parts of this work;

and besides this, they did not know that a very large tribe of people happens to live in the Alps themselves. Instead, ignorant of every one of the things I have mentioned, they claim that some hero appeared and pointed out the roads to them.

By this they naturally fall into the same case as the writers of tragedy. For with all of those, the denouements of their dramas require a god and a stage machine, because they take their initial premises to be false and irrational;

and so historians too must suffer and do the same — bringing on heroes and gods to appear — once they have set up beginnings that are implausible and false. For how is it possible to set a reasonable ending upon irrational beginnings?

Hannibal, at any rate, did not act as these men write; on the contrary, he handled his designs in these matters with great practical sense.

For he had clearly investigated both the fertility of the land into which he intended to descend and the hostility of its peoples toward the Romans, and for the difficult ground that lay between he employed local guides and leaders who were going to share in the same hopes as himself.

I speak about these things with confidence because I have inquired into the events from men who were themselves present at the time, and have surveyed the country and used the Alpine route myself, for the sake of knowledge and of seeing it firsthand.

# Book 3 Chapter 49: Scipio Is Too Late To Stop Hannibal

Nevertheless, Publius, the Roman general, arriving at the river crossing three days after the Carthaginians had broken camp, and finding that the enemy had already set out, was as thoroughly astonished as could be,

since he had been convinced that they would never dare to make their march into Italy by this route, because of the numbers and the faithlessness of the barbarians inhabiting those regions.

But seeing that they had in fact dared it, he hurried back to his ships, and on arriving began to embark his forces.

He sent his brother off to handle affairs in Iberia, while he himself turned back and made the voyage to Italy, eager to outpace the enemy by going through Etruria to the crossing of the Alps.

Hannibal, meanwhile, after marching four days in succession from the crossing, came to the place called the Island, a populous and grain-bearing district that takes its name from the very circumstance of its situation.

For where the Rhone runs along one side and the river called the Isère along the other, flowing past on each flank, they bring its shape to a point at the place where they converge with each other.

It is similar in size and shape to what is called the Delta in Egypt, except that in the Delta the sea joins together its one side and the mouths of the rivers, whereas here that side is closed off by mountains hard to approach, hard to enter, and, one might almost say, inaccessible.

On reaching this district, he found in it two brothers quarreling over the kingship and encamped against each other with their armies.

When the elder courted him and called on him to join in helping him secure the throne, he agreed, since the advantage this would bring him for the present was almost obvious.

And so, by joining the attack and helping to drive out the other brother, he gained much assistance from the victor.

For this man not only supplied the army lavishly with grain and the other provisions, but also exchanged all their old and worn-out weapons and renewed the equipment of the whole force at an opportune moment;

and further, by furnishing most of the men with clothing and, on top of that, with footwear, he provided great practical benefit for the crossings of the mountains.

But most important of all, since the Carthaginians were apprehensive about the march through the territory of the Gauls called Allobroges, he brought up the rear with his own force and secured them a safe passage until they drew near to the crossing of the Alps.
