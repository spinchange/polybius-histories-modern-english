# Book 29 Chapter 10: Rhodes Decides to Seek Peace

When the vote was put before the Rhodians, the proposal to send envoys to negotiate a settlement won the day.

This was how the council settled the Rhodian opposition, as I have explained in my discussion of public speaking.

In that division, the men who sided with Perseus proved stronger than those who were trying to save their country and its laws.

The prytaneis immediately appointed envoys to bring the war to an end: Agepolis, Diocles, and Cleombrotus were sent to Rome; Damon, Nicostratus, Agesilochus, and Telephus to the general and to Perseus.

But they did not stop there. They went on in the same course and added to it, making their offence beyond excuse:

for they at once sent envoys to Crete, to renew their former friendly relations with all the Cretans and to urge them to look at the times and the danger, to stand with the people of Rhodes, and to choose the same enemies and friends as they did;

and they planned to speak in the same way in private to the individual cities.

# Book 29 Chapter 11: Turbulent Assembly at Rhodes

When the envoys from Genthius, together with those led by Parmenio and Morcus and accompanied by Metrodorus, arrived at Rhodes, and the council had been summoned,

the assembly was in a complete uproar. The men around Deinus and Polyaratus were now openly daring to speak on Perseus' behalf, while the party around Theaedetus was stunned by what was happening.

For the arrival of the galleys, the number of cavalry that had been lost, and Genthius' defection crushed them.

So the outcome of the assembly followed naturally from what has just been said:

the Rhodians voted to give both kings a friendly answer, to tell them that they had decided to put an end to the war, and to urge each of them not to make difficulties over peace terms.

They also received Genthius' envoys at the common hearth with great friendliness.

# Book 29 Chapter 12: On Proportion in History

Again, other writers have gone on at length about the Syrian war. The reason for this is what I have already explained more than once.

For when men take a simple and straightforward subject and yet want not the facts but the bulk of their books to make them seem like historians, and want that sort of display to create the impression of importance, it becomes necessary for them to make small things big,

and to dress up matters that were briefly stated, and in some cases to turn incidental happenings into full-scale actions and affairs—staging contests and announcing battle lines in which sometimes ten infantrymen fell, sometimes only a little more, and cavalry even fewer than that.

As for sieges, geography, and similar topics, no one could say their treatment is adequate, so far as they develop them at all, because they have no material to work with.

But the opposite is true for those who write universal history:

and so one should not blame us as if we were dragging out events when we sometimes pass over things that others have given great attention and embellishment, and sometimes mention them only briefly; rather, one should trust that we give each subject the treatment it deserves.

For when those writers describe, somewhere in the course of their whole work, the siege of Phanoteia or Coroneia or Haliartus, they are forced to set out every contrivance, every bold move, and every detail of the siege,

and beyond that to dwell on the capture of Tarentum, Corinth, Sardis, Gaza, Bactra, and, above all, Carthage, adding details of their own; yet they are not at all satisfied if we give a plain and truthful account of such matters.

The same judgment should apply to our treatment of battles and speeches, and likewise to the other parts of history:

in all these matters we would fairly deserve much forgiveness, and the same is true of what I am about to narrate, even if I should seem inconsistent in my subject matter, in my handling of events, or in my style of expression;

and also if I should sometimes slip in the names of mountains, rivers, or local places. The size of the undertaking itself is excuse enough in all these matters,

unless I am caught deliberately making false statements for gain; for that I ask no pardon, as I have repeatedly and expressly said in this work.

# Book 29 Chapter 13: Character of Genthius

In the twenty-ninth book, the same writer says that Genthius, king of the Illyrians, committed many shameful acts in his life because of his heavy drinking, being drunk night and day alike:

and after killing his brother Plator, who was about to marry the daughter of Monunius, he himself married the girl and treated his subjects with savage cruelty.
