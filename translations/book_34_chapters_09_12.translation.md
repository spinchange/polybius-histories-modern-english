# Book 34 Chapter 9: Chapter 9

V. Spain

The inhabitants are called the Turdetani and the Turduli; some think these are the same people, others that they are different.

Polybius is among those who say that the Turduli live to the north of the Turdetani as their neighbors.

The fertility of the land also gave the Turdetani a gentle and civilized character, and likewise the Celtic peoples because of their proximity—or, as Polybius says, because of their kinship.

Dicaearchus, Eratosthenes, Polybius, and most Greek writers place the Pillars at the strait.

Polybius says that there is a spring in the Heracleum at Gades, with a short flight of steps leading down to drinkable water, and that it behaves opposite to the sea tides: at high tide it runs low, and at ebb tide it fills.

His explanation is that the breath rising from the depths to the earth’s surface, when the ground is covered by the sea during the incoming tide, is blocked from its natural outlets; turned back inward, it chokes the channels of the spring and causes a lack of water.

But when the ground is laid bare again, it finds an open passage and frees the veins of the spring, so that the water gushes up readily.

Polybius, mentioning the silver mines around New Carthage, says that they are extremely large, about twenty stades from the city, and enclosed within a circuit of four hundred stades.

There forty thousand workers live, and at that time they brought in for the Roman people twenty-five thousand drachmas each day.

As for the rest of the working process, I pass it over—it would take too long—but he says that the silver-bearing ore is crushed and washed through sieves in water; then the sediment is crushed again, and after being filtered again and the water poured off, it is crushed once more.

When the fifth sediment has been smelted, and the lead has been run off, the pure silver is extracted.

Polybius also says that the Ana and the Baetis both flow from Celtiberia, about nine hundred stades apart from one another.

Polybius, in going through the peoples and territories of the Vaccaei and the Celtiberians, includes among their other cities Segesama and Intercatia.

In its design and splendor it resembles the palace of a certain Iberian king mentioned by Polybius.

He says that this king rivaled the luxury of the Phaeacians, except that the mixing bowls standing in the middle of the house, though made of silver and gold, were full of barley wine.

VI. Gaul

Polybius, in the thirty-fourth book of his Histories, says that from the Pyrenees to the river Narbo there is a plain, through which flow the rivers Illeberis and Rhoscynus, passing by cities of the same names inhabited by Celts.

In this plain are found what are called underground fish.

The plain has a light soil and produces a great quantity of agrostis grass. Beneath this, because the ground is sandy to a depth of two or three cubits, the water that spreads out from the rivers filters below.

With this water the fish also make their way under the ground during the overflow in search of food—for they are fond of the root of the agrostis—and so they have made the whole plain full of underground fish, which people dig up and catch.

As for the mouths of the Rhone, Polybius criticizes Timaeus, saying that the river does not have five mouths but two.

The Liger empties between the Pictones and the Namnitae. Formerly there was an emporium on this river called Corbilo, of which Polybius speaks when mentioning the fictions invented by Pytheas.

He says that none of the people from Massalia who met Scipio could tell him anything worth remembering when he asked about Britain, nor could any from Narbo or Corbilo, though these were the finest cities in that region; yet Pytheas had the boldness to tell so many lies.

Polybius also says that in these regions there is bred an unusual animal, shaped like a deer except for its neck and coat, and in those respects resembling a wild boar.

Under its chin it has a tuft about a span long, hairy at the tip and as thick as a colt’s tail.

Polybius further says that in his own time, near Aquileia among the Norican Taurisci, a gold mine was discovered so rich by nature that, after scraping away only two feet of topsoil, one immediately found gold-bearing ore.

The vein was no more than fifteen feet deep.

Some of the gold was already pure, in pieces the size of a bean or lupin, and when smelted lost only one eighth; the rest required more working in the furnace, but was still highly profitable.

When Italians joined the barbarians in working it, within two months the price of gold throughout Italy immediately fell by a third.

When the Taurisci realized this, they drove out their partners and kept the monopoly themselves.

The same writer, speaking of the size and height of the Alps, compares them with the greatest mountains in Greece—Taygetus, Lycaeus, Parnassus, Olympus, Pelion, and Ossa—and in Thrace with Haemus, Rhodope, and Dunax.

He says that each of these can more or less be climbed by active men in a single day, and likewise gone around in a single day.

But no one could climb the Alps even in five days; and their length along the plains is two thousand two hundred stades.

He names only four passes: one through Liguria, nearest the Tyrrhenian Sea; next the one through the Taurini, which Hannibal used; then that through the Salassi; and fourth that through the Raeti, all of them precipitous.

He says that there are several lakes in the mountains, but three are large. The first, Benacus, is five hundred stades long and one hundred and thirty wide, and from it flows the river Mincius.

The next, Larius, is four hundred stades long and narrower than the first, and it sends out the river Addua.

The third, Verbanus, is nearly three hundred stades long and thirty wide, and it sends out the great river Ticinus. All these rivers flow into the Padus.

VII. Italy

Polybius says that at Capua a remarkable wine is produced, called Anadendrites, with which no other can be compared.

He says that the coast from Iapygia to the strait is three thousand stades by land, washed by the Sicilian Sea, but by sea voyage it is less than five hundred.

They say that the greatest length of the Etrurian coast, from Luna to Ostia, is about two thousand five hundred stades, while its width inland toward the mountains is less than half that. From Luna to Pisa is more than four hundred stades; from there to Volaterrae two hundred and eighty; then from there to Populonium two hundred and seventy; and from Populonium to Cosa nearly eight hundred, though some say six hundred. Polybius is not correct in giving the total as one thousand three hundred and thirty.

Aethaleia, an island of the Tyrrhenians.

Polybius in the thirty-fourth book says that Aethaleia is called Lemnos.

The gulf between the two promontories, Misenum and Athenaeum, is called the Crater.

Above these shores lies all Campania, the most fertile plain of all.

Antiochus says that this land was inhabited by the Opici, who were also called Ausones.

Polybius, however, indicates that he regards these as two peoples; for he says that both the Opici and the Ausones inhabit this land around the Crater.

Polybius says that the distances from Iapygia have been measured in miles,

and that there are five hundred and sixty-two miles to the city of Sila, and from there to Aquileia one hundred and seventy-eight.

Then comes Lacinium,

a sanctuary of Hera, once wealthy and full of many offerings.

The lengths are not stated clearly; but for the most part Polybius gives the distance from the strait to Lacinium as one thousand three hundred stades,

and from there across to the Iapygian promontory as seven hundred.

Polybius says that of the three craters one has partly collapsed, while the other two remain intact.

The largest has a circular rim with a circumference of five stades, but it gradually narrows to a diameter of fifty feet.

From there the depth to the sea is one stade, so that in calm weather the sea can be seen. If this is trustworthy, then perhaps the stories told about Empedocles should not be dismissed either.

He says that when a south wind is about to blow, a misty haze spreads all around the little island, so that even Sicily cannot be seen from it at a distance; but when a north wind is coming,

bright flames leap up from the crater mentioned above to a great height, and louder rumblings are sent out; the west wind produces something between the two.

The other craters are of similar shape, but weaker in the force of their eruptions.

From the differences in the rumblings, and from the point where the blasts, flames, and smoke begin, it is possible to foretell which wind will blow again on the third day.

He says that when ships were weather-bound in the Lipari Islands, some men there would predict what wind was coming and were not proved wrong.

So it appears that what seems most mythical in the poet was not spoken in vain, but that he expressed the truth in a riddle when he called Aeolus the keeper of the winds.

VIII. Thrace, Macedonia, Greece

He also says other things that are not trustworthy: that the seas are connected by channels because Chian and Thasian pottery is found in the Naro; that both seas can be seen from a certain mountain; that among the Liburnian islands is Titheis, with a circuit of five hundred stades; and that the Ister pours one of its mouths into the Adriatic.

Some statements of Eratosthenes too are misunderstandings shaped by popular talk, as Polybius says when discussing him and other historians.

From Apollonia to Macedonia the Via Egnatia runs eastward, measured out by the mile and marked with milestones as far as Cypsela and the river Hebrus; the total is five hundred and thirty-five miles.

If one reckons, as most people do, eight stades to the mile, the total comes to four thousand two hundred and eighty stades.

But according to Polybius, by adding to the eight-stade mile two plethra, which is one third of a stade, one must add another one hundred and seventy-eight stades, that is, one third of the number of miles.

It turns out that those who set out from Apollonia and those who set out from Epidamnus come together onto the same road after an equal distance.

The whole road is called the Egnatian, but the first stretch is called the Candavian road, from Mount Candavia in Illyria, and it runs through the city of Lychnidus and through Pylon, the place on the route that marks the boundary between Illyria and Macedonia.

From there it goes past Barnous, through Heracleia and the regions of the Lyncestae and Eordi, to Edessa and Pella, and on to Thessalonica.

Polybius says this section is two hundred and sixty-seven miles.

Since the distance from Perinthus to Byzantium is six hundred and thirty stades, and from the Hebrus and Cypsela to Byzantium as far as the Cyanean Rocks is three thousand one hundred, as Artemidorus says, the total length from the Ionian Gulf at Apollonia to Byzantium is seven thousand three hundred and twenty stades; Polybius adds another one hundred and eighty,

counting the extra third of a stade on top of the eight stades in each mile.

The circumference of the Peloponnese, if one does not follow every inlet, is four thousand stades, according to Polybius.

When Polybius said that the distance from Malea northward to the Ister was about ten thousand stades, Artemidorus corrects this, and not unreasonably. According to him, the distance from the Ister to Malea is six thousand five hundred. The reason is that Polybius did not measure the direct route, but some incidental one that one of the generals had taken.
