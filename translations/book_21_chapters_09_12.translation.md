# Book 21 Chapter 9: Antiochus At Pergamum

Diophanes of Megalopolis had great practical experience in warfare, because during the long war against Nabis the Megalopolitans, being a neighboring people, had spent all that time serving under Philopoemen and had thus gained real training in military action.

Besides this, the man was powerful and impressive both in appearance and in bodily vigor.

And most important of all, he was a brave soldier in war and exceptionally skillful in the use of arms.

# Book 21 Chapter 10: Antiochus Proposes Peace

King Antiochus invaded the territory of Pergamum; but when he learned that King Eumenes was present and saw that not only the naval but also the land forces were coming against him, he began to consider opening negotiations for peace at once with the Romans, Eumenes, and the Rhodians.

So he moved with his whole army to Elaea, occupied a hill opposite the city, stationed his infantry on it, and encamped his cavalry—more than six thousand strong—close by the city itself.

He himself took position between these forces and sent messengers into the city to Lucius about a settlement.

The Roman general then called together the Rhodians and Eumenes and asked each to state plainly what seemed best in the present situation.

Eudamus and Pamphilidas were not opposed to a settlement, but the king said that peace was at present neither honorable nor practicable.

“How,” he said, “could the outcome be honorable if we make peace while shut up behind walls?

And indeed how can it be practicable now? Without the consul being present, how could any agreed terms be valid without his approval?

Besides, even if some sign of agreement were reached with Antiochus, neither the fleet nor the army could properly go home before the people and senate had ratified the terms.

We would then have nothing left but to winter here waiting for their decision, doing nothing while consuming the supplies and resources of our allies.

Then, if the senate should refuse the settlement, we would have to begin the war afresh from the beginning, after letting the present opportunity pass—an opportunity in which, if the gods are willing, we may bring the whole matter to an end.”

Such was Eumenes' view. Lucius approved the advice and answered Antiochus's envoys that no settlement could be made before the proconsul arrived.

When Antiochus's people heard this, they at once began to ravage the territory of Elaea.

After this Seleucus remained in that district, while Antiochus continued his march and entered the plain called Thebe.

There, having moved into a rich and prosperous region full of resources, he filled his army with every kind of plunder.

# Book 21 Chapter 11: Prusias Refuses To Help Antiochus

When King Antiochus returned to Sardis from the expedition just mentioned, he kept sending messages to Prusias, urging him to join his alliance.

In earlier times Prusias had not been unwilling to make common cause with Antiochus, because he was very much afraid that the Romans were crossing into Asia in order to overthrow all monarchs.

But a letter reached him from the brothers Lucius and Publius. When he received it and read it, his thinking changed greatly, and he gained a reasonable foresight of what was coming.

For the men around Publius had written with great clarity, and had added many proofs to make their case convincing.

They did not merely defend their own policy,

but that of the Roman people as a whole, showing that they had not taken kingdoms away from the kings who originally held them; rather, in some cases they had themselves established rulers, and in others had enlarged their power and made their dominions many times greater.

They cited Indibilis and Colichas in Spain, Massanissa in Libya, and Pleuratus in Illyria.

All of these, they said, had been raised by Rome from small and insignificant dynasts into acknowledged kings.

In the same way they mentioned Philip and Nabis in Greece. Though they had defeated Philip in war and reduced him to hostages and tribute, after receiving from him only a slight proof of goodwill they had restored his son and the young men who had been hostages with him, released him from tribute, and returned many cities taken in war.

And although they could have removed Nabis altogether, they had not done so, but had spared him, tyrant though he was, after receiving the customary guarantees.

In view of all this they urged Prusias by letter not to be afraid for his throne, but confidently to choose the Roman side, since he would never regret such a decision.

After hearing these arguments Prusias changed his mind. And when envoys headed by Gaius Livius also arrived and met with him, he gave up all expectation of support from Antiochus.

Antiochus, having failed in this hope, came to Ephesus; and reflecting that only by mastering the sea could he prevent the crossing of the land army and in general keep the war away from Asia, he resolved to fight a naval battle and decide the issue by a contest at sea.
