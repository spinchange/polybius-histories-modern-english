# Book 16 Chapter 5: Attalus Barely Escapes Capture

The most brilliant and dangerous fighting was done by three Rhodian quinqueremes: the flagship on which Theophiliscus sailed, the ship commanded by Philostratus, and a third steered by Autolycus with Nicostratus aboard.

This last ship rammed an enemy vessel and left its own ram fixed in the hull. The enemy ship sank with all hands, but because sea water was pouring into Autolycus' ship through the prow, he and his men were surrounded by the enemy.

At first they fought bravely, but in the end Autolycus, wounded, fell overboard in his armor, and the rest of the marines died fighting stoutly.

At that moment Theophiliscus came to help with three quinqueremes. He could not save the ship, because it was already full of water, but after smashing two enemy vessels he drove their marines overboard.

Soon, however, he himself was swarmed by a larger number of light boats and decked ships, and he lost most of his marines after they had fought with conspicuous bravery.

He himself received three wounds and, after exposing himself to the utmost danger, only just saved his own ship, with Philostratus coming to his aid and nobly sharing the peril.

Once he had rejoined his own squadron, he attacked the enemy again from a fresh angle. Though his bodily strength was failing from his wounds, the courage in his spirit made him more brilliant and more formidable than before.

So there were really two sea-battles taking place far apart. Philip's right wing, in keeping with the original plan, kept edging toward land and was not far from Asia,

while the left wing, having turned back to support the ships in the rear, was fighting the Rhodians not far from Chios.

# Book 16 Chapter 6: Attalus Abandons his Ship but Escapes

Even so, Attalus' side was decisively overpowering Philip's right wing and was already drawing near the little islands under which Philip had anchored to watch the battle.

Attalus then noticed one of his own quinqueremes disabled and sinking under attack from an enemy ship, and he rushed to help it with two quadriremes.

When the enemy ship turned and tried to escape toward land, Attalus pursued too eagerly, eager to capture it.

Philip saw that Attalus had become separated by a wide distance from his own fleet. He took four quinqueremes, three hemioliai, and the nearest light boats, dashed forward, cut Attalus off from his own ships, and forced him in great panic to run his vessels aground.

Once that happened, the king himself escaped with his crews to Erythrae, but Philip captured the ships and the royal equipment on board.

Yet Attalus' people showed ingenuity even in this crisis. They spread the finest pieces of the royal equipment out on the deck.

So when the first Macedonians came up in their light craft and saw quantities of cups, purple robes, and the like, they gave up the pursuit and turned aside to plunder these things.

That is how Attalus managed to make his retreat safely to Erythrae.

Philip, though badly worsted in the battle as a whole, was elated by what had happened to Attalus. He put to sea again, busily gathering his ships and encouraging his men, saying that they had won the battle.

People found this plausible because Philip's men were towing away the royal ship, and so many assumed that King Attalus himself had perished.

But Dionysodorus guessed what had happened to his king, raised the signal, gathered his own ships around him, and then sailed off safely to the harbors on the Asiatic coast.

At the same time the Macedonians who had been fighting the Rhodians, after suffering badly for a long time, gradually slipped out of danger by withdrawing in small groups under the pretense of hurrying to help their own ships.

The Rhodians towed off some captured ships, destroyed others with their rams, and then sailed away to Chios.

# Book 16 Chapter 7: Losses in the Battle

Philip lost, in the battle against Attalus, a ten-banked ship, a nine-banked ship, a seven-banked ship, a six-banked ship, ten other decked ships, three triemioliai, and twenty-five light boats, together with their crews.

In the battle against the Rhodians he lost ten decked ships and about forty light boats, while two quadriremes and seven light boats with their crews were captured.

On Attalus' side one triemiolia and two quinqueremes were sunk, while two quadriremes and the king's own ship were captured.

Of the Rhodian fleet, two quinqueremes and one trireme were destroyed, but none were taken.

Of the men, the Rhodians lost about sixty, Attalus about seventy, while Philip lost about three thousand Macedonians and six thousand sailors and marines.

About two thousand of Philip's Macedonians and allies were taken alive, while of their opponents about seven hundred were captured.

# Book 16 Chapter 8: Philip Vainly Claims the Victory At Chios

Such was the outcome of the naval battle off Chios.

Philip claimed the victory on two grounds: first, because he had forced Attalus ashore and had taken his ship; second, because after anchoring at the place called Argennum he seemed to have made his anchorage among the wrecks.

In keeping with this claim, on the next day he gathered up the wreckage and recovered for burial such of the dead as could still be recognized, all to strengthen that appearance.

But that he did not really believe he had won was soon proved by both the Rhodians and Dionysodorus.

For on the following day, while Philip was still occupied with these matters, they communicated with one another, sailed out against him, drew up their ships in line before him, and then, when no one put out to meet them, sailed back again to Chios.

Philip had never before lost so many men at one moment, either by land or by sea, and he took the event very hard. Much of his spirit for the enterprise was broken.

Still, toward the outside world he tried by every means to hide his discouragement, though the facts themselves would not allow it.

Quite apart from everything else, even what followed after the battle struck horror into all who saw it.

So many men had been destroyed that on the very day of the battle the whole strait was full of corpses, blood, weapons, and wreckage, and on the following days the beaches could be seen heaped together in confused masses with all these things mingled.

Because of this, not only Philip himself but all the Macedonians were thrown into no small shame and consternation.
