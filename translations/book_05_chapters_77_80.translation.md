Achaeus, having brought Milyas and the greater part of Pamphylia under his control, broke camp and, on arriving at Sardis, waged continuous war against Attalus, kept up his threats against Prusias, and made himself an object of fear and oppression to all who dwelt on this side of the Taurus.

Now at the very time when Achaeus was conducting his campaign against the Selgians, Attalus, with the Aegosagae Gauls in his force, marched through the cities of Aeolis and those bordering on them—all the cities that had earlier gone over to Achaeus out of fear.

Of these, the greater number joined him willingly and even with goodwill, while a few had to be compelled by force.

The cities that went over to him at that time were, first, Cyme, Smyrna, and Phocaea; after these the people of Aegae and Temnus came over, struck with alarm at his advance.

Ambassadors also arrived from the Teians and Colophonians, putting themselves and their cities into his hands.

He received these too on the same terms as before and took hostages; but he dealt especially kindly with the envoys from Smyrna, because they above all had kept faith with him.

Continuing his advance without a break, he crossed the river Lycus and pushed on toward the settlements of the Mysians, and from there he came to Carseae.

Having overawed its people, and likewise those guarding the Twin Walls, he took over these positions as well, Themistocles handing them over—the man who happened to have been left by Achaeus as commander of this district.

Setting out from there and ravaging the plain of Apia, he crossed the mountain called Pelecas and encamped near the river Megistus.

# Book 5 Chapter 78: Attalus Conciliates the Gauls

While he was here, an eclipse of the moon occurred. The Gauls, who had long been chafing at the hardships of the marches—since they were conducting the campaign together with their wives and children, who followed them in the wagons—

now took the event as an omen and declared they would advance no farther.

King Attalus, who got no real service of any consequence out of them and saw that they straggled apart during the marches, encamped on their own, and in general refused obedience and were full of arrogance, fell into no ordinary perplexity.

For on the one hand he was anxious that they might defect to Achaeus and join in attacking his affairs, and on the other he was wary of the ill repute that would dog him if he surrounded his soldiers and destroyed all the men who were thought to have made the crossing into Asia in reliance on his own pledge.

Therefore, seizing on the aforementioned pretext, he promised that for the present he would restore them to the crossing and would give them a well-suited place for settlement, and that afterward he would lend them his aid in whatever they might ask of him that lay within his power and was honorable.

So Attalus,

having restored the Aegosagae to the Hellespont and dealt kindly with the people of Lampsacus, Alexandria, and Ilium because they had kept faith with him, returned [with his army to Pergamum].

# Book 5 Chapter 79: Forces Available to Antiochus and Ptolemy

[He withdrew] with his army to Pergamum. Antiochus and Ptolemy, as the spring season was setting in and their preparations were ready, set about deciding the conflict by battle.

Ptolemy's forces, then, set out from Alexandria with some seventy thousand infantry, five thousand cavalry, and seventy-three elephants.

Antiochus, learning of their advance, gathered his own forces together. These were: Daae, Carmanians, and Cilicians, equipped in light-armed fashion, about five thousand in number; over these Byttacus the Macedonian held both charge and command.

Under Theodotus the Aetolian, the man who had carried out the betrayal, were ten thousand men picked from the whole kingdom and armed in the Macedonian manner; most of these were Silver Shields.

The strength of the phalanx came to some twenty thousand, led by Nicarchus and Theodotus called Hemiolius.

In addition to these there were Agrianians and Persian archers and slingers, two thousand. With them were a thousand Thracians, led by Menedemus of Alabanda.

There were also Medes, Cissians, Cadusians, and Carmanians, in all about five thousand, who were assigned to obey Aspasianus the Mede.

The Arabs and some of their neighbors numbered about ten thousand, and were under the command of Zabdibelus.

The mercenaries from Greece were led by Hippolochus the Thessalian, and numbered about five thousand.

He had fifteen hundred Cretans with Eurylochus, and a thousand Neo-Cretans under the command of Zelys of Gortyna;

along with them were five hundred Lydian javelin-men and a thousand Cardaces under Lysimachus the Gaul.

The whole body of cavalry came to some six thousand; of these, Antipater, the king's nephew, held command of four thousand, while Themison was set over the rest.

The total strength of Antiochus's army was sixty-two thousand infantry, together with six thousand cavalry, and a hundred and two beasts.

# Book 5 Chapter 80: Antiochus Advances To Raphia

Ptolemy, making his march toward Pelusium, at first halted at that city.

Then, having taken up the stragglers who trailed behind and issued the army its grain rations, he set it in motion and advanced, marching along Mount Casius and the so-called Barathra through the waterless region.

Completing the journey to his objective on the fifth day, he encamped fifty stades from Raphia, which, after Rhinocolura, is the first of the cities of Coele-Syria as one comes from Egypt.

At the same time Antiochus arrived with his army; having reached Gaza and taken on additional forces there, he again advanced at a steady pace. Passing the aforesaid city, he encamped by night, about ten stades from the enemy.

At first, then, set at this distance, they remained encamped opposite each other.

But after some days Antiochus, wishing both to take up more favorable ground and to instill confidence in his forces, moved his camp up against Ptolemy's, so that the palisades were no more than five stades apart.

During this time there were frequent clashes over the watering-places and the foraging, and likewise skirmishes broke out between the camps, sometimes of cavalry, sometimes of infantry as well.
