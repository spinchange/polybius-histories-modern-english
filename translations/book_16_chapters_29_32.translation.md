# Book 16 Chapter 29: The Hellespont Compared with Gibraltar

Philip wanted to deprive the Romans of their bases and landing points in that region.

That way, if he later chose to cross again into Asia, he would have Abydos as his landing place.

As for the position of Abydos and Sestos and the advantages of these cities, it seems to me pointless to describe everything at length, because all who gain even a little benefit from such knowledge already know these places on account of their special character.

Still, to remind my readers of the main points is not useless for the present discussion.

One can best understand the situation of these cities not simply by looking at the places themselves, but by setting beside them and comparing the places that I am about to mention.

Just as no one can sail from what some call Ocean and others the Atlantic Sea into our sea without passing through the mouth by the Pillars of Heracles,

so no one can sail from our sea into the Propontis and the Pontus without making the passage through the channel between Sestos and Abydos.

And as if Fortune had deliberately shaped the two straits in answer to one another, the passage by the Pillars of Heracles turns out to be many times wider than the Hellespont.

The one is sixty stades across, while that at Abydos is two, as though one might infer this from the fact that the outer sea is so much greater than ours.

Yet the crossing at Abydos is more serviceable than that at the Pillars of Heracles.

For the former, inhabited on both sides, has the character of a gate because of the intercourse between the two shores: at times people even bridge it for those who wish to cross on foot between the continents, and at all times it is traversed by shipping.

But the channel at the Pillars of Heracles is rarely used and by few, because the peoples living at the far ends of Libya and Europe have little intercourse with one another and because the outer sea remains largely unknown.

The city of Abydos itself is enclosed on both sides by promontories from Europe, and it has a harbor able to shelter ships lying there from every wind.

Apart from entering that harbor, there is nowhere at all near the city where one can anchor, because of the sharpness and violence of the current running through the strait.

# Book 16 Chapter 30: The Siege of Abydos

Philip, after surrounding the Abydenians partly with palisades and partly with entrenchments, besieged them both by land and by sea.

This siege was not remarkable for the scale of the preparations or for the variety of inventions in the engineering, by which besiegers and besieged usually try to outdo one another in skill.

But in respect to the courage of the defenders and the extraordinary greatness of their spirit, it was, if any siege ever was, worthy of memory and record.

At first the inhabitants of Abydos, trusting in themselves, stoutly withstood Philip's preparations. Of the machines brought against them by sea, they shook some apart by striking them with stone-throwers and destroyed others with fire, so that the enemy could scarcely drag their ships back out of danger.

Against the works on land they also held out bravely for a time, not despairing of mastering the enemy.

But when the outer wall fell because of the mines, and then the Macedonians by further tunneling reached the inner wall that had been built behind the breach,

they then sent ambassadors, Iphiades and Pantagnotus, instructing Philip to take over the city on condition that he let the soldiers from Rhodes and Attalus depart under truce, and that he allow all free persons to save themselves, each wherever he chose, with the clothing on his body.

But Philip ordered them to leave everything to his decision or else fight bravely.

# Book 16 Chapter 31: The People of Abydos Resolve to Conquer or Die

After this answer the envoys returned. When the Abydenians heard what had been said, they gathered in assembly and, utterly beside themselves in their thinking, took counsel about the crisis.

They resolved first to free the slaves, so that they might have comrades in the struggle who would fight without excuse; next, to gather all the women into the temple of Artemis and the children with their nurses into the gymnasium.

After that they decided to collect all the silver and gold into the marketplace, and likewise to bring all valuable clothing onto the Rhodian quadrireme and the Cyzicene trireme.

After proposing and carrying out these measures unanimously, they again assembled in the public meeting and chose fifty of the older men, those most trusted and still strong enough in body to carry out what had been resolved.

These they bound by oath before all the citizens that, whenever they saw the inner wall taken by the enemy, they would slaughter the women and children, burn the ships already mentioned, and cast the silver and gold into the sea in accordance with the curses pronounced.

After this they brought forward the priests, and all swore either to prevail over the enemy or to die fighting for their country.

To crown all, after sacrificing victims and pronouncing curses, they compelled the priests and priestesses to utter imprecations over the burnt offerings concerning all these resolutions.

Once they had ratified these decisions, they gave up countermining the enemy and settled on this course: when the inner wall fell, they would fight on the breach itself and die there resisting those who forced their way in.

# Book 16 Chapter 32: Courage of the Abydenians

From this one might say that the courage of the Abydenians surpassed both the proverbial recklessness of the Phocians and the bravery of the Acarnanians.

For the Phocians are thought to have taken similar counsel about their families, but they had not utterly given up hope of victory, because they were going to fight the Thessalians in open country in a pitched battle. The same was true of the Acarnanians,

when, foreseeing an Aetolian invasion, they resolved on similar measures for the emergency, as I have explained earlier in detail.

But the Abydenians, shut in and almost despairing of salvation, chose as a whole people rather to meet their fate along with their children and wives than to go on living with the foreknowledge that their children and wives would fall under enemy power.

So one might especially blame Fortune in the case of the Abydenians, because in the earlier disasters she seemed to pity the sufferers and at once set matters right by giving victory together with safety to those who had despaired, whereas in the case of the Abydenians she reached the opposite decision.

For the men died, the city was taken, and the children along with their mothers fell into the enemy's hands.
