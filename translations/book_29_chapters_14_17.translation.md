# Book 29 Chapter 14: Scipio Nasica and Fabius Maximus Volunteer to Outflank the Macedonians

The first man to volunteer to lead the outflanking movement was Nasica, also called Scipio, the son-in-law of Scipio Africanus; later he became a man of great influence in the Senate.

The second was Fabius Maximus, the oldest of Aemilius' sons, still only a youth, who stood up eagerly.

Aemilius was pleased and gave them not as many men as Polybius says, but as many as Nasica himself says he received, in a letter he wrote to one of the kings, about these events.

The Romans were holding their own stoutly against the force of the parma shields and the Ligurian shields.

# Book 29 Chapter 15: A Cretan Deserter Brings Intelligence to Perseus

Meanwhile Perseus, seeing that Aemilius was staying still and not reckoning with what was happening, when a Cretan deserter slipped away from the road and came to him with news of the Roman outflanking move,

was thrown into confusion. He did not move his camp, but handed over ten thousand mercenary foreigners and two thousand Macedonians to Milo and sent them off, urging them to hurry and seize the mountain passes.

Polybius says that the Romans fell on them while they were still asleep, but Nasica says there was a sharp fight and real danger on the heights.

# Book 29 Chapter 16: Battle of Pydna

Polybius says that when the moon was eclipsed, the rumor spread among many of Perseus' Macedonians that it meant the king himself was being eclipsed.

This made the Romans bolder and brought the Macedonians low in spirit.

So true is the saying that war is full of empty alarms.

# Book 29 Chapter 17: Perseus Loses His Resolve

Lucius, though he had never seen a phalanx at all until he saw one in Perseus' army, used to tell people in Rome afterward that he had never seen anything more frightening and formidable than a Macedonian phalanx, even though he had seen and taken part in many battles, like anyone else.

Many schemes seem plausible and possible in theory, but when they come to practical use, like counterfeit coins thrown into the fire, they no longer live up to their first design.

Perseus, with only one thought in mind — either to win or to die — did not hold steady in his spirit at that moment, but lost heart, like cavalry scouts who break and run.

As time went on and the strain grew heavier, Perseus' spirit gave way, like athletes in poor condition; for when danger drew near and everything had to be decided, he could not keep his courage firm.
