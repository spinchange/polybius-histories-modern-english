# Book 2 Chapter 22: Several Gallic Tribes Join Forces

Accordingly the greatest of the tribes—those of the Insubres and the Boii—at once came to an agreement and sent messengers to the Gauls living about the Alps and along the river Rhône, who are called Gaesatae because they serve for pay; for this is what the word properly means.

To their kings, Concolitanus and Aneroestes, they offered on the spot a large sum of gold, and for the future they pointed out the greatness of Rome's prosperity and the abundance of goods that would be theirs if they prevailed; and in this way they urged them on and incited them to the campaign against the Romans.

They persuaded them easily, since along with the arguments already mentioned they gave pledges of their own alliance and reminded them of the exploit of their own ancestors—

in which those men, when they took the field, not only defeated the Romans in battle but, after the battle, seized Rome itself by direct assault;

and having become masters of all it contained and having held the city itself for seven months, they at last handed it back willingly and as an act of grace, returning home with their gains unbroken and unharmed.

When the leaders among them heard this, they were so roused to the expedition that never before had more men, or more renowned, or more warlike, gone out from these regions of Gaul.

During this same period the Romans, partly from what they heard and partly from guessing at what was to come, fell into continual fears and disturbances to such a degree

that at one time they were enrolling legions and laying in supplies of grain and provisions, and at another were even leading their forces out to the frontiers, as though the enemy were already in their land—though the Celts had not yet stirred from their own country.

This movement also did not least help the Carthaginians toward securing their affairs in Iberia.

For the Romans, as has been said by us before, judging these matters the more pressing because they lay right at their side, were forced to overlook affairs in Iberia, being eager first to put matters with the Celts on a good footing.

Therefore, having secured their position with the Carthaginians through the agreements with Hasdrubal, which we have just described, they set about with one accord at this time to deal with the enemy in Italy, considering it to their advantage to settle matters decisively with them.

# Book 2 Chapter 23: The Roman Forces

The Gaesatae Gauls, having assembled a costly and heavily armed force, came across the Alps to the river Padus in the eighth year after the distribution of the land.

Now the people of the Insubres and the Boii stood nobly by their original designs, but the Veneti and the Cenomani, after the Romans had sent envoys, chose to ally themselves with the Romans.

On this account the kings of the Celts were compelled to leave behind part of their force to guard their country against the threat from these tribes.

They themselves set out confidently with the whole army, making their march toward Etruria, with about fifty thousand foot and about twenty thousand horse and chariots.

As soon as the Romans heard that the Celts had crossed the Alps, they sent out the consul Lucius Aemilius with a force toward Ariminum to watch for the enemy's advance by that route, and one of the praetors into Etruria.

For the other of the consuls, Gaius Atilius, happened to have already gone out to Sardinia with his legions.

All those in Rome were terrified, supposing that a great and fearful danger was bearing down on them. And they felt this naturally, since the old fear of the Gauls still sat deep in their souls.

So, with this thought in mind, they partly gathered and partly enrolled legions, and ordered those of the allies who were ready to stand prepared.

In general they directed their subjects to submit registers of those of military age, eager to learn the total number of the force available to them.

And of grain, missiles, and the other equipment for war they made such great provision as no one could remember before.

Everything was supplied to them readily and from every quarter.

For the inhabitants of Italy, struck with terror at the Gauls' advance, no longer thought of themselves as allies of the Romans, nor that the war was about Roman supremacy, but each people reckoned that the danger was being brought against themselves and their own city and territory. They therefore obeyed the orders readily.

# Book 2 Chapter 24: Forces Available to the Romans

In order that it may become clear, from the facts themselves, how great were the undertakings that Hannibal dared to attack, and against how mighty a power he recklessly set his eyes—yet succeeded in his purpose so far as to involve the Romans in the greatest disasters—

I must state the preparation and the size of the force available to them at that time.

With the consuls there had marched out four Roman legions, each having five thousand two hundred foot and three hundred horse.

The allies with each of the two consuls were, together, thirty thousand foot and two thousand horse.

Of the Sabines and Etruscans who came to Rome's aid for that occasion there were about four thousand horse and more than fifty thousand foot.

These they gathered and posted in advance toward Etruria, appointing a praetor as their commander.

The Umbrians and Sarsinatae who inhabit the Apennines were assembled to the number of twenty thousand, and with these were twenty thousand Veneti and Cenomani. These they stationed on the frontiers of Gaul,

so that by invading the territory of the Boii they might in turn draw off the tribes that had marched out. These, then, were the armies posted to guard the country.

In Rome itself there remained, held ready against the chances of war and serving as a reserve, twenty thousand foot of the Romans themselves and with them one thousand five hundred horse, and of the allies thirty thousand foot and two thousand horse.

Registers had been submitted of: the Latins, eighty thousand foot and five thousand horse; the Samnites, seventy thousand foot and with them seven thousand horse;

and likewise of the Iapygians and Messapians together, fifty thousand foot and sixteen thousand horse;

of the Lucanians, thirty thousand foot and three thousand horse; of the Marsi, Marrucini, Ferentani, and also Vestini, twenty thousand foot and four thousand horse.

Moreover, in Sicily and Tarentum two legions were held in reserve, each of about four thousand two hundred foot and two hundred horse.

The total number of Romans and Campanians enrolled was about two hundred and fifty thousand foot, and of horse, in addition to twenty thousand there were a further three thousand.

So that the grand total of the forces stationed in advance to defend Rome was more than one hundred and fifty thousand foot,

and about six thousand horse; while the whole multitude of those able to bear arms, both the Romans themselves and the allies, was over seven hundred thousand foot and up to seventy thousand horse.

Against these Hannibal invaded Italy with fewer than twenty thousand men. But on these matters the narrative that follows will make it possible to understand them more clearly.

# Book 2 Chapter 25: Defeat of the Romans Near Faesulae

The Celts, having descended into Etruria, advanced through the country, plundering it fearlessly; and since no one opposed them, they finally pushed on against Rome itself.

When they were already near a city called Clusium, three days' march from Rome, news was brought that the Roman forces stationed in Etruria were following close on their rear and were catching up with them.

On hearing this, they turned back to meet them, eager to engage.

When the two armies had drawn near each other, already about sunset, both then encamped at a moderate distance apart and bivouacked.

When night came on, the Celts lit fires and left their cavalry behind, instructing them that at daybreak, once they had become visible to the enemy, they should withdraw along the same track.

They themselves made a secret withdrawal toward the city of Faesulae and took up position there, intending both to wait for their own cavalry and to harass the enemy's advance by surprise.

When day came, the Romans, seeing the cavalry alone and supposing the Celts had fled, eagerly pursued the cavalry along the line of their withdrawal.

As they drew near the enemy, the Celts rose up and fell upon them, and at the outset the struggle on both sides was fierce.

In the end, as the Celts had the upper hand in daring and in numbers, it came about that no fewer than six thousand of the Romans were killed and the rest fled; most of these withdrew to a certain strong position and stayed there.

The Celts at first set about besieging them; but being in a wretched state from the night's march and their hardship and exhaustion, they turned to rest and recovery, leaving a guard of their own cavalry around the hill,

intending on the following day to besiege those who had taken refuge there, unless they should surrender of their own accord.

# Book 2 Chapter 26: Aemilius Deters the Gauls

At this time Lucius Aemilius, who was posted in the regions along the Adriatic, having heard that the Celts had invaded by way of Etruria and were drawing near to Rome, came to the rescue in all haste, arriving fortunately at the critical moment.

When he had encamped close to the enemy, those who had taken refuge on the hill, seeing his fires and grasping what had happened, quickly recovered their courage and sent some of their men, unarmed, by night through the woods to report to the general what had occurred.

He, having heard them out and seeing that no other course of deliberation was left to him in the present circumstances, ordered the tribunes to lead out the infantry at daybreak, while he himself took the cavalry and led the way for the force, making his march toward the aforementioned hill.

The leaders of the Gauls, observing the fires during the night and reckoning that the enemy was at hand, held a council.

At it King Aneroestes put forward the view that, since they had become masters of so much booty—for the quantity of captives and cattle, and also of the baggage they had, was, it seems, beyond reckoning—

they ought therefore no longer to take risks nor stake everything, but should withdraw home in safety; and once they had disposed of all this and made themselves unencumbered, they should then, if it seemed good, attack the Romans' state again in full force.

Having resolved to deal with the present situation according to Aneroestes' opinion, they took this decision in the night, broke camp before daylight, and advanced along the coast through the country of the Etruscans.

Lucius, taking up from the hill the surviving part of the army together with his own forces, judged that it was in no way to his advantage to risk a pitched battle, but rather to watch for favorable times and places as he followed, in case he might somewhere be able to harm the enemy or strip away part of the booty.

# Book 2 Chapter 27: Atilius Meets the Gauls

At this same time the consul Gaius Atilius, having sailed down from Sardinia with his legions to Pisae, was advancing with his force toward Rome, making his march toward the enemy from the opposite direction.

When the Celts were already near Telamon in Etruria, their foragers fell in with the advance party from Gaius's force and were captured;

and on being questioned they made clear to the general what had taken place and reported the presence of both armies, indicating that the Celts were very close at hand and that behind them came the forces of Lucius.

He, partly surprised at this news and partly becoming hopeful at the thought that the Celts had been caught in the middle on the march, ordered the tribunes to draw up the legions and advance at a walk to the front, as far as the ground would allow a frontal advance.

He himself, having opportunely spotted a hill lying above the road, beneath which the Celts had to march, took his cavalry and pressed on, eager to seize the crest first and to be the first to open the action, convinced that in this way he would win for himself the greatest share of the credit for the outcome.

The Celts at first, unaware of the presence of Atilius's force and supposing from what was happening that Aemilius's cavalry had marched around during the night and were seizing the positions ahead of them, immediately sent out their own cavalry and some of the light troops to contest the ground about the hill.

But on quickly learning of Gaius's presence from one of the prisoners who had been brought in, they hastily drew up their infantry, forming them up to face both ways at once—both to the rear and to the front;

for they knew that one army was following them, while they expected to meet another head-on, judging both from the reports reaching them and from what was

happening at that very moment. Aemilius's men, who had heard of the legions' landing at Pisae but did not yet expect them to be drawing near, now recognized clearly, from the struggle around the hill, that their own forces had come quite close.

Therefore they at once sent out the cavalry to help those fighting it out on the hill, while they themselves, arranging the infantry in the customary order, advanced against the enemy.

The Celts stationed the so-called Gaesatae from the Alps facing the rear, where they expected Aemilius's men, and behind these the Insubres;

and facing the front they posted the Taurisci and the Boii who dwell on this side of the Padus, in the position opposite to the others, looking toward the advance of Gaius's legions.

Their wagons and chariots they placed outside each wing, and the booty they collected onto one of the neighboring hills, setting a guard around it.

With the Celtic army thus made double-fronted, the formation proved to be not only striking in appearance but also effective.

Now the Insubres and Boii drew up wearing their trousers and their light cloaks;

but the Gaesatae, out of love of glory and bravado, threw these off and took their stand at the front of the force naked, with nothing but their weapons, supposing they would thus be most effective, since some of the ground was overgrown with brambles that would catch on clothing and hamper the use of their weapons.

At first the action at the hill itself was the whole of the fighting, plainly visible to all, since so great a number of cavalry from each army had fallen in together in confused combat.

In this engagement it happened that the consul Gaius, fighting recklessly in hand-to-hand combat, lost his life, and his head was carried off to the kings of the Celts; while the Roman cavalry, after struggling stoutly, finally prevailed over both the position and the enemy.

After this, when the infantry forces were now close upon one another, the event that took place was remarkable and astonishing—not only to those present at that very moment, but also to those able afterward to bring it before their own eyes from the accounts of it.
