# Book 29 Chapter 6: Reasons to Suspect Intrigue between Eumenes and Perseus

I have already stated that Cydas the Cretan, while serving in Eumenes' army and being highly honored by him, first met with Cheimarus, one of the Cretans serving with Perseus, near Amphipolis, and then, when he came close to the wall at Demetrias, spoke first with Menecrates and then with Antimachus.

And the fact that Herophon twice went as an envoy from Perseus to Eumenes, and that on that account most of the Romans came to hold a not unreasonable suspicion about King Eumenes, is clear from what happened in the case of Attalus:

for they allowed him to come to Rome from Brundisium and to conduct whatever business he wished, and in the end they gave him a friendly reply and sent him away,

although he had done them nothing noteworthy, either before or during the war with Perseus;

whereas Eumenes, who had provided them with the greatest services and cooperated with them most of all in the wars against Antiochus and Perseus, they not only prevented from going to Rome, but also ordered him to leave Italy within a fixed number of days, though it was midwinter.

From this it is plain that some sort of intrigue had taken place between Perseus and Eumenes, and that because of it the Romans had become so alienated from Eumenes.

What this intrigue was, and how far it went, is what we must now examine.

# Book 29 Chapter 7: Eumenes Intrigues With Perseus

It is easy to see that Eumenes would not have wished Perseus to prevail in the war and become master of everything.

Quite apart from the inherited enmity and ill will they had toward each other, the similarity of their positions was enough in itself to create distrust, jealousy, and, in general, the deepest hostility between them.

What remained was for them to deceive and outscheme each other through secret dealings; and that is what both of them did.

For when Eumenes saw Perseus in difficulty, hemmed in on every side and willing to accept any terms at all in order to end the war, and when he saw him sending embassies year after year to the Roman generals about this business,

and when he saw the Romans themselves dissatisfied with the whole course of events—partly because the war had made no progress until Paulus took command, and partly because affairs in Aetolia were in an unsettled state—

he thought it quite possible that the Romans might consent to some settlement and bring the war to an end.

And he believed that he himself was especially well suited to act as mediator and bring this about.

With these ideas in mind, he began the year before to test Perseus through Cydas the Cretan, asking how much he would be willing to pay for such a chance.

# Book 29 Chapter 8: Struggle between Eumenes and Perseus

This, I think, was the beginning of their connection.

And when one compares the two men, one the most unscrupulous and the other the most money-loving, their contest naturally became ridiculous.

For Eumenes kept holding out every hope and every kind of bait, convinced that he would catch Perseus with promises;

while Perseus rushed eagerly toward what was held out to him, yet could never bring himself to swallow it far enough to part with any money of his own.

The bargain they haggled over was this: Eumenes asked for five hundred talents as the price of standing idle in the fourth year and not cooperating with the Romans, either on land or at sea; and for ending the war altogether he demanded fifteen hundred, promising at once to give hostages and guarantees.

Perseus accepted the proposal about hostages, naming how many were to be sent, when they were to be sent, and how they were to be kept safe at Cnosus.

But as for the money, he said it would be shameful both for the giver and even more for the receiver to appear to remain neutral for pay; however, he said he would send the fifteen hundred talents by Polemocrates and his companions to Samothrace, where they could be deposited.

Since he himself was master of Samothrace, while Eumenes, like a bad doctor, preferred a retainer to payment after the treatment, he at last gave up the attempt when he found that his own cleverness was no match for Perseus' meanness.

So they parted on even terms, like good wrestlers leaving the contest of greed a draw.

Some of these details became known immediately, others later reached Perseus' close friends; and from them we were enabled to learn that avarice is, so to speak, the fastening-pin of every vice.

# Book 29 Chapter 9: The Avarice of Perseus

I will add one further point of my own: avarice not only does harm, it also makes men foolish.

For who would not remark the ignorance of both kings? How on earth did Eumenes imagine that, in spite of the deep hostility between them, he would be trusted and would receive such a large sum of money, when he could give Perseus no sufficient guarantee that it would be returned if he failed to carry out his promises?

And how could he suppose that, after taking so large a sum, he would escape the notice of the Romans?

Even if he had managed to hide it at the time, he would certainly not have escaped discovery later.

At the very least, he would necessarily have had to exchange his quarrel with Rome for the money he received; and because of that he was about to lose both the money itself and his kingdom, and very likely his life as well, once he appeared as an enemy of the Romans.

For even as things stood—when he had done nothing, but had merely entertained the scheme—he was brought into the gravest danger; what, then, ought one suppose would have happened to him if the plan had been carried through to completion?

And again, who would not marvel at Perseus for thinking anything more profitable or more urgent than to give the money and let Eumenes swallow the bait?

For if Eumenes had carried out even part of his promises and ended the war, the payment would have been well spent.

But if that hope had failed, Perseus would at least have thrown him into open hostility with Rome; for Perseus himself had complete control over making the whole transaction public.

And how valuable that would have been to Perseus, whether he succeeded in the war or failed, is easy to see:

he would have regarded Eumenes as the guilty cause of all his misfortunes, and there was no more effective way he could have avenged himself on him than by making him an enemy of Rome.

So what was the cause of such obvious folly? Avarice. What else could one say?

One man, in order to get what did not belong to him, overlooked everything else and was ready to do anything;

while the other, in order not to give anything away, was willing to endure anything and to ignore everything else.
