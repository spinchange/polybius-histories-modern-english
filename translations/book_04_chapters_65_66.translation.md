# Book 4 Chapter 65: Philip Takes Paeanium

When the camp was fully stocked with every kind of provision, he came to Oeniadae.

Pitching his camp near Paeanium, he decided to capture this place first. By mounting continuous assaults he took it by storm—a city not large in its perimeter, for it measured less than seven stades, but in the whole construction of its houses, walls, and towers inferior to none.

He razed the entire wall to the ground, and, dismantling the houses, he fitted the timber and roof-tiles onto rafts and kept floating them down the river to Oeniadae with great enthusiasm.

At first the Aetolians set out to hold the citadel at Oeniadae, having secured it with walls and the rest of their works; but as Philip drew near they took fright and abandoned it.

The king, having taken over this city as well, advanced from it and encamped in Calydonia at a stronghold called Elaeus, which had been made exceptionally secure with walls and the other defenses—Attalus having undertaken to build up its fortifications for the Aetolians.

Carrying this place too by storm, the Macedonians ravaged the whole of Calydonia and then returned again to Oeniadae.

Philip, observing how advantageously the place was situated, both for other purposes and especially for the crossings into the Peloponnese, set about walling the town.

For Oeniadae happens to lie by the sea, at the edge of Acarnania where it borders on the Aetolians, around the mouth of the Corinthian Gulf.

The town is positioned opposite the coastline of the Dymaeans in the Peloponnese, and lies nearest to the region around Araxus, for it is no more than a hundred stades distant. With his eye on these advantages, he fortified the citadel by itself,

and, throwing a wall around the harbor and the dockyards together, he undertook to connect them to the citadel, using the materials from Paeanium to manage the work.

# Book 4 Chapter 66: Philip Recalled To Macedonia

While the king was still occupied with this work, a messenger arrived from Macedonia making it clear that the Dardani, suspecting his intended expedition into the Peloponnese, were gathering forces and making great preparations, having resolved to invade Macedonia.

On hearing this, and judging it necessary to come quickly to the aid of Macedonia, he dismissed the envoys from the Achaeans, giving them the reply that, once he had dealt with what had just been reported to him, he would after that make nothing more pressing than to help them to the best of his power.

He himself broke camp and made his return march in haste, by the same route as that by which he had come.

Just as he was about to cross the Ambracian Gulf from Acarnania into Epirus, Demetrius of Pharos appeared, sailing in a single boat, having been driven out of Illyria by the Romans—matters I have made clear in the earlier parts of this work.

Philip received him kindly and told him to sail toward Corinth and from there to come through Thessaly into Macedonia; while he himself crossed into Epirus and pressed steadily onward.

When he reached Pella in Macedonia, the Dardani—learning of Philip's arrival from some Thracian deserters—took fright and at once broke up their expedition, even though they were already close to Macedonia.

Philip, on learning of the Dardani's change of heart, dismissed all the Macedonians to gather in the autumn harvest, while he himself went to Thessaly and spent the remaining part of the summer at Larisa.

It was around this time that Aemilius brought his triumph into Rome in splendid style for his Illyrian campaign, and that Hannibal, having taken Saguntum by storm, dismissed his forces into winter quarters;

while the Romans, when news of the capture of Saguntum reached them, sent envoys to demand the surrender of Hannibal from the Carthaginians, and at the same time made their preparations for the war, appointing as consuls Publius Cornelius and Tiberius Sempronius.

I have set out the particulars of these matters in the previous book; but I have now brought them forward by way of reminder, in keeping with my original undertaking, so that the corresponding events may be known.
