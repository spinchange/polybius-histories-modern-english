# Book 3 Chapter 29: The Roman Case For War

We have now set out what was said at that time by the Carthaginians; next we will give the arguments of the Romans. They did not, in fact, use these arguments on that particular occasion, because of their anger over the destruction of Saguntum, but they have been advanced often and by many of their people.

First, they argued that the agreement made with Hasdrubal could not be set aside, as the Carthaginians had the nerve to claim. For it carried no rider —

unlike the treaty of Lutatius — that "these terms shall be binding only if the Roman people also approve them." Hasdrubal had made the agreement on his own full authority, and in it stood the clause: "the Carthaginians shall not cross the river Iber

for the purpose of war." Furthermore, in the treaty concerning Sicily it was set down in writing — as the Carthaginians themselves admit — that "the allies of each party shall enjoy security from the other," and this applied not only to those who were allies at that time, as the Carthaginians construed it.

For had that been the meaning, a clause would have been added either forbidding the taking on of any allies beyond those already existing, or excluding from the terms of the treaty those admitted as allies at a later date.

But since neither provision was written in, it was plainly the case that all the allies of either party — both those existing at the time and those who would be taken on afterward — were always to be guaranteed security by both sides.

And this, indeed, is altogether the reasonable conclusion. For surely they were not going to make a treaty of a kind that stripped them of their own freedom to take on, as occasion arose, any people who proved to be suitable friends and allies;

nor, having taken such people into their trust, were they going to stand by and watch them wronged by anyone.

Rather, the binding sense of the treaty, on both sides, was this: that they would keep their hands off the allies each side already had, and that in no way would either party receive any of the other's allies into its own alliance;

and concerning those who would be taken on afterward, precisely this — that neither side should recruit mercenaries or impose levies within the other's provinces and alliances, and that security from both parties should be guaranteed to all.

# Book 3 Chapter 30: Both Sides Are in the Wrong

Since this was how matters stood, it was also agreed on all hands that the Saguntines had given themselves over to the protection of the Romans a good many years before the time of Hannibal.

And the strongest proof of this — one admitted even by the Carthaginians themselves — is that when civil strife broke out among the Saguntines, they entrusted the settlement not to the Carthaginians, even though these were close at hand and already active in the affairs of Iberia, but to the Romans, and it was through the Romans that they set their constitution right again.

Therefore, if one makes the destruction of Saguntum the cause of the war, it must be conceded that the Carthaginians went to war unjustly — both under the treaty of Lutatius, by which the allies of each party were to enjoy security from the other, and under the treaty of Hasdrubal, by which the Carthaginians were forbidden to cross the river Iber for war.

But if one makes the cause the seizure of Sardinia and the money exacted along with it, then it must certainly be granted that the Carthaginians had good reason to wage the war of Hannibal: for having yielded once to circumstance, they took the opportunity, when circumstance favored them, to defend themselves against those who had injured them.

# Book 3 Chapter 31: The Value of Such Discussions

Some readers, examining such matters without discrimination, might perhaps say that there is no need for us to go into such close detail in arguments of this kind.

For my part, if anyone supposed himself to be self-sufficient in the face of every circumstance, I would say that knowledge of past events, while admirable, was perhaps not essential.

But since no human being, whether in private or in public affairs, would dare to claim this — for even if one is fortunate in the present, no sensible person could reasonably guarantee, on the basis of present conditions, anything about the future —

for these reasons I say that knowledge of the past is not only admirable but all the more essential.

For how, when a man is himself wronged, or when his country is wronged, could he find helpers and allies; or, when he is eager to acquire something or to take the initiative, could he rouse others to assist him in his designs?

And how, when he is content with the present state of things, could he justly stir up men to confirm his own policy and preserve the established order, if he knew nothing of the record of what has happened in each people's case?

For with respect to the present, everyone is always adapting himself somehow and playing a part, so that what each person says and does is such that his real purpose is hard to discern, and in many cases the truth is heavily obscured.

But past actions, tested by the facts themselves, truly reveal the choices and judgments of each party, and show us from whom we may expect gratitude, kindness, and help, and from whom the opposite.

From these we can often, and in many situations, identify the one who will pity us, the one who will share our indignation, and the one who will see justice done on our behalf.

And this offers the greatest support to human life, both in public and in private.

For this reason, neither the writers nor the readers of history should be so concerned with the bare narration of the events themselves as with what came before, what accompanied, and what followed them.

For if you take away from history the why, the how, and the purpose for which a thing was done, and whether the outcome was a reasonable one, what is left of it becomes a display piece rather than a lesson;

it may please for the moment, but it is of no use whatever for the future.

# Book 3 Chapter 32: This History is Not Difficult to Understand

For this reason, those who suppose that my work is hard to acquire and hard to read through, on account of the number and size of the books, must be regarded as mistaken.

For how much easier it is both to acquire and to read through forty books, woven together as it were on a single thread, and so to follow clearly the events in Italy, Sicily, and Libya from the time of Pyrrhus down to the capture of Carthage,

and the events in the rest of the world from the flight of Cleomenes the Spartan, in unbroken sequence, down to the battle between the Achaeans and the Romans at the Isthmus — easier than to read or to acquire the compositions of those who write of particular events?

For quite apart from the fact that such works are many times more numerous than my memoirs, it is impossible for their readers to gain any reliable understanding from them: first, because most of these writers do not give the same account of the same matters;

and next, because they leave out the events that run parallel to one another — events which, when set side by side and compared, allow each to be assessed quite differently than under a piecemeal treatment — and because they cannot so much as touch upon the most decisive matters at all.

For we maintain that the most essential parts of history are precisely the consequences that attend events, the circumstances that accompany them, and above all the matter of causes.

We observe, for instance, that the war with Antiochus took its origin from the war with Philip; the war with Philip from the war with Hannibal; and the war with Hannibal from the war over Sicily; and that the events in between had many varied aspects, yet all of them converged upon one and the same end.

Now all this can be recognized and learned through writers of universal history, but it is impossible to learn it through those who treat the wars individually — such as the war with Perseus or the war with Philip —

unless one imagines that, merely by reading their accounts of the pitched battles themselves, one has gained a clear understanding of the conduct and disposition of the war as a whole.

But this is not so at all. Rather, by as much as real learning surpasses mere hearsay, by so much, I believe, does my history surpass the works that deal with particular events.

# Book 3 Chapter 33: Hannibal's Preparations

Now the Roman envoys — for it was from this point that I made my digression — having heard out what the Carthaginians had to say, made no other reply,

but the eldest among them, holding out the fold of his toga to those in the senate, said that there in it he carried both war and peace for them, and that he would shake out and leave behind whichever they bade him.

The king of the Carthaginians told him to shake out whichever one he pleased.

And when the Roman said he would shake out war, many of those in the senate cried out together that they accepted it. On these terms, then, the envoys and the senate parted.

Hannibal, meanwhile, while wintering at New Carthage, first dismissed the Iberians to their own cities, wishing to have them ready and eager for what lay ahead.

Second, he gave instructions to his brother Hasdrubal on how he should administer the government and rule of the Iberians, and how to manage the preparations against the Romans, in case he himself should be away somewhere.

Third, he took forethought for the security of affairs in Libya.

And reasoning with great experience and prudence, he transferred soldiers from Libya into Iberia and from Iberia into Libya, binding together the loyalty of each toward the other through this arrangement.

Those who crossed over into Libya were the Thersitae, the Mastiani,

and besides these the Oretes (an Iberian people) and the Olcades; from these nations together there were twelve hundred cavalry and thirteen thousand eight hundred and fifty infantry, and in addition eight hundred and seventy Balearians,

whom one properly calls slingers, and from this very function their nation and their island take their shared name.

Of the troops just mentioned he stationed most of them in Metagonia in Libya, and some in Carthage itself.

From the cities of the people called the Metagonitae he sent another four thousand infantry into Carthage, to serve both as hostages and at the same time as a reinforcing garrison.

In Iberia he left with his brother Hasdrubal fifty quinqueremes, two quadriremes, and five triremes — of these, thirty-two of the quinqueremes and all five triremes had crews aboard;

and also cavalry: four hundred and fifty Libyophoenicians and Libyans, three hundred Lergetes, and one thousand eight hundred Numidians of the Masylian, Masaesylian, Maccoean, and Maurusian tribes who live along the Ocean;

and infantry: eleven thousand eight hundred and fifty Libyans, three hundred Ligurians, five hundred Balearians, and twenty-one elephants.

No one should be surprised at the precision of this record — that we have used for Hannibal's operations in Iberia the kind of detail one could scarcely supply even after handling the individual matters oneself — nor should anyone condemn us in advance, supposing we have done what those historians do who lie plausibly.

For we found this record at Lacinium, set down on a bronze tablet by Hannibal during the period when he was campaigning in the regions of Italy, and we judged it to be wholly trustworthy on such matters; that is why we chose to follow this record.

# Book 3 Chapter 34: Hannibal Contacts the Celts

Hannibal, having taken every precaution for the security of his affairs both in Libya and in Iberia, now waited expectantly for the messengers being sent to him from the Celts.

For he had carefully investigated both the fertility of the land at the foot of the Alps and along the river Padus and the size of its population, and moreover the boldness of its men in war,

and — most important of all — the hostility they felt toward the Romans from the earlier war, which we recounted in the book before this one, precisely so that those who read it might be able to follow what is now about to be told.

He therefore clung to this hope and made every kind of promise, sending word diligently to the Celtic chieftains, both those on the near side of the Alps and those dwelling in the Alps themselves,

reckoning that the only way he could establish the war against the Romans in Italy was if he could get through the difficult country that lay before him, reach the regions already mentioned, and use the Celts as collaborators and allies in the undertaking before him.

When the messengers arrived and reported the willingness and eager expectation of the Celts, and declared that the crossing of the Alpine mountains, though laborious and exceedingly difficult, was nevertheless not impossible, he gathered his forces out of their winter quarters as spring approached.

And since news from Carthage had also recently reached him, his spirit was lifted and, trusting in the goodwill of his fellow citizens, he now openly summoned his forces to the war against the Romans,

making clear to them how the Romans had taken it upon themselves to demand his own surrender and that of all the officers of the army, and pointing out the fertility of the land they would be entering and the goodwill and alliance of the Celts.

When the troops eagerly rose up alongside him, he commended them, named a fixed day on which he would set out, and then dismissed the assembly.

# Book 3 Chapter 35: Hannibal Crosses the Pyrenees

Having carried out the measures already described during his winter quarters, and having arranged sufficient security for his affairs both in Libya and in Iberia, when the appointed day came he advanced, with up to ninety thousand infantry and about twelve thousand cavalry.

Crossing the river Iber, he set about subduing the nation of the Ilurgetes and the Bargusii, and also the Aerenosii and the Andosini, as far as the region called the Pyrenees.

Having brought them all under his control and taken some cities by storm — which he did with unexpected speed, but only after many great struggles and heavy loss of men —

he left Hanno as governor over all the country on the near side of the river, and as master over the Bargusii in particular, since these were the people he most distrusted because of their goodwill toward the Romans.

He assigned to Hanno, out of the forces he had, ten thousand infantry and a thousand cavalry, and left with him the baggage of the troops who were to set out with himself.

He also released to their own homes an equal number, wishing both to leave these men well disposed toward him and to hold out to the rest the hope of returning home — both to those Iberians serving with him on the march and no less to those remaining at home — so that all would set out eagerly, if ever there should be need of reinforcement from them.

Then, taking up the rest of his army, lightly equipped — fifty thousand infantry and close to nine thousand cavalry — he led them through the mountains called the Pyrenees toward the crossing of the river called the Rhone,

having a force not so much large as serviceable, and exceptionally well trained from the unbroken succession of struggles in Iberia.
