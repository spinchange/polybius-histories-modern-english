# Book 3 Chapter 60: Hannibal Attacks the Taurini

I have already given an account of the size of the army with which Hannibal invaded Italy.

After completing the invasion, he encamped at the very base of the Alps and at first set about restoring his forces.

For his entire army had not only been terribly worn down by the climbs and descents, and still more by the rough ground along the passes, but it was also in a wretched condition from the scarcity of provisions and from the lack of any care for the men's bodies.

Many had given themselves up entirely, broken by the want and by the unrelenting hardships. It had been impossible to carry an ample supply of food for so many tens of thousands across such terrain, and even what they did bring along was for the most part lost when the pack animals perished.

Thus, when he set out from the crossing of the Rhône he had some thirty-eight thousand infantry and more than eight thousand cavalry, and he lost nearly half of his force in the passes, as I stated above.

Even those who survived had become almost like wild beasts, in both their appearance and their general condition, because of the relentless hardships I have described.

Hannibal therefore took great care over their treatment and worked to restore both the spirits and the bodies of his men, and likewise of his horses.

After this, once his army had recovered its strength, he found that the Taurini, who happen to live near the foot of the mountains, were at odds with the Insubres and distrustful of the Carthaginians.

At first he invited them into friendship and alliance; but when they would not listen, he laid siege to their strongest city and stormed it within three days.

By slaughtering those who had resisted him, he instilled such fear in the neighbouring barbarian tribes that they all came over to him at once, surrendering themselves into his trust.

As for the rest of the Celts inhabiting the plains, they were eager to make common cause with the Carthaginians, in keeping with their original intention.

But since the Roman legions had by now advanced past most of them and cut them off, they kept quiet; and some were even compelled to serve alongside the Romans.

Seeing this, Hannibal decided not to delay but to push forward and to do something that would give heart to those who wished to share in the same hopes as himself.

# Book 3 Chapter 61: Approach of Scipio

While he was set on this purpose, Hannibal heard that Publius had already crossed the Padus with his forces and was close at hand. At first he refused to believe the reports,

reflecting that only a few days earlier he had left Publius behind near the crossing of the Rhône, and reckoning that the voyage from Massalia to Tyrrhenia was long and difficult to make.

Besides this, he had learned that the route from the Tyrrhenian Sea through Italy up to the Alps was a long one and hard for an army to traverse.

But as more and more men reported it, each with greater certainty, he was astonished and amazed at the consul's whole plan and the way he had carried it out.

Publius felt much the same. At first he had not even expected that Hannibal would attempt the march through the Alps with an army of foreign peoples; and even if he did dare it, he supposed the man would clearly be destroyed. So, while he was occupied with such thoughts,

when he learned that Hannibal had come through safely and was already besieging some towns in Italy, he was amazed at the man's audacity and reckless daring.

The people at Rome experienced just the same feelings about the news that reached them.

For the last report about the Carthaginians—that they had taken Saguntum—had only just ceased to be the talk of the city, and they had only just adopted measures in response to it: they had sent off one of the consuls to Libya to besiege Carthage itself, and the other to Iberia to wage war against Hannibal there. Then word arrived that Hannibal was present in Italy with his army and was already besieging some of its towns.

Since what was happening seemed to them incredible, they were thrown into immediate confusion and sent dispatches to Tiberius at Lilybaeum, informing him of the enemy's arrival and judging that he must abandon his current operations and come in all haste to aid their own affairs.

Tiberius at once assembled the crews of the fleet and sent them off, with orders to make the voyage home; and through the military tribunes he put the land forces under oath, fixing a day by which all of them were to be present at Ariminum for the night.

This is a city lying on the Adriatic, at the southern edge of the plains around the Padus.

With this upheaval breaking out on every side, and with events befalling everyone contrary to expectation, there was in every quarter no small anxiety about what was to come.

# Book 3 Chapter 62: Gallic Prisoners

At this time, as Hannibal and Publius were now drawing near to one another, each set about exhorting his own troops, presenting whatever suited the present circumstances.

Hannibal undertook to deliver his encouragement in the following way.

He gathered the men together and brought forward some young captives whom he had taken while they were harassing his march in the difficult country around the Alps.

He had treated these men harshly, preparing them for what he now intended: they wore heavy chains, were wasted by hunger, and their bodies were ruined by the lashes they had received.

Seating these prisoners in the middle, he set out before them suits of Gallic armour, of the kind in which their kings are accustomed to deck themselves when they are about to fight in single combat; and in addition he placed horses there and brought in costly cloaks.

Then he asked the young men which of them were willing to fight one another in single combat, on the terms that the victor would take the prizes set before them, while the loser would be released from his present miseries by death.

When they all cried out together that they wished to fight, he ordered them to draw lots, and commanded the two on whom the lot fell to arm themselves and fight each other.

The moment the young men heard this, they lifted up their hands and prayed to the gods, each eager that he himself might be one of those chosen by lot.

And when the result of the lottery was announced, those who had been picked were overjoyed, while the rest felt the opposite.

When the combat took place, the captives who were left behind counted the dead man no less fortunate than the victor, since the one had been released from many great miseries, while they themselves were still left to endure them.

altogether fortunate. Having by these means worked the desired disposition into the spirits of his troops, Hannibal

then came forward himself and declared that this was why he had brought in the captives: so that, seeing vividly in the misfortunes of others what was at stake, his men might make better plans regarding their own situation.

For Fortune, he said, had confined them to a struggle and a moment very like that of the captives, and had set before them prizes very like those just offered.

They must either conquer, or die, or fall alive into the hands of their enemies. The prize for victory was not horses and cloaks, but to become the most fortunate of all men, having mastered the prosperity of Rome.

If they should fall while fighting—battling to their last breath for the noblest of hopes—they would exchange this life in the press of combat without ever tasting any misery;

but for those who were beaten, and who out of a craving to stay alive chose to flee, or by some other means clung to life, every misery and every misfortune would be their portion.

For not one of them, he said, could be so unreasoning or so dull that, remembering the length of the road he had travelled from his homeland, remembering the multitude of enemies that lay between, and knowing the size of the rivers he had crossed, he could ever hope to reach his own country by running away.

Therefore he thought that, with such a hope cut off entirely, they ought to take the very same view of their own situation that they had just now taken of the misfortunes of others.

For just as, in the case of those captives, everyone counted both the victor and the dead man fortunate while pitying the survivors, so, he thought, they ought to judge their own case, and all of them go into the contest meaning above all to win, but, if that should not be possible, to die.

The hope of living on in defeat he urged them not to entertain in their minds in any way whatever.

For if they adopted this reasoning and this resolve, then both victory and survival would clearly follow for them.

For all those who, whether by deliberate choice or by necessity, have adopted such a resolve have never been cheated of mastery over their opponents.

And when it also happens that the enemy hold the opposite hope—as is now the case with the Romans, so that for most of them safety plainly lies in flight, with their homeland near at hand—then it is clear that the daring of men who have abandoned all hope would prove irresistible.

Since the mass of the men accepted both the example and the words, and took on the drive and resolve that the speaker had striven to produce, Hannibal then praised them and dismissed them; and on the next day he ordered them to break camp at first light.
