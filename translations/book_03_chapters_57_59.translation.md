# Book 3 Chapter 57: The Limits of History

Now that we have brought both the narrative and the commanders of both sides, along with the war itself, into Italy, I wish, before the battles begin, to discuss briefly a few matters that bear on my work.

For some readers will perhaps ask why, having devoted so much discussion to the regions of Libya and Iberia, I have said nothing further about the strait at the Pillars of Heracles, or about the outer sea and its distinctive features,

nor indeed about the British Isles and the production of tin there, nor about the silver and gold mines of Iberia itself—subjects on which the historians, disputing with one another, expend a great deal of discussion.

Now, I passed these over not because I think this branch of inquiry foreign to history, but, in the first place, because I did not wish to keep breaking up the narrative at every turn, nor to lead those eager to listen away from the practical subject before us;

and, in the second place, because I judged it best not to treat these matters in a scattered way or as a mere by-the-way digression, but to set aside a place and an occasion of their own for this subject and, as far as I am able, to give a truthful account of them there.

For this reason no one should be surprised if, in what follows as well, when I come to regions of this kind, I pass over this subject for the reasons already stated.

But if there are those who absolutely insist on hearing about such matters region by region and part by part, they are perhaps unaware that they are doing much the same as greedy dinner guests.

For those guests, by sampling everything set before them, neither truly enjoy any of the dishes at the moment nor draw from them any wholesome digestion and nourishment for the future, but quite the opposite;

and those who do the same with their reading likewise fail to attain, as they should, either genuine pleasure in the present or benefit for the future.

# Book 3 Chapter 58: Necessity of Refuting Past Errors

That this department, if any in history, also requires discussion and a more accurate correction is plain from many considerations, but above all from the following.

For since nearly all writers—or at any rate the great majority—have attempted to set out the peculiar characteristics and positions of the regions at the far edges of the world we inhabit, yet in many points most of them have gone badly wrong,

it is by no means proper to pass this over; rather, something must be said to them, not as a by-the-way or scattered remark, but with careful attention—and it must be said not by way of censure or rebuke,

but rather with praise and with a correction of their ignorance, recognizing that they too, had they had access to our own times, would have brought many of their statements to correction and revision.

For in earlier times one could find very few Greeks who undertook to investigate the matters of these remote regions, because the attempt was impossible.

For the dangers by sea were then many and hard to reckon, and those by land many times greater still.

But even if someone did reach the ends of the inhabited world, whether by necessity or by choice, even so he did not accomplish his purpose.

For it was difficult to become an eyewitness of more than a few of these places, since some had grown wholly barbarous and others were uninhabited; and it was harder still to learn and understand by conversation anything about what one had seen, because of the strangeness of the language.

And even if someone did gain such understanding, it was harder still than any of these things for one who had seen the places to handle the matter with restraint—to scorn marvel-mongering and sensationalism for his own glory, to value the truth above all, and to report to us nothing of what lies outside it.

# Book 3 Chapter 59: Recent Advances in Geography

Since, then, a true account of the matters just mentioned was, in former times at least, not merely difficult but very nearly impossible, it is not right to censure the historians if they omitted something or made mistakes;

rather, it is just to praise and admire them for whatever knowledge they did gain and for the way they advanced our experience of these matters in such an age.

But in our own day, the regions of Asia have become accessible through the empire of Alexander, and the remaining regions through the supremacy of the Romans, so that nearly all of them can now be reached by sea and by land;

and since men of action, released from their ambitions in military and political affairs, have through these means gained many great opportunities for investigation and for the pursuit of knowledge about the matters just mentioned,

it would only be fitting that we should have both better and truer knowledge concerning what was formerly unknown. This is what I myself will also try to do,

taking a fitting place in my work for this subject, and I will wish to inform those eager to learn more fully about the matters just mentioned;

for it was largely for this purpose that I undertook the dangers [and the hardships] that befell me in my wanderings through Libya and Iberia, and also Gaul and the sea that lies beyond these countries and borders upon them,

in order that, by correcting the ignorance of those who came before, I might make these parts of the inhabited world too familiar to the Greeks.
