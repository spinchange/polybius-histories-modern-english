# Book 11 Chapter 23: Scipio Outflanks the Enemy

He ordered the right wing to wheel outward to the right and the left wing the opposite way. He himself took from the right wing the three leading squadrons of cavalry, while Lucius Marcius and Marcus Junius took the corresponding force from the left, and in front of these they placed the usual javelin-fighters and three companies of infantry—this formation of infantry the Romans call a cohort.

The men on one side wheeled shieldwise and those on the other spearwise, then advanced straight at the enemy with great energy, while the units behind successively moved up and followed the turning movement.

When these troops had come to within no great distance of the enemy, while the Iberians in the frontal line still remained a considerable way off because they were advancing at a walking pace, the Roman forces struck both enemy wings at once exactly as Scipio had intended from the start.

The later movements, by which the troops following behind came up and formed on the same straight line with those in front and joined battle with the enemy, were opposite to one another in their arrangement, the right wing being the reverse of the left, and within each wing the infantry of the cavalry.

For on the right wing the cavalry and light troops came up spearwise and tried to outflank the enemy, while the infantry came into line the other way.

On the left wing, by contrast, the companies advanced spearwise, while the cavalry with the javelin-men came up from the rein side.

Thus by this movement the right of the cavalry and light troops on both wings effectively became their left.

The general paid little attention to that lesser point and concentrated on the greater one, namely the outflanking movement, and he reasoned correctly.

For a commander must understand what is happening, but he must use whatever movements suit the occasion.

# Book 11 Chapter 24: Carthaginians Driven From Spain

Once these troops had closed, the elephants, being pelted with javelins and thrown into confusion on all sides by the skirmishers and cavalry, suffered badly and did no less harm to friends than to enemies, since in their blind rushes they kept trampling and killing whoever came under them on either side.

As for the infantry, the Carthaginian wings were being broken, while the center, where the Libyans stood and which was the most useful part of the army, remained inactive to the end.

For they could neither leave their own post to go aid the wings because of the Iberian advance, nor, by staying where they were, could they do anything effective, since the enemy opposite them did not come to close quarters.

Even so, for a time the wings fought bravely, because on both sides the struggle concerned everything.

But by now the midday heat was at its height, and the Carthaginians were giving way because they had not taken the field when they themselves intended and had been prevented from making the proper preparations.

The Romans, by contrast, had the advantage both in strength and in morale, especially because through the general's foresight their most effective troops had been matched against the weakest of the enemy.

At first Hasdrubal's men, pressed step by step, retired in order. Then they gave way in a body and withdrew toward the foothills, and when the Romans pressed still harder they fled headlong into the camp.

If some god had not aided their escape, they would have been driven out of the camp at once.

But an extraordinary storm gathered in the sky, and when a violent and unbroken downpour burst upon them, the Romans only with difficulty got back to their own camp.

Many Romans lost their lives in the fire while trying to recover the silver and gold that had melted and run together.

When everyone was congratulating Publius after he had driven the Carthaginians out of Iberia, and was urging him to rest and take his ease on the ground that he had brought the war to an end, he said that he counted them fortunate for having such hopes,

but that he himself was now more than ever considering how he should begin the war against the Carthaginians.

For until now, he said, the Carthaginians had been making war on the Romans, but now Fortune had handed the Romans the chance to carry the war over against the Carthaginians.

And when Publius spoke with Sophax, being naturally gifted in this respect, he dealt with him so graciously and skillfully that Hasdrubal said to Sophax on the following days that Publius had seemed more formidable to him in conversation than in arms.

# Book 11 Chapter 25: Scipio Suppresses A Mutiny in Spain

When a mutiny broke out among some of the Roman camp, Publius, though he already had plenty of experience in affairs, nevertheless found himself in greater perplexity and difficulty than ever before. And this was only natural.

For just as in the case of bodies, external causes of harm—such as cold, heat, fatigue, or wounds—can be guarded against before they occur and are easy to treat once they do occur, while the swellings and diseases that arise from the body itself are both hard to foresee and hard to cure,

so we must think about constitutions and armies in the same way.

Against attacks and wars from outside, the means of preparation and defense are easily at hand for those who pay attention,

but against rivalries, factional uprisings, and disturbances arising within, the remedy is difficult and demands great dexterity and exceptional quickness of mind.

Still, there is one rule that, in my opinion, suits armies, cities, and bodies alike.

It is this: never allow any of these to remain idle and inactive for long, least of all in times of prosperity and when supplies are abundant.

But Publius, being, as I said from the beginning, exceptionally careful, sharp-minded, and energetic, gathered the tribunes and proposed some such solution to the present troubles as follows.

He said that they must promise the soldiers payment of their arrears, and in order to make the promise credible they must now openly and zealously collect the contributions already assessed on the cities for the maintenance of the whole army, as though the money were being gathered for the settlement of the pay.

The same tribunes were then to go back and ask and urge the mutineers to lay aside their folly and come to him to receive their rations and pay, whether they wished to do so in groups or all together.

Once that was done, he said, they could then decide at the proper moment what action ought to be taken.
