# Book 15 Chapter 5: Both Sides Prepare For Battle

When the Carthaginians saw their cities being sacked, they sent to Hannibal, begging him not to delay but to come to close quarters with the enemy and decide the matter by battle.

He answered the messengers that they should concern themselves with other matters and leave this to him, since he himself would judge the right moment.

A few days later he broke camp from the region near Hadrumetum, advanced, and encamped near Zama, a city about five days’ journey west of Carthage. From there he sent out three spies,

wanting to learn where the Romans were encamped and how their general had arranged his camp.

When these spies were brought before Scipio, he was so far from punishing them, as others normally would, that he assigned them a tribune and ordered him to show them everything in the camp plainly and in full.

When that had been done, he asked the men whether the officer had shown them everything carefully.

When they said he had, Scipio gave them supplies and an escort and sent them away with orders to report accurately to Hannibal all they had seen.

When they returned, Hannibal was so struck by the man’s greatness of spirit and daring that he felt a strong desire to meet Scipio in person.

So he sent a herald, saying that he wished to confer with him about the whole situation.

When Scipio heard the herald’s message, he agreed and said he would send word himself, naming the place and time, when he was ready to meet.

The herald, after hearing this, returned to Hannibal’s camp.

The next day Massanissa arrived with about six thousand infantry and four thousand cavalry.

Scipio received him warmly and congratulated him on having made all those who had formerly obeyed Syphax subject to his power.

Then he broke camp and, after reaching the city of Naragara, encamped there, having chosen a place well suited in other respects and with a water supply close at hand.

# Book 15 Chapter 6: Meeting of Hannibal and Scipio

From there Scipio sent to the Carthaginian general, saying that he was ready to meet and speak with him.

When Hannibal heard this, he broke camp and moved nearer, until he was no more than thirty stades away, and pitched his camp by a hill that seemed suitable enough for the occasion, except that the water supply was a little too distant, so that the soldiers suffered considerable hardship over it.

On the following day both commanders advanced from their camps with a small body of cavalry. Then, leaving their escorts behind, they came together in the space between, each accompanied only by an interpreter.

Hannibal greeted him first and began by saying that he wished the Romans had never desired any possession outside Italy, nor the Carthaginians any outside Libya.

For these, he said, were the finest dominions for each people, and in a sense seemed to have been marked out by nature itself.

But since they had first gone to war over Sicily, then again over Iberia, and at last, though fortune had already given them warning, had advanced so far that one side had risked the very soil of its fatherland while the other was even now doing so,

nothing remained but, if possible, by their own efforts to placate the gods and put an end to the rivalry now before them.

For his own part, he said, experience had taught him that Fortune is quick to change and can tilt great matters either way on the slightest occasion, handling human beings almost like little children.

# Book 15 Chapter 7: Conclusion of Hannibal's Speech

And yet, Hannibal said, it was chiefly for Scipio himself that he felt concern, because Scipio was still very young, because everything in Iberia and Libya had gone according to his wishes, and because he had not yet experienced the ebb of Fortune. Hannibal feared that for these reasons Scipio might not be persuaded by words that were nevertheless true.

But, he said, consider the reality from one example, not from some story of long ago, but from their own generation.

I am that Hannibal who, after the battle of Cannae, became master of almost all Italy and, after some time, came to Rome itself and encamped within forty stades of it, deliberating how I should deal with you and the soil of your fatherland.

And now I am here in Libya, meeting with you, a Roman, to discuss the safety of myself and the Carthaginians. Looking at this, do not be proud,

but deliberate like a man, with full awareness of the human condition. That means always choosing the greatest good and the least evil.

For what sensible man would willingly rush into the danger now set before you? If you win, you will add little to your own glory or to that of your country; but if you lose, you will by your own act wipe away all the noble and splendid things you have achieved before now.

So what is the purpose of all I am saying?

That everything over which we previously disputed should belong to the Romans — Sicily, Sardinia, and the territories in Iberia — and that the Carthaginians should never again wage war against Rome over these matters; and likewise that all the other islands lying between Italy and Libya should belong to Rome.

For I am convinced that these terms would be the safest for the future of Carthage, and the most honorable for you and for all the Romans.

# Book 15 Chapter 8: Scipio's reply

Such was Hannibal’s speech. Scipio answered that in neither the war over Sicily nor that over Iberia had the Romans been the aggressors; plainly the Carthaginians had been, and no one knew that better than Hannibal himself.

The gods themselves, he said, had borne witness to this by granting victory not to those who first laid unjust hands on others, but to those who defended themselves.

He too, no less than anyone else, kept before his eyes the uncertainties of Fortune and tried, so far as he could, to reckon within the limits of human life.

But if Hannibal had withdrawn from Italy before the Romans crossed into Libya and had then proposed these terms of settlement, Scipio said, he did not think Hannibal would have been disappointed in his hopes.

As it was, Hannibal had left Italy against his will, while the Romans had crossed into Libya and taken control of the open country; so the position of affairs had plainly changed greatly.

And above all, to what point had matters now come?

After Hannibal’s countrymen had been defeated and had begged for terms, written agreements had been drawn up, in which, besides the concessions Hannibal was now offering, the Carthaginians were to return prisoners without ransom, surrender their warships, pay five thousand talents, and give hostages for the performance of these terms.

These were the terms mutually agreed. Both sides had sent embassies to senate and people alike: the Romans declaring that they approved the treaty, the Carthaginians begging that it be granted to them.

The senate had agreed, and the people had ratified it. Yet after obtaining what they asked for, the Carthaginians broke the agreement and violated the truce toward Rome.

What, then, was left for him to do?

Should he, Scipio said, put himself in Hannibal’s place and remove the harshest of the imposed terms, so that by receiving a reward for lawlessness the Carthaginians might learn in future to break faith even with benefactors? Or was he to suppose that, once granted what they demanded, they would owe Rome gratitude?

Why, after receiving as suppliants exactly what they had asked for, simply because they caught some slight hope through Hannibal, they had immediately treated the Romans as enemies and opponents.

In such circumstances, he said, if even harsher terms were imposed, it might still be possible to refer the settlement back to the Roman people; but any proposal to remove what had already been laid down was not even fit for discussion.

So this was his conclusion: the Carthaginians must place themselves and their fatherland at Roman discretion, or else win by fighting.
