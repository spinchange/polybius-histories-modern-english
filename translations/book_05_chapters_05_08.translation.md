# Book 5 Chapter 5: Philip Invades Aetolia

At the same time Lycurgus had invaded Messenia, and Dorimachus, taking half the Aetolian forces with him, had set out for Thessaly, both men being convinced that this would draw Philip away from the siege of Palaea.

On this matter envoys came to the king both from the Acarnanians and from the Messenians. The Acarnanians urged him to invade Aetolian territory, to force Dorimachus to abandon his advance against Macedonia, and then to overrun and plunder all Aetolia without fear.

The envoys from Messenia begged him to come to their aid, explaining that since the annual winds had now set in, the crossing from Cephallenia to Messenia could be made in a single day.

Therefore Gorgus of Messenia and his associates argued that an attack on Lycurgus would be sudden and effective.

Leontius and his party, keeping to their own design, strongly supported Gorgus and his friends, since they believed that Philip's summer campaign would come to nothing.

For it was easy enough to sail to Messenia,

but impossible to sail back from there while the Etesian winds were blowing.

So it was clear that Philip would be shut up in Messenia with his army and forced to remain inactive for the rest of the summer, while the Aetolians would roam through Thessaly and Epirus, sacking and plundering the whole country without danger.

Such were the destructive schemes and counsels these men were offering. But Aratus, who was present, championed the opposite view.

He said Philip ought to sail to Aetolia and make that the focus of operations; for with the Aetolians campaigning abroad under Dorimachus, this was the best possible moment to invade and ravage Aetolia. The king, already distrusting Leontius because of his deliberate slackness during the siege, and recognizing in these discussions about the voyage the same malicious maneuvering, decided to follow Aratus's advice.

Accordingly he wrote to Eperatus, the Achaean general, ordering him to take the Achaeans and help the Messenians, while he himself sailed from Cephallenia and on the second day reached Leucas by night with the fleet.

After making the necessary arrangements at the channel called the Dioryctus and getting his ships through there, he sailed into what is called the Ambracian Gulf.

This gulf stretches far inland from the Sicilian Sea into the interior of Aetolia, as I have already said.

After traversing it and anchoring shortly before daybreak near the place called Limnaea, he ordered the soldiers to eat breakfast and, leaving behind most of their baggage, to make themselves ready to march lightly equipped.

He himself gathered the guides and questioned them closely about the country and the neighboring towns.

# Book 5 Chapter 6: Aristophanes the Acarnanian Joins Forces with Philip

At this time Aristophanes the general arrived, bringing the Acarnanians in full force. Because they had suffered many terrible wrongs from the Aetolians in earlier years, they were eager to avenge themselves in every possible way and to harm the Aetolians.

So they gladly seized this chance of Macedonian support and came under arms, not only those whom the law required to serve, but even some older men as well.

The Epirotes were no less eager for similar reasons; but because of the size of their country and the suddenness of Philip's arrival, they were too late to gather in time.

Of the Aetolians, as I said, Dorimachus had marched out with half their force, while he had left the other half behind, thinking this reserve sufficient to meet any unexpected attack on their cities and countryside.

The king left an adequate guard over the baggage, marched out from Limnaea in the evening, and after advancing about sixty stades made camp.

After supper and a short rest for the army, he set out again, and marching continuously through the night reached the river Achelous just as day was breaking, between Conope and Stratus, eager to strike suddenly and unexpectedly at the district of Thermus.

# Book 5 Chapter 7: A Rapid March To Thermus

Leontius and his party saw, in two ways, that Philip was likely to achieve his purpose and that the Aetolians would be unable to deal with the situation: first, because the arrival of the Macedonians had been swift and unexpected;

and second, because the Aetolians would never suppose that Philip would dare so readily to expose himself by moving against Thermus, given the strength of the country there. So they were bound to be caught off guard and wholly unprepared for what was happening.

With this in mind, and still keeping to their own purpose, Leontius and his friends thought Philip ought to encamp by the Achelous and let the army recover from the night march, since they were eager to allow the Aetolians even a brief chance to rally to the defense.

But Aratus and his party, seeing that the opportunity for attack was fleeting and that Leontius was plainly trying to obstruct the operation, protested strongly and urged Philip not to let the moment pass or lose time in delay.

The king accepted their advice and, now already offended with Leontius, kept the army marching without interruption.

After crossing the river Achelous, he pressed on hard toward Thermus; and as he advanced he also laid waste and devastated the country.

On his left he passed by Stratus, Agrinium, and the land of the Thestians; on his right, Conope, Lysimachia, Trichonium, and Phytaeum.

When he came to the town called Metapa, which lies on Lake Trichonis itself and beside the narrow pass along it, about sixty stades from the place called Thermus,

he found that the Aetolians had abandoned it; so he brought in five hundred soldiers and occupied it, intending to use it as a reserve post to secure both the entrance to and the exit from the pass.

For the whole stretch beside the lake is mountainous and rough, thickly covered with woods, and therefore the passage there is extremely narrow and hard to traverse.

After this he put the mercenaries at the head of the whole march, the Illyrians behind them, and then followed with the peltasts and the phalanx. The Cretans brought up his rear, while on the right the Thracians and light-armed troops moved parallel to him across the country.

For on the left the lake itself secured the line of march for nearly thirty stades.

# Book 5 Chapter 8: Thermus

After traversing the places just mentioned and reaching the village called Pamphia, he likewise secured this place with a garrison and advanced toward Thermus by a road that was not only steep and exceptionally rough, but also hemmed in on both sides by deep precipices,

so that in some places the path was very dangerous and narrow, the whole ascent being nearly thirty stades.

Yet he covered this stretch too in a short time, because the Macedonians made the march with great energy.

He reached Thermus late in the day, and after pitching camp he let the army plunder the surrounding villages and sweep over the plain of Thermus; likewise they looted the houses in Thermus itself, which were full not only of grain and similar provisions, but also of the finest equipment and possessions belonging to the Aetolians.

For since every year their most notable markets and festivals, and also the appointments of magistrates, were held in this place, each person stored there the most splendid of his possessions for entertaining guests and for the preparations connected with those occasions.

Quite apart from their practical use, they also believed this place gave them the greatest security, because no enemy had ever yet dared to invade these districts, and because the natural character of the site made it, as it were, the acropolis of all Aetolia.

That is why, as the country had long enjoyed peace, the buildings around the sanctuary and all the surrounding places were full of abundant goods.

So that night they encamped there loaded with every kind of profit; but on the following day they gave orders to carry off the most valuable furnishings and whatever could be transported, while they piled the rest in heaps before the tents and burned it.

They treated in the same way the arms dedicated in the porticoes: the finest they took down and carried off, some they exchanged for their own, and the rest they gathered together and burned. These amounted to more than fifteen thousand.
