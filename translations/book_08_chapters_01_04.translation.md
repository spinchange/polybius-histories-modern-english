# Book 8 Chapter 1: The Necessity of Caution in Dealing with an Enemy

It does not seem to me out of place, in view of the whole plan of this work and the purpose I set out at the beginning, to call my readers' attention to the greatness of the events and to the ambitious resolve shown by each of the two states—I mean Rome and Carthage.

For who would not be struck by the fact that, while they were engaged in so great a war over Italy, and in another no less serious over Iberia, and while both alike still faced uncertain hopes for the future and dangers equally pressing in the present,

they were nevertheless not content with the enterprises already before them, but were disputing over Sardinia and Sicily as well, and were trying to grasp everything, not only in their hopes, but also with money and military preparation?

This becomes even more astonishing when one looks at the details. The Romans had two complete armies in Italy with the consuls, and two in Iberia, of which Gnaeus held the land command and Publius the naval command.

The same thing, naturally enough, was true among the Carthaginians.

And besides this, a fleet was stationed off the coasts of Greece to watch Philip's movements, first under Marcus Valerius and afterward under Publius Sulpicius.

At the same time Appius, with a hundred quinqueremes, and Marcus Claudius, with land forces, lay ready for operations in Sicily.

Hamilcar on the Carthaginian side was doing the same.

# Book 8 Chapter 2: Immense Exertions of Rome

These facts, I think, now prove by the events themselves the truth of what I said more than once at the beginning of this history.

What I said was that it is impossible, from writers who treat history in separate parts, to gain a view of the management of the whole.

For how can anyone who reads only a narrative of Sicilian affairs or Iberian affairs, taken by themselves, come to understand either the scale of what happened or the continuous connection of events, or by what method and under what form of government Fortune accomplished the most astonishing achievement of our age?

That achievement was to bring all the known parts of the inhabited world under one rule and power—something for which no earlier precedent can be found.

It is possible, to some extent, to learn from separate accounts how the Romans took Syracuse, or how they got possession of Iberia.

But how they reached dominion over all, and what in these separate regions opposed their larger plans, and what in turn helped them, and at what moments—this is hard to grasp without a general history of events as a whole.

For the same reasons, it is not easy to understand either the greatness of their achievements or the strength of their constitution.

Taken by itself, the statement that the Romans laid claim to Iberia or to Sicily, and campaigned there by land and sea, would not seem particularly marvelous.

But when all this is happening at once, together with many other undertakings carried out at the same time by the same state and constitution, and when one considers along with them the crises and wars within their own country that were pressing upon the men directing all these affairs,

only then do the events become fully clear and truly astonishing, and only then do they receive the attention they deserve.

Let this stand as my answer to those who suppose that acquaintance with partial narratives gives them knowledge of universal and common history.

# Book 8 Chapter 3: Rome and Carthage Continue to Covet Sardinia and Sicily

When Epicydes and Hippocrates gained control of Syracuse and alienated both themselves and the other citizens from friendship with Rome, the Romans, who had by then also received news of the fall of Hieronymus, tyrant of Syracuse, appointed Appius Claudius to command the land force, while Marcus Claudius was given charge of the fleet.

These commanders pitched their camp not far from the city, and decided to attack by land in the region of the Hexapylae, and by sea against Achradina at the place called the Scythian Stoa, where the wall runs directly along the edge of the sea.

Having prepared screens, missiles, and the other equipment needed for a siege, they expected, thanks to the large number of men employed, to overwhelm the defenders with their preparations within five days. They did not reckon with the power of Archimedes, nor did they foresee that, at certain moments, a single mind can achieve more than the labor of multitudes. But in this case they learned the truth from experience.

For the city was strong because the wall ran all around along high ground and overhanging ridges, to which even without opposition one could not easily approach except at certain fixed points.

The man just mentioned had prepared such defenses within the city, and likewise against those attacking from the sea, that the defenders would never need to waste time in improvising, but could meet every move of the enemy immediately and with resources already in hand.

Appius therefore advanced with screens and ladders toward the section of wall adjoining the Hexapylae on the eastern side.

# Book 8 Chapter 4: History of Universal Supremacy Must Be a Universal History

Marcus, meanwhile, sailed against Achradina with sixty quinqueremes, each one filled with men carrying bows, slings, and javelins, by which they meant to drive back those fighting from the battlements.

At the same time, with eight quinqueremes whose oars had been removed on one side—on the right side of some and the left of others—and which had then been lashed together in pairs along the cleared sides, they brought up against the wall, by means of the outside banks of oars, the machines called sambucae.

The construction of these engines was as follows.

They prepared a ladder four feet wide and of a height equal to that of the wall from the point where it would be landed; they fenced both sides of it and roofed it over with protective coverings, and then laid it slantwise across the touching sides of the coupled ships so that it projected far beyond their prows.

Pulleys with ropes were attached high up on the masts.

Then, when they came near the point of action, they fastened the ropes to the top of the ladder, and men standing on the sterns drew on them through the pulleys, while others standing on the prows braced the machine and secured its lifting with props.

Then, using the oars on the outer sides of the paired ships, they brought the vessels close inshore and tried to set the engine against the wall.

At the top of the ladder there was a platform protected on three sides by wicker shields, on which four men stood and fought against those on the battlements who tried to prevent the sambuca from being set in place.

Once they had planted it and stood above the level of the wall, these men unfastened the side shields on either hand and stepped out onto the battlements or towers.

The rest followed them up the sambuca, since the ladder was firmly secured by ropes to both ships.

The device naturally got this name, because when it is raised the combined shape of ship and ladder looks very much like the musical instrument called a sambuca.
