# Book 12 Chapter 2: The Lotus

Polybius of Megalopolis, having been an eyewitness, reports much the same things as Herodotus and his followers about the so-called lotus in Libya, writing as follows in the twelfth book of his *Histories*:

The lotus is a tree that is not large, but rough and thorny; it has a green leaf resembling that of the rhamnus (buckthorn), somewhat deeper in color and broader.

The fruit, at first, is like ripe white myrtle-berries both in color and in size,

but as it grows it turns crimson in color, and in size it comes to resemble round olives, and it has a very small stone.

When it has ripened, they gather it: the portion meant for the household slaves they pound up with groats and pack into jars, while the portion meant for the free men they likewise store away after removing the stone, and on this they feed.

The food is much like a fig or a date, but superior in fragrance.

A wine is also made from it by soaking and crushing it in water—sweet and pleasant to the taste, like good mead—which they drink without diluting it with water.

It cannot keep for more than ten days; and so they make it only in small quantities, as the need arises. They also make vinegar from the fruit.

# Book 12 Chapter 3: Timaeus Wrong about Libya

II. The Errors of Timaeus concerning Africa and Corsica

Anyone would marvel at the excellence of the country's soil,

but of Timaeus one would have to say that he has not only failed to investigate the facts about Libya, but is also childish and utterly incapable of reasoning, and still bound fast to those old traditional tales we have inherited—that Libya is wholly sandy, parched, and barren.

The same applies to its animals. For the abundance of horses, cattle, and sheep, and along with these of goats, throughout the country is so great that I doubt whether its match could be found anywhere else in the inhabited world,

because many of the tribes in Libya do not rely on cultivated crops, but draw their livelihood from their flocks and live among their flocks.

And indeed, who has not recorded the number and strength of its elephants, lions, and leopards, and besides these the beauty of its antelopes and the size of its ostriches? Of these there is nothing whatever in Europe, whereas Libya is full of the creatures just mentioned.

About all this Timaeus, having recorded nothing, gives an account that—as though deliberately—is the very opposite of what truly exists.

And just as he has improvised about the affairs of Libya, so too he has done about the island called Cyrnus (Corsica).

For in mentioning that island in his second book, he says that there are in it many wild goats, sheep, and wild oxen, and besides these deer, hares, wolves, and some of the other animals, and that the people busy themselves in hunting these and pass their whole way of life in such pursuits.

But on the island in question there is not so much as a wild goat or ox—nor even a hare, a wolf, a deer, or any other animal of the kind—except foxes, rabbits, and wild sheep.

greater part, underground. All the animals on the island, however, do seem to be wild, for the following reason.

The herdsmen cannot follow alongside their flocks at pasture, because the island is wooded, precipitous, and rugged; but whenever they wish to gather them, they take their stand at convenient spots and call the animals together with a trumpet, and every one of them runs unfailingly to its own trumpet.

Consequently, when people sail up to the island and see goats or cattle grazing untended, and then try to seize them, the animals will not let them approach, since they are unaccustomed to them, but flee.

But when the herdsman, seeing the men disembark, sounds his trumpet, the animals at once rush headlong and run together to the trumpet. This gives the impression of wildness—about which Timaeus, having inquired poorly and perfunctorily, made a slapdash report.

Their obedience to the trumpet is nothing to wonder at: for in Italy too those who raise swine manage the pasturing in the same way.

The swineherds do not follow at the heels of the beasts, as among the Greeks, but go on ahead sounding the horn at intervals, while the beasts follow behind and run together toward the sound;

and so great a familiarity develops in the animals toward their own horn that those who hear of it for the first time are amazed and can scarcely believe it.

For owing to the abundance of labor and the other resources available, the droves of swine in Italy turn out to be enormous—especially along the coast, among the Etruscans and the Gauls—so that a single breeding sow rears a thousand pigs, and sometimes even more.

For this reason they make their drives out from the night-pens according to litters and ages.

Hence, when several droves are brought forward to the same place, they cannot keep them sorted by litter, but they get mixed up with one another both during the drives out and at pasture, and likewise during the drives home.

From these circumstances the device of the horn has been contrived by them for separating the animals, whenever they get mixed together, without toil or trouble.

For when one of the herdsmen goes off toward one quarter sounding his horn, and another turns aside toward another, the beasts separate of their own accord and follow their own horns with such eagerness that it is impossible by any means to force or to check their rush.

But among the Greeks, in the oak-forests, when the herds get mixed together while chasing the mast, the man who has more hands and seizes the opportunity gathers up his neighbor's beasts along with his own and drives them off.

And sometimes a thief, lying in wait, drives them off, without the herdsman in charge even realizing how he lost them—because the animals stray far from their drivers in their scramble for the mast, just when it is beginning to fall. But enough of this.
