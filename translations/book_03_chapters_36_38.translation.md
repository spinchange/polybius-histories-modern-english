# Book 3 Chapter 36: Geography of Hannibal's March

So that my account may not become entirely obscure through ignorance of the localities involved, I ought to state from where Hannibal set out, through which regions—and how many of them—he passed, and into which parts of Italy he came down.

But I should not merely cite the bare names of the places, rivers, and cities, as some historians do, supposing that this alone is enough in every case to produce knowledge and clarity.

In my view, where places are already familiar, the listing of their names contributes something—and indeed a great deal—toward recollection; but where they are unknown, reciting their names has in the end no more force than meaningless, jangling noises.

For when the mind has nothing to fasten upon and cannot fit what is said onto anything familiar, the narrative becomes ungraspable and dead.

We must therefore point out a method by which it will be possible, in speaking of unknown places, to lead our listeners to some degree toward genuine and recognizable ideas.

The first and most important means of knowledge,

and one moreover that is common to all people, is the division and ordering of the heaven that surrounds us, by which all of us who are worth anything at all recognize east, west, south, and north.

The second is that, by assigning the regions of the earth to each of these distinctions and always carrying in our minds whatever is mentioned onto one of the directions named above, we arrive at familiar and customary notions about things unknown and

unseen. Now that these matters have been laid down concerning the earth as a whole, it follows that we should divide up the inhabited world that is ours by the same principle and bring it to our listeners' attention.

This world is divided into three parts with three names: one part of it they call Asia, the second Libya, and the third Europe.

The boundaries that separate these are the river Don, the Nile, and the strait at the Pillars of Heracles.

Asia, then, happens to lie between the Nile and the Don, and falls within that span of the surrounding heaven that lies between the summer sunrise (northeast) and the south.

Libya lies between the Nile and the Pillars of Heracles, and falls within the southern portion of the heaven and, continuously, within the region of the winter sunset (southwest), as far as the equinoctial setting point, which falls at the Pillars of Heracles.

These two regions, then, viewed in broad terms, occupy the southern zone of our sea, running from east toward west.

Europe lies opposite both of them, facing toward the north, and stretches continuously from east to west.

Its largest and deepest portion lies directly beneath the north, between the river Don and the Narbo—a river not far to the west of Marseilles and of the mouths of the Rhone, through which the aforesaid river empties into the Sardinian Sea.

From the Narbo and the surrounding country the Celts dwell, as far as the mountains called the Pyrenees, which extend without a break from our sea to the outer sea.

The remaining part of Europe, from those mountains onward—the part adjoining the west and the Pillars of Heracles—is enclosed by our sea and the outer sea; and the portion stretching along our sea as far as the Pillars of Heracles is called Iberia,

while the portion lying along the outer sea, which is called the Great Sea, has no common name, because it has only recently been explored. It is inhabited entirely by barbarous and populous peoples, about whom I will give a detailed
