# Book 9 Chapter 5: Hannibal Advances Upon Rome

With this in mind, Hannibal sent a letter-carrier into Capua. He persuaded one of the Libyans to desert to the Roman camp and from there make his way into the city, taking precautions to ensure that the letter would be delivered safely.

For he was very anxious that, if the Capuans saw him departing without understanding the plan, they might lose heart as though he had given them up and so surrender to the Romans.

So he wrote to explain the purpose behind his withdrawal and sent the Libyan off, in order that once the Capuans understood his intention and the reason for the separation, they might endure the siege with confidence.

Meanwhile, when news reached Rome from the region of Capua that Hannibal had pitched camp opposite their lines and was besieging their forces, everyone in the city was thrown into alarm and fear, since the crisis seemed likely to affect the whole war.

Accordingly all alike bent their efforts toward this one object, devoting themselves to dispatches, reinforcements, and military preparations.

The Capuans, however, after receiving the letter brought by the Libyan and learning the Carthaginian plan, held firmly to their former resolve, deciding to put this fresh hope to the test.

Then, on the fifth day after his arrival, Hannibal had his men take supper, left the campfires burning, and so conducted the withdrawal that none of the enemy noticed what was happening.

He marched through Samnium at speed and without interruption, constantly sending his advance troops ahead to scout the route and seize key positions.

Thus, while the minds of those in Rome were still fixed on Capua and the operations there, he crossed the Anio unnoticed, came close to the city, and encamped no more than forty stades from Rome.

# Book 9 Chapter 6: Rome Saved by Luck

When this happened and the news reached Rome, the people of the city were thrown into complete confusion and terror.

The whole affair was sudden and wholly unexpected, since Hannibal had never before advanced so near the city; and another thought also crept in among them—that the enemy could never have dared come so close and with such confidence unless the Roman armies around Capua had been destroyed.

So the men occupied the walls and the strong positions in front of the city, while the women went around the temples imploring the gods, brushing the floors of the sanctuaries with their hair.

For this is their custom whenever some overwhelming danger overtakes their country.

But just when Hannibal had encamped and was planning on the following day to test the city itself, an extraordinary and fortunate accident occurred that helped save Rome.

Gnaeus and Publius had already completed the enrollment for one army, and the soldiers had sworn to appear in arms at Rome on that very day; and for the other army the enrollment and examination were then in progress.

As a result, a large number of men happened to be assembled at Rome at exactly the right moment. The commanders boldly led them out, encamped in front of the city, and checked Hannibal's advance.

At first the Carthaginians pushed forward, not altogether despairing of taking Rome itself by storm. But when they saw the opposing force drawn up in battle order and quickly learned from a prisoner what had happened, they abandoned the attack on the city and instead marched through the countryside, ravaging the land and burning the houses.

At the beginning they gathered into their camp an immense quantity of plunder, as though they had come upon a hunting ground of exactly the kind no one had ever expected an enemy to enter.

# Book 9 Chapter 7: Hannibal In Sight of Rome

But afterward, when the consuls boldly ventured to encamp only ten stades away, Hannibal—having now amassed a great quantity of booty, having lost hope of any success against the city, and above all calculating that by this time Appius ought, according to the original plan, to have learned of Rome's danger and either to have raised the siege altogether and come to help, or at least to have left part of his force behind and hurried off with the greater part—broke camp at dawn.

He believed that whichever of these things happened would serve his purpose well, and so set his army in motion before daybreak.

Publius and his men, however, broke down the bridges over the river just mentioned and forced Hannibal to take his army across the stream itself.

As the Carthaginians were making the crossing they pressed hard upon them and caused them great trouble.

They could not accomplish anything decisive because of the number of Hannibal's cavalry and the readiness of the Numidians on every kind of ground; but after stripping away a fair part of the booty and killing about three hundred of the enemy, they then withdrew to their camp.

After that, thinking that the Carthaginians were retreating in haste out of fear, they followed in their rear along the foothills.

Hannibal at first kept up his speed, eager to carry out the plan before him; but on the fifth day, when word was brought that Appius and his army were still remaining at the siege, he halted, waited for the pursuers to come up, and while it was still night attacked their camp.

He killed many of them and drove the rest out of the camp.

When day came and he saw that the Romans had withdrawn to a strong hill, he gave up any thought of continuing the attack against them.

Instead he marched through Daunia and Bruttium and suddenly appeared in the region of Rhegium, coming so unexpectedly that he came within a little of taking the city itself, cut off all those who had gone out into the countryside, and in that visit made himself master of a large number of Rhegines.

# Book 9 Chapter 8: Epaminondas and Hannibal Compared

At this point, I think, one may fairly pause to note the courage and ambition both of the Carthaginians and of the Romans in war.

For just as everyone admires the Theban Epaminondas because, after coming to Tegea with the allies and observing that the Lacedaemonians themselves had all assembled at Mantinea, together with their allies, in order to fight the Thebans,

he ordered his own men to take supper early and led out the army as night was just coming on, pretending that he was hurrying to seize certain advantageous positions for the coming battle,

but after creating that impression among the rank and file actually marched straight toward Sparta itself,

and, arriving unexpectedly at about the third hour, found Sparta empty of defenders, forced his way as far as the marketplace, and occupied the parts of the city that slope toward the river,

yet then suffered a reversal because some deserter got through during the night to Mantinea and informed King Agesilaus what was happening, and help arrived just at the critical moment when the city was on the point of being taken,

so that he failed in that hope; but afterward, having had breakfast by the Eurotas and allowed his troops to recover from their exertions, he again set out by the same road in the opposite direction,

calculating that since the Lacedaemonians and their allies had gone off to help Sparta, Mantinea would in turn be left undefended—which is exactly what happened.

So he encouraged the Thebans, pushed on with a vigorous night march, and came up to Mantinea about midday, when it was completely abandoned by those who might have defended it.

But at this very moment the Athenians, eager under their alliance to take part with the Lacedaemonians in the struggle against the Thebans, arrived.

And just as the Theban vanguard was reaching the sanctuary of Poseidon, which lies seven stades in front of the city, it so happened that the Athenians appeared on the hill above Mantinea.

At the sight of them, the Mantineans who had been left behind only then found courage enough to mount the walls and block the Theban assault.

For this reason historians are justified in their criticism of those events, when they say that the commander did everything that a good general could do, and that Epaminondas was superior to his enemies but inferior to Fortune.
