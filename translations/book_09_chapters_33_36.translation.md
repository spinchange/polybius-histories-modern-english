# Book 9 Chapter 33: Defence of Macedonian Policy

If you cannot do that, then for the present at least remain quiet. But since these men have dared from ancient times onward to accuse the house of Macedon, I think it necessary first to speak briefly on those matters, so as to remove the ignorance of those who have trusted what was said.

Chlaeneas said, then, that Philip son of Amyntas became master of Thessaly through the misfortune of the Olynthians.

I, however, believe that through Philip not only the Thessalians, but the rest of the Greeks as well, were saved.

For when Onomarchus and Philomelus seized Delphi in impious and lawless fashion and made themselves masters of the god's treasures, who among you does not know that they assembled so great a force that no Greek any longer dared face them?

Indeed, together with their impiety toward the divine, they were on the point of becoming masters of all Greece as well.

At that moment Philip voluntarily offered himself, overthrew the tyrants, secured the sanctuary, and became the cause of Greek freedom, as the facts themselves have testified both then and to posterity.

For it was not because Philip had wronged the Thessalians, as this man dared to claim, but because he was a benefactor of Greece, that all chose him as commander by land and sea—an honor no human being before him had received.

Yes, by Zeus, but he came into Laconia with an army.

Not by his own choice, as you know, but because the friends and allies in the Peloponnese repeatedly invited and summoned him, until at last he consented.

And once he came, Chlaeneas, look at how he handled the situation. Though he could have made use of the eagerness of the neighboring cities to destroy this people's land and humble their city, and could have done so with the greatest gratitude from others,

he did not at all give himself over to such a course. Instead, after overawing both sides, he compelled them by argument, in the common interest, to bring their disputes before an assembly.

And he did not appoint himself judge over the matters in dispute, but set up a common court selected from all the Greeks. Is that something worthy of reproach and denunciation?

# Book 9 Chapter 34: Contrast Between Alexander and the Aetolians

Again, you bitterly reproached Alexander because, believing himself to have been wronged, he punished the city of Thebes.

But you made no mention of the fact that he exacted vengeance from the Persians for the insults done to all the Greeks,

or that he delivered all of us together from great evils by subduing the barbarians and taking away from them the resources with which they had ruined the Greeks—sometimes by setting the Athenians and their ancestors against others, sometimes the Thebans—and in the end made Asia subject to the Greeks.

And as for his successors, how do you dare even mention them? In the circumstances of their times they often became the cause of good to some and of harm to others.

Perhaps other people might have some right to resent them for this. But it is not at all fitting for you to do so, since you have never been responsible for any good to anyone, but for many evils to many people and many times over.

Who was it that called in Antigonus son of Demetrius to divide up the Achaean nation?

Who was it that made oaths and treaties with Alexander of Epirus for the enslavement and partition of Acarnania?

Was it not you? And what other people has ever sent out such commanders as you have—men who even dared to lay hands on sacred and inviolable sanctuaries?

Timaeus plundered the sanctuary of Poseidon at Taenarum and the sanctuary of Artemis at Lusi.

Pharycus and Polycritus robbed, one the precinct of Hera at Argos, the other that of Poseidon at Mantinea.

And what of Lattabus and Nicostratus? Did they not, in time of peace, violate their agreement at the assembly of the Pam-Boeotians, carrying out deeds worthy of Scythians and Gauls? Nothing of this kind was done by the Successors.

# Book 9 Chapter 35: Services of Macedonians To Greece

And though you can make no defense against any of these charges, you still speak grandly of how you resisted the barbarians in their attack on Delphi, and claim that for this Greece ought to be grateful to you.

But if gratitude is owed to the Aetolians for this one service, what honor, and how great an honor, should be judged due to the Macedonians, who spend most of their lives never ceasing to fight the barbarians for the safety of the Greeks?

For who does not know that Greek affairs would always have been in great danger if we had not possessed the Macedonians as a barrier and the ambition of their kings?

And the clearest proof of this is that, as soon as the Gauls learned to despise the Macedonians after defeating Ptolemy called Ceraunus, they at once despised the rest as well, and Brennus marched with his army straight into central Greece. And this would often have happened if the Macedonians had not stood in the way.

Still, though I could say much about past events, I think this is enough.

Yet of all Philip's acts, these men chose to reproach him with the destruction of a temple as evidence of impiety, without adding a word about their own outrage and lawlessness in what they did to the temples and sacred precincts at Dium and Dodona.

That was the first thing that ought to have been said. But the wrongs you yourselves suffered you recount to these men at length, exaggerating what happened, while the many times greater wrongs that you committed first you pass over in silence,

well knowing that everyone always lays the blame for acts of violence and damage upon those who first set their hands to unjust deeds.

# Book 9 Chapter 36: Sparta Should Have Allied Herself with the Macedonians

I wish to mention Antigonus only so far as not to seem to despise what happened or to treat so great a deed as a side issue.

For my own part, I do not believe that any greater benefaction than the one Antigonus conferred on you is even recorded in history; indeed, the deed seems to me beyond all comparison.

And one may see this from the facts. Antigonus went to war with you and afterward defeated you in battle. By force of arms he became master of both your territory and your city.

He might have exercised all the rights of war. Yet so far was he from doing anything terrible against you that, besides other acts, he expelled the tyrant and restored your laws and ancestral constitution.

In return, at the common festivals, calling the Greeks as witnesses, you yourselves proclaimed Antigonus your benefactor and savior.

What, then, ought you to have done? I will say what seems true, gentlemen; and you, I trust, will bear with me. For I say this now not from any desire to heap insults on you without cause, but because the pressure of events forces me to speak for the common good.

What do I mean? That both in the previous war you ought to have allied yourselves not with the Aetolians but with the Macedonians, and that now too, when invited, you ought to attach yourselves to Philip rather than to these men.

Yes, by Zeus—but then you would be violating treaties.

And which would be the worse thing to do: to disregard the private agreements you have made with the Aetolians, or the arrangements ratified before all the Greeks and consecrated on a public stele?

And how is it that you shrink from breaking with these men, from whom you have received no prior benefit, while you feel no shame toward Philip and the Macedonians, through whom you possess even the power to deliberate at all today?

Or do you think it necessary to do what is just toward friends?

Yet it is not so holy a thing to keep written pledges secure as it is unholy to make war on those who saved you—and that is exactly what the Aetolians have now come here to urge you to do.
