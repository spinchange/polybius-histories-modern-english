# Book 36 Chapter 1: The Third Punic War

Some may perhaps ask why I have not made use of dramatic speeches in narrating a subject and events of such importance.

That is in fact what most historians do: they arrange the arguments on both sides and present the speeches that could have been delivered.

As for myself, I have shown in many places in my history that I do not reject this practice altogether, since I have often reported speeches and political statements made by statesmen.

But that I do not choose to do this in every case will now be clear: for it would not be easy to find a subject more striking than this one, or material more abundant and better suited to comparison.

Indeed, no form of composition could be more convenient for me.

Yet I do not think it right for statesmen, on every question brought before them, to be ready with arguments and long elaborations, but rather to speak in the way appropriate to the particular occasion;

and in the same way I do not think it right for historians to display their skill for the amusement of their readers, but rather to devote all their effort to discovering and recording what was truly said, and, among such words, only those that are most timely and most essential.

# Book 36 Chapter 2: The Romans Find a Justification for War

This principle having long since been firmly fixed in everyone's mind, the Romans were looking for a suitable occasion and a decent pretext for outsiders.

They were in fact especially concerned with this point, and rightly so.

For the way in which a war is entered upon, as Demetrius says, makes victory seem more glorious and defeat more secure; but if the pretext is shabby and unworthy, the opposite result follows.

So at that time, while they were disputing among themselves about how outsiders would judge the matter, they nearly gave up the war.

# Book 36 Chapter 3: War With Carthage Resolved Upon

While the Carthaginians had long been deliberating how they ought to answer the Roman demand, the people of Utica forestalled their plan by surrendering their city to the Romans, and this left the Carthaginians in utter helplessness.

For there was still one hope left to them: if they also submitted and placed themselves under Roman control, they thought they would certainly win Roman favour;

for they had never done this even in their greatest extremity, when they had been beaten and the enemy stood at their walls, and had never before surrendered their country.

But they lost the benefit of this idea because the Uticans had got there first.

For now nothing would seem novel or surprising to the Romans if the Carthaginians merely did the same thing.

So, with only two evils left to choose from — to accept war bravely or to surrender their independence — they held a long and anxious secret debate in the council-house and appointed two envoys with full powers, instructing them to do what seemed best for the country's advantage in the circumstances.

These envoys were Gisco, called Strytanus, Hamilcar, Misdes, Gillimas, and Mago.

When they arrived in Rome from Carthage, they found that war had already been decided and that the generals had actually set out with their forces.

Since circumstances no longer allowed them any room for negotiation, they made an unconditional surrender.

# Book 36 Chapter 4: Meaning of Surrender

We have spoken before about what surrender to Roman control means, but it is necessary to remind the reader briefly here as well.

Those who give themselves up to Roman control first surrender their territory and the cities in it, along with all the men and women living in that land and in those cities.

Likewise they surrender rivers, harbours, sanctuaries, and tombs — in short, everything — so that the Romans become complete masters of all these things, while those who surrender are left masters of nothing at all.

When the Carthaginians had made such a surrender and were then summoned into the council-house, the commander conveyed the Senate's decision: since they had acted wisely, the Senate granted them liberty and the protection of their laws, and likewise all their territory and all their other property, both public and private.

The Carthaginians were pleased to hear this, believing that, since they had had to choose between two miserable alternatives, the Senate had treated them well by allowing them the most necessary and important things.

But then the commander explained that they would receive these concessions only if they sent three hundred hostages to Lilybaeum within thirty days — sons of men from the council and the senate — and obeyed whatever orders the consuls might give them.

For a time they were at a loss as to what those orders would be, but they set out at once, eager to report the matter to their countrymen as quickly as possible.

When they reached Carthage and explained everything in detail to the citizens,

the people thought that, in everything else, the envoys had acted with proper caution; but the fact that there had been no mention at all of the city itself filled them with great alarm and distress.
