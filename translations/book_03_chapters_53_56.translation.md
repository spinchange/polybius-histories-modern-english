# Book 3 Chapter 53: The White Rock

While they were crossing, there came a moment when Hannibal's entire force would have been completely destroyed, had he not still been wary to some degree and, foreseeing what lay ahead, kept the baggage train and the cavalry in the vanguard and the heavy infantry in the rear.

Because these troops were posted as a rearguard, the damage that occurred was less severe, for they absorbed the onslaught of the barbarians.

Even so, despite this precaution, a great number of men, pack animals, and horses were destroyed.

For since the high ground was held by the enemy, the barbarians moved along the slopes parallel to the column, rolling rocks down on some and striking others at close range with stones, and so threw them into utter confusion and peril,

to such a degree that Hannibal was forced to spend the night with half his force beside a certain fortified white rock, cut off from the horses and the pack animals, standing guard over them, until through the course of the whole night these were at last drawn out of the ravine with great difficulty.

The next day, once the enemy had withdrawn, he rejoined the cavalry and the pack animals and pushed on toward the highest passes of the Alps. He no longer ran into any large concentrated body of the barbarians, but was harassed by them piecemeal and from place to place.

Some of them, attacking from the rear and others from the front whenever the opportunity arose, would carry off part of the baggage. His elephants were of the greatest service to him in this:

for at whatever point in the march these animals happened to be, the enemy did not dare to approach that part of the line, terrified by the strange sight of the creatures.

On the ninth day he reached the summit of the pass, encamped there, and remained for two days, wishing both to rest the survivors and to wait for the stragglers to catch up.

During this time it happened that many of the horses that had bolted in panic and many of the pack animals that had thrown off their loads unexpectedly came back, having followed the tracks of the army, and rejoined the column.

# Book 3 Chapter 54: The Descent Into Italy

...and rejoined the camp. By now, since the setting of the Pleiades was drawing near, the snow was already gathering on the peaks; and seeing his men in low spirits, both because of the hardship they had already endured and because of what they still expected to face,

Hannibal tried to gather and encourage them, having one single resource for the purpose: the clear sight of Italy. For Italy lies so close beneath the mountains just mentioned that, when both are viewed together, the Alps appear to stand like a citadel over the whole of Italy.

Therefore, by pointing out to them the plains around the Po, and by reminding them in general of the goodwill of the Gauls who inhabited them, and at the same time indicating the location of Rome itself, he succeeded to some extent in restoring the men's confidence.

The next day he broke camp and began the descent. In the course of it he no longer encountered any enemy, except those who did damage by stealth, but because of the terrain and the snow he lost a number of men not much smaller than those he had lost on the ascent.

For since the descent was narrow and steep, and the snow made it impossible for each man to see where he was stepping, anyone who strayed from the path or slipped was carried over the cliffs.

Still, they endured this hardship, being by now accustomed to such troubles.

But when they came to a place that neither the elephants nor the pack animals could get past because of its narrowness—where the cliff face had fallen away for nearly a stade and a half, having been broken before but having now collapsed even more freshly—there once again the men fell into despair and confusion.

At first the Carthaginian general attempted to go around the difficult ground; but when fresh snow fell and made this route also impossible to travel,

# Book 3 Chapter 55: Snow

he abandoned the attempt. For what was happening was peculiar and out of the ordinary. The new snow of that year had just fallen on top of the old snow, which had remained from the previous winter; the fresh snow was easy to cut through, both because it was recent and soft and because it had not yet gained any depth.

But once they had trodden through this and stepped onto the snow underneath, which had hardened, they no longer cut through it; instead they slid on the surface, both feet skidding at once, just as happens on level ground to people walking through mud.

And what followed from this was still more difficult.

For the men, being unable to break through the lower snow, whenever they fell and tried to brace themselves on their knees or hands in order to get up, only slid all the more, with every support giving way at once, since the ground sloped steeply downward.

The pack animals, on the other hand, when they fell, did break through the lower snow as they struggled to rise; but once they had broken through, they remained stuck there with their loads, as if frozen in place, both because of the weight and because of the firmness of the old snow underneath.

So, giving up this hope, he encamped on the ridge, clearing away the snow on it, and then set the men to work and, with great toil, built up a road along the cliff face.

For the pack animals and the horses he made a passage wide enough in a single day. He therefore led these across at once and, encamping at the spots that were already below the snow line, sent them off to pasture;

while he brought up the Numidians in relays to work on the road, and only after three days of painful labor did he get the elephants across. These were in a wretched state from hunger,

for the highest parts of the Alps and the areas reaching up toward the passes are entirely without trees and completely bare, because the snow lies there continuously in both summer and winter, whereas the parts about halfway down the slopes, on both sides, are wooded, bear trees, and are altogether habitable.

# Book 3 Chapter 56: Hannibal Reaches the Plains

Hannibal then gathered his whole force together and continued the descent, and on the third day after passing the cliffs just described he reached the level country.

He had lost many of his soldiers to the enemy and at the river crossings during the march as a whole, and many more to the cliffs and difficult terrain in the Alps—not only men, but still greater numbers of horses and pack animals.

In the end, having made the entire march from New Carthage in five months, and the crossing of the Alps in fifteen days, he boldly descended into the plains around the Po and the territory of the Insubres,

with the surviving portion of his army: twelve thousand Libyan infantry, about eight thousand Iberian infantry, and cavalry numbering altogether not more than six thousand, as he himself sets out on the inscribed column concerning his numbers at Lacinium.

At the same time, as I stated above, Publius, having left his forces to his brother Gnaeus and urged him to hold fast to affairs in Iberia and to wage war vigorously against Hasdrubal, sailed back himself with a few men to Pisae.

He then marched through Etruria, took over from the praetors the legions that were stationed there and campaigning against the Boii, and arrived at the plains around the Po; pitching his camp, he kept watch on the enemy, eager to engage them in battle.
