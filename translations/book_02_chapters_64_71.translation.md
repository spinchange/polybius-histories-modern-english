# Book 2 Chapter 64: Cleomenes Invades Argos

After the capture of Megalopolis, Antigonus wintered in the city of the Argives. As soon as the spring season set in, Cleomenes assembled his army, gave them the encouragement the moment called for, marched the troops out, and invaded the territory of the Argives.

To most observers this seemed a reckless and audacious move, given the strength of the fortified positions guarding the approaches; but to those who reasoned correctly it was both safe and shrewd.

For he could see that Antigonus had disbanded his forces, and so he knew clearly two things: first, that he would make his incursion without danger; and second, that as the countryside was ravaged right up to the walls, the Argives, watching what was happening, were bound to grow indignant and blame Antigonus.

If, then, it turned out that Antigonus could not endure the abuse of the crowds and came out to risk a battle with the forces he had on hand, it was plain from a reasonable calculation that Cleomenes would win easily.

But if Antigonus held to his strategy and stayed quiet, Cleomenes reckoned he could withdraw home safely, having struck fear into his opponents and instilled confidence in his own troops. And that is exactly what happened.

As the land was being plundered, the crowds gathered and railed at Antigonus. But he, acting very much like a true commander and king, set nothing above acting according to sound reason, and kept his calm.

And Cleomenes, in keeping with his original plan, having ravaged the country, struck fear into his opponents, and made his own forces confident in the face of the coming danger, returned home in safety.

# Book 2 Chapter 65: Battle of Sellasia

When summer set in, and the Macedonian and Achaean troops had reassembled from their winter quarters, Antigonus took up his army and advanced with his allies into Laconia.

He had ten thousand Macedonians for the phalanx, three thousand peltasts, three hundred cavalry, a thousand Agrianians along with these, an equal number of Gauls, and in all three thousand mercenary infantry and three hundred mercenary cavalry.

There were also picked Achaean troops—three thousand infantry and three hundred cavalry—and a thousand Megalopolitans equipped in the Macedonian fashion, commanded by Cercidas of Megalopolis; and among the allies, two thousand Boeotian infantry,

two hundred Boeotian cavalry, a thousand Epirote infantry and fifty cavalry, the same numbers from Acarnania, and sixteen hundred Illyrians, under the command of Demetrius of Pharos.

The entire force thus came to about twenty-eight thousand infantry and twelve hundred cavalry.

Cleomenes, anticipating the attack, had secured the other passes into the country with garrisons, trenches, and felled trees.

He himself, with his army, was encamped at the place called Sellasia, his whole force amounting to about twenty thousand men, having judged from reasonable calculation that the enemy would make their incursion by that route—which is just what came to pass.

Two hills lie right at the entrance of the pass, one called Evas and the other Olympus,

with the road between them, running alongside the river Oenus, leading to Sparta. Cleomenes threw a trench and palisade across the front of both hills. On Evas he posted the perioeci and the allies, placing his brother Eucleidas in command of them, while he himself held Olympus with the Lacedaemonians and the mercenaries.

On the level ground beside the river, on either side of the road, he stationed the cavalry together with a portion of the mercenaries.

When Antigonus arrived and surveyed both the strength of the ground and the way Cleomenes had so skillfully occupied the advantageous points with every appropriate part of his army—so that the whole layout of the encampment resembled the defensive front of good armored fighters—

for nothing had been left undone for either attack or defense; rather, it was at once an effective battle line and a position hard to approach—

he therefore gave up any attempt to test it and engage at the first onset.

# Book 2 Chapter 66: The Two Armies Line Up

He gave up the idea of a rash attack, and instead pitched camp a short distance away, covering his front with the river called Gorgylus. There he remained for some days, studying both the peculiarities of the ground and the differences between the two armies,

while at the same time making some show of intended maneuvers to draw out the enemy's plans.

But since he could never catch any point off guard or unarmed—because Cleomenes was always ready to counter every move—

he abandoned that approach, and at last both sides agreed to settle the matter by battle: for Fortune had brought together in these two men commanders highly gifted and well matched.

Against the troops on Evas, then, the king drew up the bronze-shielded Macedonians and the Illyrians, arranged in alternating companies, placing Alexander, son of Acmetus, and Demetrius of Pharos in command.

Behind these he set the Acarnanians and the Epirotes, and behind them stood two thousand Achaeans, taking up position as a reserve.

His cavalry, around the river Oenus, he stationed opposite the enemy's horse, giving them Alexander as commander and flanking them with a thousand Achaean infantry and an equal number of Megalopolitans.

He himself, with the mercenaries and the Macedonians, resolved to make his attack along Olympus against the troops with Cleomenes.

So he placed the mercenaries in front and stationed behind them a double phalanx of Macedonians, one line close behind the other; he did this because of the narrowness of the ground.

The signal arranged for the Illyrians to begin their assault on the hill was the moment they should see a linen flag raised from the direction of Olympus—for these men had been positioned during the night along the river Gorgylus, right at the foot of the hill—

and likewise for the Megalopolitans and the cavalry, when a scarlet flag was raised by the king.

# Book 2 Chapter 67: Battle Begins

When the moment for action came, the signal was given to the Illyrians, and those responsible passed the word to do what was required; at once they all revealed themselves and began the assault on the hill.

But the light-armed troops who had been posted from the start with Cleomenes's cavalry, seeing that the Achaean companies were left exposed in their rear, fell upon them from behind and brought into the gravest danger the men who were forcing their way up toward the hill—

since Eucleidas's men were bearing down on them from the higher ground in front, while the mercenaries pressed on their rear and laid into them vigorously. At this moment Philopoemen of Megalopolis grasped what was happening,

and, foreseeing what would come of it, first tried to point out the looming result to the officers in command.

When no one paid him any heed—because he had never yet held a command and was still quite young—he rallied his own fellow citizens and charged the enemy boldly.

When this happened, the mercenaries who were pressing the rear of the men climbing the hill quickly heard the shouting, saw the cavalry locked in combat, abandoned the troops in front of them, and ran back to their original posts to help their own cavalry.

Once this happened, the body of Illyrians, Macedonians, and the others advancing with them, now free of harassment, surged eagerly and confidently against their opponents.

From this it later became clear that Philopoemen was responsible for the success against Eucleidas.

# Book 2 Chapter 68: Skill and Gallantry of Philopoemen

This is why, they say, Antigonus afterward tested Alexander, the commander of the cavalry, by asking him why he had begun the engagement before the signal was given.

When Alexander denied it, saying that some young man from Megalopolis had taken the initiative against his own wishes, Antigonus replied that the young man had acted like a good commander by seizing the moment, while Alexander, though a commander, had behaved like any ordinary youngster.

In any case, when Eucleidas's men saw the companies advancing, they failed to make use of the advantages of the ground.

What they should have done was meet the enemy and fall upon them well in advance, throwing their massed ranks into confusion and breaking them up, and then fall back step by step, steadily withdrawing in safety to the higher ground above.

For in that way, having spoiled and disrupted the special character of the enemy's equipment and formation beforehand, they would easily have routed them, thanks to the natural advantage of the terrain.

But they did none of this; instead, as though victory were already theirs for the taking, they did the very opposite.

For they stayed in their original position on the heights, intent on catching the enemy as high up as possible so that their flight would be all the longer over steep and broken ground.

What happened, naturally, was the reverse: by leaving themselves no room for retreat, and by letting the companies come up fresh and still in close order, they fell into such difficulty that they had to fight it out on the very crest of the hill against men forcing their way up.

Then, as soon as they were pushed back even a step by the weight of the enemy's armor and formation, the Illyrians immediately gained their footing, while Eucleidas's men were driven back, having left themselves no ground for retreat or maneuver.

As a result they were quickly turned and forced into a disastrous flight, since the ground over which they had to withdraw was for a long stretch steep and hard to cross.

# Book 2 Chapter 69: Defeat of Cleomenes

At the same time the cavalry engagement was reaching its climax, with all the Achaean cavalry—and Philopoemen above all—fighting outstandingly, since the whole struggle was for their own freedom.

In the course of this, Philopoemen's horse was struck a fatal blow and fell, and as he fought on foot he received a severe wound through both thighs.

Meanwhile the two kings, over on Olympus, at first carried on the fighting with their light-armed troops and mercenaries, of which each side had about five thousand.

These engaged sometimes in detachments, sometimes in their full bodies, and the fighting on both sides proved exceptional, as both the kings and their armies watched the battle in full view of one another.

They competed with each other man against man and unit against unit in displays of courage.

But when Cleomenes saw that his brother's troops had fled and that the cavalry on the level ground were on the verge of giving way, he grew alarmed that he might be attacked from every side and was forced to tear down his front fortifications and lead out his whole force in line along one side of the camp.

The light-armed troops of both sides were recalled from the ground between by the trumpet, and the phalanxes, raising their war cry together and leveling their pikes, charged one another.

A fierce struggle followed: at one moment the Macedonians fell back step by step, hard pressed by the courage of the Laconians, at another the Lacedaemonians were thrust back by the sheer weight of the Macedonian formation.

At last Antigonus's men locked their pikes together and, employing the special feature of the double phalanx, drove in with force and pushed the Lacedaemonians out of their entrenchments.

The rest of the army fled headlong, cut down as they went; but Cleomenes, with a few cavalry about him, withdrew safely to Sparta with them.

When night came on he went down to Gythium, where the means for a sea voyage had long since been prepared against just such an outcome, and sailed off with his friends to Alexandria.

# Book 2 Chapter 70: End of the Introductory Period

Antigonus, having taken Sparta at the first assault, treated the Lacedaemonians in everything else with magnanimity and kindness; and after restoring their ancestral constitution, within a few days he marched out of the city with his forces, on receiving word that the Illyrians had invaded Macedonia and were ravaging the country.

So it is that Fortune always tends to decide the greatest matters contrary to expectation.

For on this occasion too, if Cleomenes had only put off the engagement by a very few days, or had withdrawn from the battle into the city and held out against the situation even for a short while, he would have kept his throne.

As it was, Antigonus reached Tegea, restored their ancestral constitution there as well, and on the second day went on from there to Argos, arriving just in time for the festival of the Nemean games.

There, having received every honor that pertains to undying fame and glory, both from the Achaean League as a body and from each of the cities individually, he hurried off in haste to Macedonia.

He caught the Illyrians still in the country, met them in a pitched battle, and won the day; but having thrown himself so passionately into encouraging and shouting to his men during the engagement, he brought on a hemorrhage and some such condition, and not long afterward died of illness,

having held out fine hopes of himself to all the Greeks, not only for his service in the field but still more for his whole character and nobility.

He left the kingdom of Macedonia to Philip, son of Demetrius.

# Book 2 Chapter 71: Conclusion of Book 2

Why have I dwelt at such length on the war just described?

Because these times join directly onto the events I am about to relate, and it seemed useful—indeed necessary, in keeping with my original plan—to make plainly clear to everyone the state of affairs that then existed concerning the Macedonians and the Greeks.

About the same period, Ptolemy too died of illness, and Ptolemy surnamed Philopator succeeded to the kingdom.

There died as well Seleucus, son of the Seleucus who bore the surnames Callinicus and Pogon; and Antiochus, his brother, succeeded to the kingdom in Syria.

For something very similar happened to these men as had happened to the first to hold these realms after the death of Alexander—I mean Seleucus, Ptolemy, and Lysimachus.

Those men all died around the 124th Olympiad, as I noted above, and these around the 139th.

Now that I have worked through the introduction and preliminary foundation of the whole history—through which it has been shown when, how, and for what reasons the Romans, after gaining mastery over Italy, first began to lay hands on affairs beyond it and first dared to dispute the sea with the Carthaginians,

and at the same time have set out the condition then existing among the Greeks and Macedonians, and likewise among the Carthaginians—

it would now be fitting, having arrived at these times in accordance with my original plan—the times in which the Greeks were about to enter upon the Social War, the Romans upon the Hannibalic War, and the kings in Asia upon the war for Coele-Syria—

to mark off this book in line both with the outline of the events already recounted and with the deaths of the rulers who had managed affairs up to that point.
