# Book 4 Chapter 17: Factions At Cynaetha

...Achaea, together with the Illyrians. Meanwhile Ariston, the general of the Aetolians, pretended to take no notice of any of these events and stayed quietly at home, claiming that he was not making war on the Achaeans but keeping the peace—a foolish and childish way to behave.

For it is plain that a man of this sort is bound to look silly and absurd whenever he imagines that mere words can hide what the facts make obvious.

Dorimachus and his men, however, marched through Achaean territory and suddenly appeared before Cynaetha.

Now the Cynaethans, who are Arcadians, had for many years been gripped by relentless and violent civil strife. The rival parties had repeatedly inflicted on one another massacres and banishments, and on top of these the plundering of property

and redistributions of land. In the end the faction that favored the Achaean cause prevailed and took control of the city, holding the walls with a garrison and a commandant brought in from Achaea.

Such was the situation when, a little before the Aetolians arrived, the exiles sent appeals to those in the city, begging to be reconciled with them and allowed to return to their homeland.

The party holding the city was persuaded and sent an embassy to the Achaean League, wishing to carry out the reconciliation with the League's approval.

The Achaeans readily gave their consent, convinced that both sides would be well disposed toward them—since the party in control of the city had placed all its hopes in the Achaeans, while those about to return would owe their safe restoration to Achaean agreement.

So the Cynaethans dismissed the garrison and the commandant from the city, made peace, and brought back the exiles, who numbered close to three hundred, after exacting from them the pledges reckoned the most binding among mankind.

But once they were home, the returned exiles did not even wait for some cause or pretext to arise that might give the appearance of a fresh quarrel breaking out; on the contrary, the moment they were back they immediately began plotting against their country and against the very men who had saved them.

Indeed, it seems to me that at the very moment when, over the sacrificial victims, they were exchanging oaths and pledges with one another, they were already turning over in their minds this crime against the gods and against those who had trusted them.

For as soon as they were admitted to citizenship, they at once began drawing in the Aetolians and intriguing with them to seize the city, eager to bring utter ruin upon those who had saved them and upon the city that had nurtured them.

# Book 4 Chapter 18: Treason of the Cynaethans

...to destroy it root and branch. The deed they carried out with the following piece of daring, and in the following way.

Some of the returned exiles had been appointed polemarchs. It is the duty of this office to close the gates, to keep custody of the keys during the time they remain shut, and to spend the day on station at the gatehouses.

The Aetolians, then, fully equipped and with their ladders ready, watched for their opportunity;

and the polemarchs from among the exiles, after butchering their fellow officers at the gatehouse, threw open the gate.

When this happened, some of the Aetolians rushed in through the gate, while others set up their ladders, forced their way over them, and took possession of the walls. The whole population of the city,

panic-stricken at what was happening, were helpless and at a loss how to meet the situation. They could not concentrate on resisting those pouring in through the gate because of the men attacking the walls, nor could they defend the walls because of the men forcing their way through the gate.

The Aetolians, having for these reasons quickly gained control of the city, did amid all their unjust acts one wholly just thing: the very men who had let them in and betrayed the city to them they slaughtered first of all, and plundered their property.

They treated all the others of that party in the same way; and finally, quartering themselves in the houses, they ransacked them for valuables and tortured many of the Cynaethans whom they suspected of having money, plate, or anything else of unusual worth hidden away.

Having ruined the Cynaethans in this fashion, they broke camp and moved off, leaving a garrison to guard the walls, and advanced in the direction of Lusi.

On reaching the temple of Artemis, which lies between Cleitor and Cynaetha and is regarded as inviolable by the Greeks, they threatened to carry off the goddess's herds and the other property around the temple.

But the people of Lusi prudently handed over some of the goddess's sacred objects and so dissuaded the Aetolians from their impiety, sparing themselves any irreparable harm.

The Aetolians accepted the gift, immediately moved on, and pitched their camp before the city of the Cleitorians.

# Book 4 Chapter 19: More Aetolian Outrages

About this same time Aratus, the general of the Achaeans, sent off to Philip an appeal for help, began mustering his picked troops, and sent for the contingents that Sparta and Messene were bound to supply under the terms of their agreements.

The Aetolians at first tried to persuade the Cleitorians to abandon the Achaeans and choose alliance with them instead.

But when the Cleitorians flatly refused to entertain the proposal, the Aetolians launched assaults, set their ladders against the walls, and tried to take the city by storm.

When the defenders inside resisted bravely and boldly, the Aetolians gave way to circumstances, broke camp, and marched off once more toward Cynaetha, driving away with them, even so, the goddess's herds.

At first they offered Cynaetha to the Eleans; but when the Eleans refused to take it, they resolved to hold the city themselves and put Euripidas in command of it.

Afterward, however, taking fright at reports of the relief force coming from Macedonia, they set fire to the city and withdrew, advancing once more toward Rhium, where they had decided to make their crossing.

Now Taurion, learning of the Aetolian invasion and of what had been done at Cynaetha, and observing that Demetrius of Pharos had sailed in from the islands to Cenchreae, urged him to come to the aid of the Achaeans and, after hauling his galleys across the Isthmus, to fall upon the Aetolians as they made their crossing.

Demetrius, who had made his return from the islands profitable but inglorious—since the Rhodians had put to sea against him—gladly agreed to Taurion's request, once Taurion had undertaken to cover the cost of getting the galleys across.

So Demetrius hauled his ships over the Isthmus but arrived two days too late for the Aetolian crossing; after raiding some places along the Aetolian coast in advance, he sailed back to Corinth.

As for the Lacedaemonians, they shirked sending the relief force as arranged, dispatching only a very small body of cavalry and infantry, merely to keep up appearances.

Aratus, though he had his Achaean troops, dealt with the situation more as a politician than as a general.

For up to this point he kept quiet, holding back and mindful of his earlier disaster, until Scopas and Dorimachus had accomplished everything they intended and returned home—even though they made their march through country easy to attack, full of narrow defiles, where a single trumpeter would have sufficed to bring them down.

As for the Cynaethans, although they had fallen into great misfortunes and great calamities at the hands of the Aetolians, they were nevertheless thought to have suffered, of all people, the most deservedly.

# Book 4 Chapter 20: Music in Arcadia

Since the Arcadian nation as a whole enjoys a certain reputation for virtue among all the Greeks—not only for the hospitality and kindness of their character and way of life, but above all for their reverence toward the gods—

it is worth pausing briefly to consider the savagery of the Cynaethans, and to ask how it was that, while admittedly Arcadians, they so far surpassed the rest of the Greeks at that time in cruelty and lawlessness.

In my view it was because they were the first and only Arcadians to abandon those practices which their ancestors had wisely devised and thoughtfully adapted, in keeping with nature, to all the inhabitants of Arcadia. I mean music—

genuine music, that is—which it is beneficial for all people to practice, but for Arcadians is positively essential. For we must not suppose, as Ephorus does—

who, in the preface to his whole work, tossed off a remark utterly unworthy of him—that music was introduced among mankind to deceive and bewitch them.

Nor should we imagine that the ancient Cretans and Spartans had no good reason for substituting the pipe and rhythmic movement for the trumpet in war,

or that the early Arcadians acted without purpose in giving music so prominent a place in their whole constitution that they made it a compulsory part of upbringing not only for children but for young men up to the age of thirty, though in all other respects they led the most austere of lives.

For these facts are familiar to everyone: that among the Arcadians, almost alone, the children are first trained from infancy to sing, in accordance with custom, the hymns and paeans by which each community celebrates its local heroes and gods in the traditional way;

and that afterward they learn the compositions of Philoxenus and Timotheus, and dance with great enthusiasm each year to the Dionysian pipers in the theaters—the boys in the boys' contests, the young men in what are called the men's contests.

Likewise throughout their whole lives, at their gatherings, they provide their entertainment not so much through imported performers as by their own efforts, each in turn being called on to sing.

And while they consider it no disgrace to admit ignorance of any other accomplishment, singing is something they can neither disclaim—since all are compelled to learn it—nor, having learned it, openly refuse to practice, because that too is reckoned shameful among them.

Moreover, practicing marching-songs to the pipe and in formation, and laboring at dances, they display these every year in the theaters to their fellow citizens, with shared attention and at public expense.
