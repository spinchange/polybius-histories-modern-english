# Book 32 Chapter 13: Scipio's Liberality

Olympiad 155, year 4. I. Affairs in Italy.

When Gaius Fannius and his colleagues returned from Illyria and reported that the Delmatae were so far from making any amends for the charges brought against them by those who said they were being continually wronged, that they would not even grant them a hearing at all, declaring that they had nothing in common with the Romans;

and further explained that no lodging or provisions had been given them, and that the Delmatae had even taken away by force the horses they had obtained from another city;

and that they had been ready to come to blows, had they not yielded to the circumstances and made their departure with great restraint and calm;

when the senate heard this report, it was seriously angered by the disobedience and rudeness of the Delmatae, but above all it judged the moment favorable for making war on them, for several reasons.

For these parts of Illyria, the regions stretching toward the Adriatic, had been left entirely unvisited by the Romans

ever since they drove out Demetrius of Pharos; and besides, they did not want the people of Italy to grow soft in any way through a long peace.

For this was then the twelfth year since the war with Perseus and the settlement in Macedonia. Therefore they resolved

to begin a war against the people just mentioned, in order both, as it were, to renew the drive and eagerness of their own people, and by striking the Illyrians with fear to force them to obey Roman commands.

These, then, were the reasons why the Romans went to war with the Delmatae; but to outsiders they presented the war as one undertaken because of the outrage committed against their envoys.

# Book 32 Chapter 15: Scipio's Courage

II. War between Prusias and Attalus.

After defeating Attalus, Prusias advanced against Pergamum, prepared a lavish sacrifice, and offered it at the sanctuary of Asclepius.

After sacrificing oxen and obtaining favorable omens, he then returned to camp.

But on the next day he drew up his army against the Nikephorium, destroyed all the temples and sacred precincts of the gods, and plundered the statues and even the stone images.

At last he even carried off the statue of Asclepius itself, an exceptional work by Phyromachus,

and took it away for himself, though the day before he had poured libations before it, sacrificed oxen to it, and prayed to it, asking, as one would expect, that the god be gracious and favorable to him in every way.

I have said elsewhere, when speaking of Philip, that such states of mind are a kind of madness.

For to sacrifice at one and the same time and try by such acts to appease the divine power, bowing down and extravagantly flattering the sacrificial tables and altars—as Prusias used to do, dropping to his knees and acting in a soft, unmanly way—

and at the same time to ravage those same holy things and, by destroying them, display one's insolence toward the gods—how could anyone fail to call this the work of a frenzied anger and a mind driven out of all reason?

And that is exactly what happened with Prusias then. Having accomplished nothing brave in his assaults, but having behaved basely and without courage in his dealings both with gods and with men, he moved his army on to Elaea.

And after testing Elaea and making some attacks, but being unable to achieve anything because Sosander, the king's companion, had entered the city with soldiers and blocked his attempts, he marched off to Thyatira.

On the way back he violently plundered the sanctuary of Artemis at the Sacred Village.

In the same way, near Temnus, he not only plundered the precinct of Apollo Cynneius, but also destroyed it with fire.

After doing this he returned home, having made war not only on men but on the gods as well.

And on the return journey Prusias' infantry also suffered terribly from famine and dysentery, so that the vengeance that met him at once for these acts seemed to have been sent by heaven. [Text breaks.]

# Book 32 Chapter 16: Scipio's Success Due to his Character

After his defeat by Prusias, Attalus chose his brother Athenaeus and sent him with Publius and his party to explain to the senate what had happened.

For when Andronicus had come to Rome and reported the enemy's first advance,

the men there paid no attention, but suspected that Attalus, wishing to be the one to attack Prusias himself, was laying the groundwork for excuses and trying to prejudice opinion against him in advance.

And when Nicomedes and Antiphilus and the other envoys sent by Prusias insisted that none of this was true, the stories told about Prusias were believed even less.

But after some time, when the matter became clearer, the senate, being uncertain because of the conflicting reports, sent Lucius Apuleius and Gaius Petronius as envoys to inspect how matters stood between the kings just mentioned.
