# Book 15 Chapter 33: The Massacre of Agathocles and Family

The beginning of bloodshed and slaughter came about by the following chance occurrence.

One of Agathocles’ attendants and flatterers, a man named Philo, came staggering out into the stadium still drunk.

Seeing the rush of the crowd, he said to those standing by that, just as before, they would regret it again if Agathocles appeared.

Some of those who heard him abused him, others shoved him about, and when he tried to defend himself, some tore off his cloak while others thrust their spears into him and stabbed him.

As soon as he was dragged insultingly into the middle, still writhing, and the crowd had tasted blood, they all began eagerly waiting for the arrival of the others.

Not long after, Agathocles himself came first, led in bound. The moment he entered, some rushed at him and stabbed him on the spot — an act done more by friends than by enemies, since they spared him the punishment he truly deserved. After him came Nicon,

then Agathocleia naked with her sisters, and after them all the rest of the family.

Last of all they dragged Oenanthe from the Thesmophorium into the stadium, bringing her in naked on horseback.

When they were all handed over to the crowd together, some bit them, some stabbed them, some gouged out their eyes, and whenever one fell, they tore the limbs apart, until they had mangled every one of them.

For there is something terrible in the savagery of the people of Egypt when their passions are stirred.

At that same time certain girls who had grown up with Arsinoe learned that Philammon, the man who had supervised the queen’s murder, had arrived three days earlier from Cyrene, and so they rushed to his house.

Forcing their way in, they beat Philammon to death with stones and clubs, strangled his little son, and then dragged Philammon’s wife naked into the street and killed her too.

Such was the end of Agathocles, Agathocleia, and their relatives.

# Book 15 Chapter 34: Why Agathocles Died As He Did

I am not unaware of the marvels and embellishments with which some writers have treated these events in order to astonish their hearers, giving more attention to ornamental comment than to the facts themselves and what truly matters in them.

Some refer everything to Fortune and place before the eyes the uncertainty and slipperiness of her workings; others dwell on the unexpectedness of what happened and try to supply the events with causes and probabilities.

But I did not intend to handle the matter in that way, because Agathocles was neither distinguished for military daring and power,

nor successful and admirable in administration, nor finally remarkable for courtly cleverness and villainous intrigue — the sort of ability by which Sosibius and many others managed to survive under one king after another. In Agathocles’ case the opposite of all this was true.

For he had indeed gained an astonishing advancement because of Philopator’s incapacity to rule.

But after gaining that position, and after receiving the most favorable opportunity to preserve his power following the king’s death, he lost both his authority and his life through his own cowardice and indolence, being condemned in a very short time.

# Book 15 Chapter 35: Agathocles Not a Good Example of Mutability of Fortune

Therefore one ought not to attach an elaborate moralizing discourse to men of this kind, as I said, in the way one might with Agathocles and Dionysius of Sicily and certain other men whose public careers won a real name.

For one of those men began from a common and humble station, and Agathocles — as Timaeus says mockingly — was a potter who left wheel, clay, and smoke behind and came as a young man to Syracuse.

At first, in their own generations, both became tyrants of Syracuse, a city that at that time had attained the highest distinction and greatest wealth.

Afterward they were thought sovereigns of all Sicily and masters of certain parts of Italy as well.

Agathocles even made an expedition against Libya and at last died still in possession of those high fortunes.

That is why they say that Publius Scipio, the first conqueror of the Carthaginians, when asked whom he considered the most practical men of affairs and the boldest with sound judgment, answered: the Sicilians Agathocles and Dionysius.

With men like those, it is fitting to draw readers to attention, perhaps also to speak of Fortune and of human affairs, and to add an instructive reflection. But with the men we have been discussing, that is wholly out of place.

# Book 15 Chapter 36: Agathocles Was Not An Important Person

For these reasons we have rejected any expanded treatment of Agathocles.

And not least because all startling reversals have a single force at first encounter that is worth notice, but afterward the hearing and sight of them become not only useless but positively wearisome.

For those who present anything to ear or eye ought to aim at two ends, benefit and pleasure; and in history, where this obligation especially applies, excessive dwelling on shocking disasters misses both.

For who would wish to imitate extraordinary catastrophes? And again, no one enjoys continually seeing and hearing things that are unnatural and beyond common human expectation.

We are indeed eager once, and at first, to see or hear such things, in order to learn that what seemed impossible is actually possible.

But once we believe it, no one enjoys lingering over the unnatural, and no one would wish to encounter the same thing again and again.

So what is told ought to be either admirable or pleasant; but an excessive account of disasters beyond these belongs more properly to tragedy than to history.

Still, perhaps one should pardon those who do not attend either to nature or to the general course of events throughout the inhabited world.

For they suppose that the greatest and most astonishing events in history are precisely those they themselves happen to meet with or hear about from others, and so they fix all their attention on these.

Accordingly they do not notice that they are spending too much speech on things that are neither novel, since others have already recounted them, nor able to give either profit or pleasure. But let this be enough on that point.

IV. Affairs of Asia

King Antiochus at the beginning of his reign seemed a man of vast designs, boldness, and energetic execution.
