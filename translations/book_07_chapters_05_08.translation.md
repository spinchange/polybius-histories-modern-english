# Book 7 Chapter 5: Hieronymus Decides For War

When the Romans learned of this, they again sent envoys to him, protesting that he must not violate the treaties made with his forefathers.

Hieronymus therefore called the council together and put before it the question of what should be done.

The native members kept quiet, fearing the poor judgment of the ruler; but Aristomachus of Corinth, Damippus the Spartan, and Autonous the Thessalian urged him to stand by the treaties with Rome.

Andranodorus alone said that they must not let the opportunity slip, and that the present moment was the only one in which it was possible to win dominion over Sicily.

After he had said this, Hieronymus asked the party around Hippocrates what opinion they held. When they said they agreed with Andranodorus, the deliberation was brought to an end. In this way war against Rome was decided upon.

But because he wanted to seem clever rather than clumsy in answering the ambassadors, he fell into such a blunder that he was plainly about not only to displease the Romans, but to offend them outright.

For he said that he would keep the treaty if, first, they restored all the gold they had received from his grandfather Hiero; second, if they repaid for the whole past period the grain and the other gifts they had received from him; and third, if they acknowledged that the land and cities on this side of the river Himera belonged to the Syracusans.

On these terms the envoys and the council separated. From that time onward Hieronymus and his circle actively pushed the war forward, gathering forces, arming them, and preparing the rest of the necessary supplies.

# Book 7 Chapter 6: Description of Leontini

The city of Leontini as a whole faces north,

and through the middle of it there runs a level valley, in which stand the public offices, the courts, and, in general, the market-place.

On each side of this valley stretches a ridge with steep escarpments all along it; and the level tops of these hills, above the brows, are full of houses and temples.

The city has two gates. One stands at the southern end of the valley I mentioned and leads toward Syracuse; the other is at the northern end and leads to the so-called Leontine plains and the farming country.

Below one of the escarpments, the western one, there runs a river called the Lissus.

Parallel to it, immediately under the cliff, stand many continuous rows of houses; and between these houses and the river lies the road mentioned above.

# Book 7 Chapter 7: Fall of Heronymus

Some of the writers who have written about the downfall of Hieronymus have made a very long story of it and arranged it with a great deal of sensationalism, recounting the omens said to have come before his accession and the misfortunes of the Syracusans,

and then writing in tragic style about the savagery of his character, the impiety of his actions, and, on top of everything, the shocking and terrible circumstances of his fall, so that one would think neither Phalaris nor Apollodorus nor any other tyrant had ever been harsher than he.

And yet he was only a boy when he came to power, and after that lived no more than thirteen months.

During that time it is possible that one man or another was put to the torture and that some of his friends and some other Syracusans were killed; but it is not likely that there was any extreme excess of lawlessness or some unparalleled kind of impiety.

One should indeed say that in character he was exceptionally reckless and unlawful, but he ought not to be compared with any of the tyrants mentioned above.

It seems to me that writers of histories about particular episodes, once they have taken up subjects that are narrow and easy to cover, are forced by poverty of material to make small things great and to spend many words on matters not even worth remembering. Some too fall into much the same error through sheer lack of judgment.

How much more reasonably could someone have spent the space used to pad out books and spin the narrative out at length on Hiero and Gelo, leaving Hieronymus aside.

For such a subject is more pleasing to those who enjoy hearing history,

# Book 7 Chapter 8: Character of Hiero II

and altogether more useful to those who wish to learn. For Hiero, first of all, won for himself the rule over Syracuse and its allies by his own efforts, inheriting neither wealth nor reputation nor any other ready-made advantage from fortune.

Indeed, without killing, banishing, or harming a single one of the citizens, he became king of the Syracusans by his own merits,

which is the most astonishing thing of all; and still more astonishing is the fact that he not only acquired his power in this way, but preserved it in the same way.

For though he reigned for fifty-four years, he kept peace for his fatherland, preserved his own rule free from plots, and escaped the envy that usually accompanies great power.

In fact, when he more than once tried to lay down his sovereignty, he was prevented by the citizens acting together.

By proving exceptionally beneficent and eager for honor in his dealings with the Greeks, he left behind himself a great reputation and won for Syracuse no small measure of goodwill from all.

And though he lived amid immense abundance, luxury, and wealth, he survived to more than ninety years of age, kept all his senses, and preserved every part of his body unimpaired.

This seems to me no small sign, but a very great one, of a temperate life.

Gelo too, after living more than fifty years, set before himself as the noblest aim in life obedience to the father who begot him, and he counted neither wealth nor royal greatness nor anything else whatever as more important than affection and loyalty toward his parents.
