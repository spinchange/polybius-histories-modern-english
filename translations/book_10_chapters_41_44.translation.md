# Book 10 Chapter 41: Affairs in Greece: Philip V. Called In Against the Aetolians

The Aetolians, recently elated by the arrival of the Romans and King Attalus, were alarming everyone and pressing hard against all by land, while Attalus and Publius did the same by sea.

Accordingly the Achaeans came urging Philip to help, for they were anxious not only about the Aetolians but also about Machanidas, because he was encamped with his force on the borders of Argos.

The Boeotians too, fearing the enemy fleet, asked for a commander and assistance. Those inhabiting Euboea were especially insistent that some precaution be taken against the enemy. The Acarnanians made similar appeals.

There was also an embassy from the Epirotes. At the same time it was reported that Scerdilaidas and Pleuratus were taking the field, and further that the Thracians bordering Macedonia, especially the Maedi, were preparing to invade Macedonia if the king should be drawn even a little way from his own kingdom.

The Aetolians were also preoccupying the narrows around Thermopylae with ditches, palisades, and heavy guards, convinced that they would shut Philip in and altogether prevent him from coming to the aid of the allies inside the pass.

It seems to me quite reasonable to call attention to such situations and fix the reader's notice upon them, since in them a true test and proof is made of the emotional resolve and bodily powers of commanders.

For just as in hunting the strength and courage of animals become most visible when danger closes round them from every side, so too with leaders. And what Philip did on this occasion made this especially clear.

For he dismissed all the embassies, promising each to do what he could, and fixed his attention on the war pressing him from every side, watching where and against whom he would need to make his first move.

# Book 10 Chapter 42: Reinforcements Sent to Various Cities

At this time word reached him that Attalus and his men had crossed over and, after putting in at Peparethus, had occupied the island. So he sent forces there to guard the city.

To Phocis and the regions of Boeotia he dispatched Polyphantas with a suitable force, and to Chalcis and the rest of Euboea Menippus with a thousand peltasts and five hundred Agrianians.

He himself advanced on a march to Scotussa, and likewise ordered the Macedonians to meet him in that city.

When he learned that Attalus and his men had sailed into Nicaea, and that the Aetolian leaders were gathering at Heraclea to confer with one another about the present situation, he took up the army from Scotussa and set out in haste, eager to appear there and throw their meeting into panic and confusion.

He arrived too late for the gathering, but after destroying and carrying off the grain of the inhabitants around the Maliac Gulf, he returned.

He left the main army again in Scotussa, but himself, taking the light-armed troops and the royal squadron, went to Demetrias and stayed there, watching the enemy's designs.

And so that nothing they did might escape him, he sent messages to Peparethus and to those in Phocis, and likewise to those in Euboea, ordering them to report all events to him by beacon-fires directed toward Mount Tisaeum.

This is a mountain in Thessaly, well situated for viewing the regions just mentioned.

# Book 10 Chapter 43: Fire signals

Since the use of beacon-signals is of the greatest service in war, and since the subject had previously remained undeveloped, I think it useful not to pass it by but to make suitable mention of it.

That timely action plays a great part in all undertakings, and the greatest in war, is clear to everyone. Among all the devices that contribute to this, beacon-fires have the greatest power.

For by them one may learn of events that have just happened or are actually taking place, if one takes heed, at a distance of three or four days' journey, and sometimes even more.

So it often happens that help arrives unexpectedly where it is needed because the news has been conveyed by fire-signals.

Formerly, however, since the method of beacon-signalling was in most cases simple, it turned out to be useless to those employing it.

For the system had to operate by fixed conventions, while most events are indefinite and varied, so that most of them escaped the use of beacons—as in the cases just mentioned.

That a fleet had arrived at Oreus or Peparethus or Chalcis could indeed be made clear to those who had agreed on such a signal.

But if certain citizens are changing sides, or betraying the city, or a massacre has occurred,

or any other such thing—events which often happen, but for which it is impossible to make prior provision, though such unexpected developments especially demand prompt deliberation and aid—

all such matters escaped the use of fire-signals. For where no prior foresight is possible, no agreed sign for the event can be devised either.

# Book 10 Chapter 44: Methods of Signalling

Aeneas, then, the man who composed the manuals on tactics, wishing to correct this difficulty, made some progress, but his device still fell very far short of what was required.

One may see this from the following. He says that those who wish to communicate urgent information to one another by beacon-fires should prepare earthenware vessels exactly equal in width and depth, the depth being best at three cubits and the width at one.

Then they should prepare corks a little narrower than the mouths of the vessels, and in the middle of these should fix rods divided into equal sections of three fingers' breadth, with each section clearly marked.

On each section should be written one of the most obvious and general events that occur in war, for example, immediately on the first,

"cavalry have entered the country," on the second "heavy infantry," on the third "light troops," and after these,

"infantry with cavalry," then "ships," after that "grain," and so on continuously, until in all the spaces there are written those events which according to reasonable foresight are most likely to occur in the war then on hand.

When this has been done, he directs that both vessels be pierced accurately so that the outlets are equal and discharge equally; then, after filling them with water, the corks with the rods are to be placed upon them, and the outlets are to be opened at the same moment.

If this happens, then clearly, since everything is equal and alike, the fluid will run off in equal measure, the corks will sink by the same amount, and the rods will disappear into the vessels in the same proportion.

And when by experiment this operation has proved equal in speed and in agreement, the two vessels are to be taken to the places where the parties will watch for the beacon-signals and each side is to set down its vessel.

Then whenever one of the items written on the rod occurs, he says a torch should be raised and held until the observers opposite raise one in answer. Once both torches are visible, the outlets are to be opened immediately.

Then, when the cork and the rod sinking with it bring the desired inscription level with the rim of the vessel, he directs that the torch should be raised.

The others are then to stop their outlet at once and look to see what words on the rod stand level with the rim of their own vessel.

That will be the message conveyed, provided everything on both sides has moved at the same speed.
