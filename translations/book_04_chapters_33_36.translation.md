This observation of mine may perhaps find some confirmation in events of long ago as well.

For the Messenians, among many other testimonies, set up a pillar beside the altar of Lycaean Zeus in the days of Aristomenes—as Callisthenes too records—on which they inscribed these lines:

"Time in the end found out the unjust king;

with Zeus's help Messene found the traitor easily. Hard it is for a perjured man to escape the notice of a god. Hail, King Zeus, and keep Arcadia safe."

For after they had been robbed of their own land,

it was, as I see it, as though for a second fatherland that they prayed the gods to preserve Arcadia, and so dedicated this inscription.

And they did so with good reason. For not only had the Arcadians taken them in at the time of their expulsion from their own country during the Aristomenic war, sharing their hearths with them and making them citizens, but they had also voted to give their daughters in marriage to those of the Messenians who were of suitable age,

and, beyond this, had investigated the treachery of their king Aristocrates in the battle known as the Battle of the Trench, and had put him to death and wiped out his entire line.

Yet quite apart from these ancient events, what happened most recently, after the joint refounding of Megalopolis and Messene, would by itself give ample support to what I have said.

For at the time when the battle near Mantinea had left the victory of the Greeks in dispute because of the death of Epaminondas, the Lacedaemonians tried to bar the Messenians from sharing in the treaty, still coveting Messenia in their hopes;

but the Megalopolitans and all the Arcadians who shared in their alliance pressed the matter so hard that the Messenians were admitted by the allies and took part in the oaths and the settlement, while the Lacedaemonians alone among the Greeks were left out of the treaty.

Considering all this, what man of later times could fail to judge that what I set out a little earlier has been rightly said?

Let this, then, be said by me for the sake of the Arcadians and Messenians, so that, remembering the disasters that have befallen their homelands at the hands of the Lacedaemonians, they may hold genuinely fast to their goodwill and loyalty toward one another,

and may neither, out of dread of some danger nor out of longing for peace, abandon each other in the gravest crises.

# Book 4 Chapter 34: Machatas Addresses the Spartan Public

The Lacedaemonians, for their part, behaved exactly as was their habit—this being of a piece with what came before—for in the end they sent the envoys of the allies away without any answer. Such was the impasse into which their own folly and bad faith had driven them.

And this, it seems to me, proves the truth of the saying that excessive daring often ends in senselessness and comes to nothing.

Afterward, however, once new ephors had been installed, the men who had set the whole affair in motion from the start, and who were responsible for the slaughter mentioned above, sent word to the Aetolians, inviting them to dispatch an envoy.

The Aetolians readily complied, and a little later Machatas arrived at Lacedaemon as their ambassador.

He at once approached the ephors ****, the men holding that Machatas ought to be granted access to the people, that kings should be appointed in accordance with ancestral custom, and that the rule of the Heraclidae should no longer be allowed to remain abolished in defiance of the laws.

The ephors, though displeased with the whole business and unable to face down the agitation, but fearful of the band of young men, said that they would deliberate about the kings later, but granted Machatas the assembly.

When the people had gathered, Machatas came forward and at considerable length urged them to choose the alliance with the Aetolians, recklessly and brazenly denouncing the Macedonians while extolling the Aetolians without reason or truth.

When he had withdrawn, the matter became the subject of great dispute: some spoke in favor of the Aetolians and advised concluding the alliance with them, while others spoke against it.

But when some of the older men brought the people's attention to bear on the benefactions of Antigonus and the Macedonians, and on the harm done through Charixenus and Timaeus—when the Aetolians had marched out in full force and ravaged their land, enslaved the inhabitants of the surrounding country, and plotted against Sparta itself by bringing back the exiles through trickery and force—the people came round to a different view,

and in the end were persuaded to maintain the alliance with Philip and the Macedonians.

This being so, Machatas departed again without having achieved anything

# Book 4 Chapter 35: Restoration of Royalty In Sparta

and returned home; but those who had been the original authors of the upheaval, wholly unable to yield to the present circumstances, once more set about doing a thing more impious than anything else, having first corrupted some of the young men.

For at a certain ancestral sacrifice it was required that the men of military age process under arms to the temple of Athena of the Bronze House, while the ephors carried out the rites of the sacrifice, remaining there about the precinct.

At this very moment some of the young men in the procession, fully armed, suddenly fell upon the ephors as they were sacrificing and cut them down—even though the sanctuary afforded safety to all who took refuge in it, even to a man condemned to death.

But on this occasion, through the savagery of the men who dared the deed, contempt for the sacred reached such a pitch that all the ephors were slaughtered around the altar and the table of the goddess.

Next, acting in accordance with their design, they killed Gyridas and the other elders of his party, exiled those who had spoken against the Aetolians, chose ephors from among their own number, and concluded the alliance with the Aetolians.

They did all this—and submitted to the hatred of the Achaeans, the ingratitude toward the Macedonians, and, in general, their unreasonable conduct toward everyone—not least because of Cleomenes and their goodwill toward him, forever cherishing the hope and expectation of his coming and of the deliverance he would bring with it.

So true is it that men who have the skill to deal tactfully with those around them leave behind, not only while present but even when far away, certain sparks of goodwill toward themselves that are exceedingly strong.

These men, for instance, among other things, though they had been governed under their ancestral constitution for nearly three years now since the expulsion of Cleomenes, had never once even thought of appointing kings of Sparta;

yet the moment the report of Cleomenes' death reached them, both the masses and the board of ephors at once rushed to install kings.

And so the ephors who shared in the faction of the partisans—the same men who had concluded the alliance with the Aetolians of which I spoke just now—appointed the one king lawfully and properly, namely Agesipolis, who was a boy in age, son of Agesipolis and grandson of Cleombrotus;

this Cleombrotus had reigned at the time when Leonidas was driven from the throne, because by descent he was the nearest to that house.

As guardian of the boy they chose Cleomenes, son of Cleombrotus and brother of Agesipolis.

From the other royal house there were two sons of Archidamus, son of Eudamidas, by the daughter of Hippomedon, while Hippomedon himself—son of Agesilaus, son of Eudamidas—was still alive and in his prime, and there were several other members of that house besides, more distantly related than those just named but still kin by descent;

yet all of these they passed over and appointed as king Lycurgus, not one of whose ancestors had ever attained that title. By giving a talent to each of the ephors he became a descendant of Heracles and king of Sparta.

So cheap, everywhere, has nobility become. And so it was that not the children's children, but the very men who set it up, were the first to pay the wages of their folly.

# Book 4 Chapter 36: Machatas Proposes Foreign War to Quell Domestic Strife

Machatas, learning what had taken place among the Lacedaemonians, turned back once more and came to Sparta, and urged the ephors and the kings to carry the war to the Achaeans;

for only in this way, he said, could an end be put to the contentiousness of those Lacedaemonians who were trying by every means to sever the alliance with the Aetolians, and likewise of those in Aetolia who were acting in the same fashion.

The ephors and the kings were persuaded, and Machatas departed, having accomplished his aim through the blindness of those who worked with him.

Lycurgus then took the soldiers and some of the citizens and invaded the Argolid, the Argives being completely off their guard because of the settlement that had previously been in place.

By a sudden assault he seized Polichna, Prasiae, Leucae, and Cyphanta, but in his attacks on Glympes and Zarax he was beaten off.

After he had done this, the Lacedaemonians proclaimed the right of plunder against the Achaeans. Machatas and his party also persuaded the Eleans, using arguments much like those they had used with the Lacedaemonians, to carry the war to the Achaeans.

Since matters had advanced for the Aetolians beyond expectation and to their liking, they entered upon the war with confidence, while the Achaeans were in the opposite case.

For Philip, on whom their hopes rested, was still occupied with his preparations; the Epirotes were only about to go to war;

the Messenians remained inactive; and the Aetolians, having now added the folly of the Eleans and Lacedaemonians to their cause, were hemming them in with war from every side.
