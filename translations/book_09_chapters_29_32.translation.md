# Book 9 Chapter 29: Speech of Chlaeneas

And as for the successors who inherited his power, why need I speak in detail of the way they have treated the Greeks?

For there is no one alive so unconcerned with public affairs that he has not heard how Antipater, after defeating the Greeks in the Lamian War, treated the wretched Athenians most cruelly, and the others likewise.

His violence and lawlessness went so far that he appointed men to hunt down exiles and sent them to the cities after all who had spoken against, or in any way offended, the house of Macedon.

Some of these men were dragged by force from sanctuaries, others torn away from altars and put to death under torture, while those who escaped were driven out of all Greece; for they had no refuge except among the Aetolian people alone.

And who does not know what Cassander and Demetrius did, and with them Antigonus Gonatas? Since these events happened not long ago, the memory of them is perfectly clear.

Some of them planted garrisons in the cities, others installed tyrants, and so left no city untouched by the name and reality of slavery.

But passing over these, I will now come to the last Antigonus, so that none of you, by viewing his conduct too innocently, may suppose yourselves under an obligation of gratitude to the Macedonians.

For Antigonus did not take up war against you because he meant to save the Achaeans, nor because he was offended by the tyranny of Cleomenes and wished to set the Lacedaemonians free.

That way of thinking would be very simple-minded indeed, if any of you hold it.

No: he saw that his own power would not be secure if you won mastery over the Peloponnesians; and he also saw that Cleomenes was naturally well fitted for that task, while Fortune was splendidly aiding you.

So he came in fear and jealousy, not to help the Peloponnesians, but to deprive you of your hopes and to humble your superiority.

Therefore you ought not so much to feel gratitude to the Macedonians because, after taking your city, they did not plunder it, as to regard them as enemies and hate them, because more than once already they have prevented you, when you were capable, from taking the leadership of Greece.

# Book 9 Chapter 30: Impiety of Philip

And as for Philip's lawless conduct, what need is there to say more?

Of his impiety toward the gods, the outrages committed against the temples at Thermus are enough; and of his cruelty toward men, his bad faith and treaty-breaking toward the Messenians.

For the Aetolians alone among the Greeks stood face to face with Antipater for the safety of those unjustly deprived of security; they alone resisted Brennus and the barbarians with him; and they alone, when called upon, fought beside you,

wishing to help restore to you your ancestral leadership of the Greeks.

Let that be enough on these matters. As for the present proposal before you, it is in one sense necessary to write and vote as though you were deliberating about war; yet in truth you must not think of this as a war at all.

For the Achaeans are not even going to imagine that they can harm your territory, and I think they will be deeply grateful to the gods if they can preserve their own when war closes around them—war from the Eleans and Messenians because of their alliance with us, and from us as well.

And I am fully persuaded that Philip will cease from his attack when he is being warred upon by land by the Aetolians and by sea by the Romans and King Attalus.

What is coming can be very easily inferred from what has already happened.

For if he was never able to subdue the Aetolians when he was fighting them alone, how could he reasonably be expected to hold out in the present war?

# Book 9 Chapter 31: Conclusion of the Speech

Let what I have said stand, then, in keeping with my original purpose: namely, to show you all that, even if you are not already committed, but are deliberating from a free starting point, it is more fitting for us to be allied with the Aetolians than with the Macedonians.

But if you are already prepossessed and have made up your minds about these matters, what argument is left?

For if you had made the alliance you now have with us before the benefits Antigonus conferred on you, there might perhaps have been room to question whether you ought, in deference to later favors, to disregard something of your earlier obligations.

But since it was after Antigonus had brought about that much-trumpeted freedom and safety—which these men throw in your teeth at every turn—that you then deliberated, repeatedly considered with yourselves whether you ought to join the Aetolians or the Macedonians, and chose to side with us Aetolians, giving us guarantees on this point, receiving the same from us, and even fighting beside us in the last war against the Macedonians, who can reasonably still be in doubt?

For whatever kindly relations you had with Antigonus and Philip were then set aside.

What remains is either to show that some wrong has since been done to you by the Aetolians, or some favor by the Macedonians; or else, if neither of these things has happened, how can you now, under the persuasion of those to whom you once rightly refused to listen when you were still free, be about to undo treaties, oaths, and the strongest pledges known among men?

With words like these Chlaeneas ended his speech, having spoken, it seemed, in a way that left little room for reply.

# Book 9 Chapter 32: Lyciscus Replies To Chlaeneas

After this Lyciscus, envoy of the Acarnanians, came forward. At first he paused, seeing that most of the audience were talking among themselves about what had just been said.

But when at last they fell silent, he began to speak more or less as follows:

"We, men of Lacedaemon, have come to you sent by the Acarnanian league; and since we have almost always shared the same hopes as the Macedonians, we regard this embassy as common to us and to them.

For just as in danger, because of the superiority and greatness of Macedonian power, our safety is included in their strength, so also in diplomatic contests the interest of the Acarnanians is included in the justice of the Macedonians.

Therefore you should not be surprised if we speak mostly on behalf of Philip and the Macedonians.

Now Chlaeneas, in bringing his speech to a close, gave a sharp summary of the claims that stand between you and his people.

For he said that if, after you had made your alliance with them, some fresh injury and offense had been committed by the Aetolians, or, by Zeus, some act of kindness by the Macedonians, then the present proposal might properly be considered as though from a new starting point.

But if nothing of that sort had happened, and we think that by bringing forward the services of Antigonus and your former decisions we shall now persuade you to undo oaths and treaties, then we are the simplest fools alive.

I for my part admit that, if nothing has happened since, as he says, and affairs in Greece remain just as they were before when you made the alliance with the Aetolians, then I am indeed the greatest fool of all and am about to offer you arguments that are empty.

But if the situation has taken exactly the opposite turn, as I shall clearly show as the speech goes on, then I think it will appear that I am giving you useful advice, and that Chlaeneas is mistaken.

For we have come persuaded that we ought to make our argument precisely on this point: to show why it is both fitting and advantageous for you, if possible, after hearing of the danger now threatening Greece, to resolve upon some noble and worthy course by sharing our hopes.
