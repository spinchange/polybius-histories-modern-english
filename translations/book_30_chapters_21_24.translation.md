# Book 30 Chapter 21: The Athenians and Rhodians

At this time Theaetetus appeared before the senate and spoke about the alliance.

But the senate postponed consideration of the matter, and meanwhile he died a natural death, for he was more than eighty years old.

Then exiles from both Caunus and Stratoniceia came to Rome and were admitted before the senate, and a decree was passed ordering the Rhodians to withdraw their garrisons from both Caunus and Stratoniceia.

Philophron, Astymedes, and their colleagues, after receiving this reply, sailed home in haste,

fearing that if the Rhodians failed to comply with the order to remove the garrisons, they would create fresh grounds for accusation.

# Book 30 Chapter 22: Death of Theaetetus of Rhodes

Lucius Anicius, who had also served as a Roman general, after defeating the Illyrians and bringing Gentius, the king of the Illyrians, captive along with his children, celebrated victory games at Rome and did things that were utterly ridiculous, as Polybius relates in Book 30.

For he sent for the most distinguished performers from Greece and built an enormous stage in the circus, and first he brought on all the flute-players together.

These were Theodore the Boeotian, Theopompus, Hermippus, and Lysimachus, all men of the highest reputation.

He stationed them on the front of the stage with the chorus and ordered them all to play at once.

But when they were going through their rhythms with the proper movements, he sent instructions that they were not playing well and told them they ought rather to compete with one another.

When they were at a loss, one of the lictors showed them that they should turn, advance against each other, and stage something like a battle.

The flute-players quickly understood, and, taking up behavior well suited to their own theatrical excesses, created a tremendous uproar.

Turning the middle choruses to face the outer ones, the flute-players blew with absurd force and drove them against one another in turns, waving their pipes about.

At the same time the choruses beat time, charged across the stage against their opponents, and then wheeled about and retreated again.

And when one of the dancers, tightening his belt and turning at the right moment, raised his fists like a boxer against the oncoming flute-player, then the spectators at last broke into extraordinary applause and shouting.

While these groups were still contending in battle order, two dancers were brought into the orchestra with musical accompaniment, and four boxers mounted the stage along with trumpeters and horn-blowers.

With all these performers going at once, what resulted was beyond description. And as for the tragic actors, Polybius says, whatever I might choose to say would seem to some people like mockery.

# Book 30 Chapter 23: Public Hatred of Callicrates and His Faction

At this time the Cnossians, together with the Gortynians, made war on the Raucians and swore agreements with one another that they would not end the war until they had taken Raucus by force.

Meanwhile the Rhodians, when they received the news about the Caunians and saw that Roman anger was still not subsiding, even though they had obeyed every instruction in accordance with the replies they had been given, immediately appointed Aristotle and his colleagues as ambassadors and sent them to Rome, instructing them to make another attempt concerning the alliance.

When they arrived in Rome, at the height of summer, they entered the senate and at once explained how the people had obeyed the commands laid upon them, and they pleaded for the alliance, speaking at length in many different ways. The senate gave them a reply

in which it passed over friendship in silence, and regarding the alliance said that it was not proper for it to grant that request to the Rhodians at present.

# Book 30 Chapter 24: Delight at Peraea

For the inhabitants of the Peraea were like slaves unexpectedly released from their chains,

who, unable to believe the good fortune before them, go beyond normal behavior, because they think that those they meet do not recognize or clearly see that they have truly been set free, unless they do something strange and out of the ordinary.
