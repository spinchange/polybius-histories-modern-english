# Book 9 Chapter 11a: Chapter 11a

The Romans sent envoys to Ptolemy because they wanted grain supplies, since there was a severe shortage among them.

All the countryside in Italy had been devastated by the armies as far as the very gates of Rome, and no help had come from outside, because wars were in progress and armies were encamped in every part of the inhabited world except the regions of Egypt.

So extreme had the scarcity at Rome become that a Sicilian medimnus was selling for fifteen drachmas.

And yet, though their situation was like this, they did not neglect the war.

# Book 9 Chapter 26a: Chapter 26a

Most people judge the size of the places just mentioned from the mere length of their perimeter.

So if someone says that the city of the Megalopolitans has a circuit of fifty stades, while that of the Lacedaemonians is forty-eight, and yet that Sparta is twice as large in area as Megalopolis, the statement seems unbelievable to them.

And if, wanting to increase their perplexity, someone adds that a city or a camp whose circuit is forty stades can be twice as large as one whose perimeter is a hundred stades, what is said appears altogether astonishing to them.

The reason for this is that we do not remember the lessons taught us in childhood through geometry.

I have been led to speak about these matters because not only ordinary people, but also some of those engaged in public life and in positions of command, are bewildered, sometimes wondering whether the city of the Lacedaemonians can really be larger—indeed much larger—than that of the Megalopolitans, though it has the smaller circuit,

and sometimes trying to estimate the number of men in camps by guessing from the perimeter alone.

A similar mistake also happens in judging the appearance of cities. Most people suppose that cities full of bends and hills contain more houses than level cities.

But this is not so, because the walls of buildings are constructed at right angles, not to the slopes of the ground, but to the level surfaces beneath them; and it turns out that the hills themselves stand upon those same surfaces.

Still, one can grasp the point in a simple way from what is visible.

For if one imagines the houses on slopes rising upward so that all of them are of equal height, it is clear that the single plane formed at the level of the roofs will be equal and parallel to the plane lying beneath the hills and beneath the foundations of the walls.
