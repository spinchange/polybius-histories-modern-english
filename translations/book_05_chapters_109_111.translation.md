# Book 5 Chapter 109: Philip Prepares to Invade Italy

During the winter, Philip reflected that his plans would require ships and a force to serve at sea—though not for fighting a naval battle.

He could never have hoped to be capable of that, of fighting the Romans at sea; what he needed instead was a fleet that could ferry soldiers, cross quickly to whatever point he intended, and appear before the enemy unexpectedly.

Concluding, therefore, that the Illyrian style of shipbuilding was best suited to these purposes, he set about constructing a hundred light galleys—being almost the first of the Macedonian kings to do so.

Once these were ready, he assembled his forces at the beginning of summer, and after giving the Macedonians a little practice at the oars he put out to sea.

It was at this same time that Antiochus crossed the Taurus, while Philip, sailing through the Euripus and around Cape Malea, came to the region of Cephallenia and Leucas. There he came to anchor and waited anxiously, busily seeking news of the Roman fleet.

Learning that it lay at anchor off Lilybaeum, he took heart and put to sea, advancing on his course toward Apollonia.

# Book 5 Chapter 110: Philip Withdraws to Cephallenia

As he was already drawing near the area of the river Aous, which flows past the city of the Apolloniates, a panic broke out among his crews much like those that occur in armies on land.

Some of the galleys sailing at the rear of the fleet, which had put in at the island called Sason—lying at the entrance to the Ionian channel—came to Philip by night reporting that certain ships sailing from the Strait had anchored there alongside them,

and that these men reported leaving behind at Rhegium some Roman quinqueremes bound for Apollonia and for Scerdilaidas.

Philip, supposing that the fleet was all but upon him, was seized with fear; he hastily weighed anchor and gave the order to sail back the way he had come.

Making his withdrawal and return voyage in complete disorder, he reached Cephallenia on the second day, sailing continuously day and night.

Recovering a little of his confidence, he stayed there, creating the impression that he had turned back for some operations in the Peloponnese.

As it happened, the whole alarm about him proved to be false.

For Scerdilaidas, hearing that during the winter Philip was building a considerable number of galleys, and expecting him to come by sea, had sent messages to the Romans explaining this and asking for help;

and the Romans had dispatched a squadron of ten ships from their fleet off Lilybaeum—this being the very squadron that had been sighted near Rhegium.

Had Philip not fled from it in such groundless terror, that would have been the very moment when he could best have accomplished his aims in Illyria, since the Romans were occupied with all their plans and preparations against Hannibal and the battle at Cannae; and in all likelihood he would also have captured the ships.

As it was, thrown into confusion by the report, he made his way back to Macedonia—without harm, but without honor.

# Book 5 Chapter 111: The Gauls In Asia

At about this same time something happened involving Prusias that deserves to be recorded.

The Gauls whom King Attalus had brought over from Europe for the war against Achaeus, because of their reputation for bravery, had parted from that king on account of the suspicions just mentioned, and were ravaging the cities along the Hellespont with great wanton violence, until at last they even set about besieging the people of Ilium.

In these circumstances a noble action was performed by the inhabitants of Alexandria in the Troad as well.

For they sent out Themistes with four thousand men, raised the siege of Ilium, and drove the Gauls out of the whole Troad by cutting off their supplies and frustrating their designs.

The Gauls then seized the place called Arisba in the territory of Abydos, and from there went on plotting against and making war on the cities founded in that region.

Against them Prusias marched out with an army, and in a pitched battle he destroyed the men in hand-to-hand fighting during the engagement itself, slaughtered nearly all their children and women in their camp, and let his soldiers who had done the fighting plunder the baggage.

By this action he freed the cities of the Hellespont from great fear and danger, and he left behind a fine example to later generations, warning the barbarians of Europe not to take crossing into Asia lightly.

Such, then, was the state of affairs in Greece and Asia. In Italy, after the battle of Cannae had been fought, most of the country went over to the Carthaginians, as I have shown in the earlier parts of this work.

At this point in time I shall break off my narrative, since I have now covered the events in Asia and Greece that fell within the 140th Olympiad.

In the book that follows, after briefly recalling the introductory matters laid out here, I shall return, in accordance with the promise made at the outset, to the account of the Roman constitution.
