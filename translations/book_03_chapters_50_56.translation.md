# Book 3 Chapter 50: Hannibal Harassed By the Natives

Hannibal, after marching for ten days along the river a distance of about eight hundred stades, began the climb up into the Alps, and it happened that he fell into the gravest dangers.

For as long as they were in the lowlands, the various chieftains of the Allobroges all kept their distance from them, partly in fear of the cavalry and partly of the barbarians escorting them. But once those escorts had departed back to their own country,

and Hannibal's men began to advance into the difficult terrain, the chieftains of the Allobroges then gathered a considerable force and seized in advance the commanding positions through which Hannibal's men were necessarily obliged to make their ascent.

Now if they had kept their plan hidden, they would have utterly destroyed the Carthaginian army. As it was, having been detected, they did great harm to Hannibal's force, but no less to themselves.

For when the Carthaginian general learned that the barbarians had occupied the commanding positions in advance, he pitched camp near the passes and waited,

and he sent forward some of the Gauls who were guiding them in order to scout out the enemy's intentions and their whole design.

When these men had carried out their orders, the general learned that during the day the enemy diligently kept their ranks and guarded the positions, but at night withdrew to a nearby town. Adapting his plan to this situation, he devised the following maneuver.

Taking up his force, he advanced openly, and on drawing near the difficult ground he pitched camp not far from the enemy. When night fell, he gave orders to keep the campfires burning, and left the larger part of his force there; then, making the fittest men travel light, he passed through the narrows during the night and took possession of the positions the enemy had previously seized, since the barbarians had withdrawn, as was their custom, into the town.

# Book 3 Chapter 51: The Gauls Attack the Baggage Train

When this had happened and day came on, the barbarians, seeing what had occurred, at first abandoned their attempt.

But afterward, observing the great number of pack animals and the cavalry working their way painfully and slowly through the difficult ground, they were tempted by the opportunity to fasten upon the column.

When this happened, and the barbarians fell upon them at several points, the Carthaginians suffered heavy destruction—not so much from the men as from the terrain—and especially among the horses and pack animals.

For since the approach was not only narrow and rough but also bordered by precipices, at every disturbance and every commotion many of the pack animals were carried over the cliffs together with their loads.

It was above all the wounded horses that caused this kind of confusion: for some of them, panicked by their wounds, collided head-on with the pack animals, while others, in their forward rush, shoved aside everything in their path along the narrow ground, and so produced great disorder.

Seeing this, and reckoning that there would be no safety even for those who escaped the danger if the baggage train were destroyed, Hannibal took the men who had seized the heights during the night and rushed to the aid of those who had gone ahead on the march.

When he did this, many of the enemy were killed, because Hannibal made his charge from the higher ground, but no fewer of his own men perished as well;

for the tumult along the column was increased on both sides by the shouting and grappling of those just mentioned.

But once he had killed most of the Allobroges and routed the rest, forcing them to flee to their own country, then at last the surviving mass of pack animals and horses made its way, with great hardship and difficulty, through the difficult ground.

He himself then gathered as many men as he could from the engagement and assaulted the town from which the enemy had launched their attack.

Finding it nearly deserted, since everyone had been drawn out after the plunder, he took possession of the town. From this he gained many things useful to him, both for the present and for the future.

For the moment, he recovered a quantity of horses and pack animals, along with the men who had been captured with them; and for the future he obtained a supply of grain and livestock sufficient for two or three days.

But the most important thing was that he instilled fear in the tribes ahead, so that none of those living near the passes would readily dare to attack him.

# Book 3 Chapter 52: Treachery of the Gauls

For the time being, then, he made his camp there, and after remaining one day he set out again.

For the following days he led the army along safely up to a point; but on the fourth day he again found himself in great danger.

For the people living along the route, having conspired together with treacherous intent, came to meet him carrying branches and garlands—for among nearly all the barbarians this is a token of friendship, just as the herald's staff is among the Greeks.

Wary of trusting such an assurance, Hannibal closely questioned their intention and their whole design.

They claimed to be well aware both of the capture of the town and of the destruction of those who had tried to do him wrong, and explained that they had come for this very reason—wishing neither to inflict nor to suffer any harm—and they promised to hand over hostages from among themselves.

For a long while Hannibal remained cautious and distrustful of what they said. But reckoning that, if he accepted what was offered, he would perhaps make these new arrivals more wary and gentler, whereas if he refused he would have them as openly declared enemies, he assented to their words and pretended to enter into friendship with them.

When the barbarians had handed over the hostages, supplied livestock in abundance, and altogether put themselves into his hands without reservation, Hannibal's men trusted them to a certain degree, so far as even to use them as guides through the difficult ground ahead. But after they had gone in front for two days,

the aforementioned tribes assembled and, having kept close behind, fell upon them at a certain gorge that was hard to pass and full of precipices.

# Book 3 Chapter 53: The White Rock

—as they were making their way across it. At that moment Hannibal's men would all have perished utterly, had he not still been somewhat on his guard and, foreseeing what was coming, kept the baggage and the cavalry at the front of the column and the heavy infantry at the rear.

Since these troops were holding in reserve, the disaster turned out to be less severe, for they withstood the onslaught of the barbarians.

Even so, when this took place, a great number of men, pack animals, and horses were destroyed.

For since the positions were higher and favored the enemy, the barbarians moved along the slopes parallel to the column, rolling rocks down on some and striking others at close range with stones, and so reduced them to utter confusion and danger—

so much so that Hannibal was forced to spend the night with half his force near a certain fortified white rock, apart from the horses and pack animals, keeping guard over these until, in the course of the whole night, they slowly worked their way out of the ravine.

The next day, when the enemy had withdrawn, he rejoined his cavalry and pack animals and pressed on toward the highest passes of the Alps. He no longer encountered any large body of the barbarians, but was harassed by them piecemeal and from place to place;

some of them, attacking at opportune moments from the rear and others from the front, dragged off some of the baggage. His greatest help in this was the elephants;

for wherever along the march these animals happened to be, the enemy did not dare to approach that part of the column, terrified by the strange sight of the beasts.

On the ninth day he completed the climb to the passes, and there he pitched camp and remained two days, wishing both to rest those who had come through safely and to wait for the stragglers.

During this time it happened that many of the horses that had bolted in panic, and many of the pack animals that had thrown off their loads, unexpectedly came running up, following the tracks—

# Book 3 Chapter 54: The Descent Into Italy

—and rejoined the camp. By now the snow was already gathering on the peaks, since the setting of the Pleiades was approaching; and seeing his men in low spirits, both because of the hardship they had already endured and because of what was still expected,

he tried to gather and encourage them, having one resource for this: the clear sight of Italy. For Italy lies so close beneath the aforementioned mountains that, when both are viewed together, the Alps appear to stand as a citadel over the whole of Italy.

So, by pointing out to them the plains around the Padus and reminding them in general of the goodwill of the Gauls who dwelt there, and at the same time indicating the location of Rome itself, he made the men somewhat bolder.

The next day he broke camp and began the descent. In it he no longer met with any enemies except those who did harm in secret, but because of the terrain and the snow he lost almost as many men as had perished on the ascent.

For since the descent was narrow and steep, and the snow made it impossible for each man to see where he was stepping, anyone who strayed from the path or slipped was carried down over the precipices.

This hardship, however, they bore, being by now accustomed to such troubles.

But when they came to a place that neither the elephants nor the pack animals could get past because of its narrowness—a sheer drop of about three half-stades, which had existed before but had now been made even worse by a fresh landslide—then once again despondency and dismay fell upon the men.

At first the Carthaginian general attempted to go around the difficult ground; but when snow came on and made this route too impassable,

# Book 3 Chapter 55: Snow

he gave up the attempt. For what was happening was peculiar and out of the ordinary. The fresh snow of that year had just fallen on top of the snow already there, which had remained from the previous winter; this new snow was easily broken through, both because, being fresh, it was soft and because it had as yet no depth.

But whenever they had trodden through this and stepped onto the lower, compacted snow, they no longer broke through it; instead they slid along, skidding with both feet at once, just as happens to people walking over ground covered with a layer of mud.

What followed from this was still more difficult.

For the men, unable to break through the lower snow, whenever they fell and tried to brace themselves with their knees or hands to get up, only slid all the more, together with everything they leaned on, since the terrain sloped steeply downward.

The pack animals, on the other hand, when they fell, would break through the lower snow as they struggled to rise; but having broken through, they stuck there with their loads, as if frozen fast, both from the weight and from the firmness of the underlying snow.

So, giving up this hope, he encamped on the ridge after clearing away the snow on it, and then, setting the troops to work, he built up the cliff face with great toil.

For the pack animals and horses he made a passage adequate in a single day. So he immediately led these across, pitched camp at a spot below the snow line, and sent them off to pasture;

then he brought up the Numidians in relays for the construction work, and with much suffering he got the elephants across in three days. These were in a wretched state from hunger;

for the highest parts of the Alps and the regions reaching up to the passes are entirely treeless and bare, because the snow remains there continuously, both summer and winter, whereas the parts halfway down the slopes on both sides bear timber and trees and are, on the whole, fit for habitation.

# Book 3 Chapter 56: Hannibal Reaches the Plains

Hannibal, having gathered his whole force together, continued the descent, and on the third day after passing the aforementioned precipices he reached the level country.

He had lost many of his soldiers throughout the whole march, both at the hands of the enemy and in crossing the rivers, and many more on the precipices and difficult passes of the Alps—not only men, but still greater numbers of horses and pack animals.

At last, having completed the entire march from New Carthage in five months and the crossing of the Alps in fifteen days, he descended boldly into the plains around the Padus and the territory of the Insubres,

with the surviving portion of his force: twelve thousand Libyan infantry, about eight thousand Iberians, and cavalry numbering no more than six thousand in all, as he himself records on the inscribed column concerning the size of his army at Lacinium.

At about the same time, as I stated above, Publius—having left his forces to his brother Gnaeus, with instructions to take charge of affairs in Iberia and to wage vigorous war against Hasdrubal—sailed with a small force to Pisae.

Then, marching through Etruria and taking over the legions of the praetors that were stationed there and waging war against the Boii, he came to the plains around the Padus and, pitching camp, confronted the enemy, eager to join battle.
