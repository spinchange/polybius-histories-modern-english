# Book 28 Chapter 1: Antiochus and Ptolemy Appeal to Rome

Olympiad 152, year 3. Affairs in Italy.

Since the war over Coele-Syria had already begun between Kings Antiochus and Ptolemy, envoys came to Rome: from Antiochus, Meleager, Sosiphanes, and Heracleides; from Ptolemy, Timotheus and Damon.

At that time Antiochus held the advantage in the affairs of Coele-Syria and Phoenicia.

For ever since Antiochus, the father of the king now reigning, defeated Ptolemy's generals in the battle near Panium, all the regions just mentioned had been subject to the kings in Syria.

So Antiochus, thinking that possession won in war was the strongest and noblest title, pursued the matter as though he were defending property that was his own.

Ptolemy, on the other hand, believed that the former Antiochus had unjustly taken advantage of his father's orphaned condition and stripped them of the cities of Coele-Syria; so he could not bring himself to yield those places to him. That was why Meleager and his party came,

with instructions to call the Senate to witness that Ptolemy, against every rule of justice, had been the first to lay hands on territory that was not his.

Timotheus and his colleagues came both to renew friendly relations and to speak about ending the war with Perseus, but above all to observe the interviews of Meleager's party.

They did not dare speak about ending the war, however, because Marcus Aemilius advised them against it. So, after renewing the friendship and receiving answers in line with what they had requested, they returned to Alexandria.

To Meleager and his colleagues the Senate replied that it would entrust Quintus Marcius to write to Ptolemy on these matters in whatever way he judged advantageous and consistent with his own good faith. This, then, was how the matter was handled for the present.

# Book 28 Chapter 2: Embassy from Rhodes

About this same time, and now with summer already drawing to a close, envoys also arrived from Rhodes: Agesilochus, Nicagoras, and Nicander.

They came to renew the friendship, to obtain permission to ship out grain, and at the same time to answer the slanders being spoken against their city.

For faction at Rhodes seemed to be quite open: Agathagetus, Philophron, Rhodophon, and Theaetetus rested all their hopes on the Romans, while Deinon and Polyaratus looked to Perseus and the Macedonians.

As a result, when objections were repeatedly raised in the course of their public business and deliberations were pulled in opposite directions, those who wanted to invent stories against the city found plenty of opportunities.

Nevertheless, on that occasion the Senate took no notice of any of this, though it knew clearly what had happened among them; and it granted them permission to export one hundred thousand medimni of grain from Sicily.

That was the business the Senate handled privately with the Rhodian envoys.

After that it gave a similar answer to all the other envoys who had arrived from Greece and were pursuing the same policy.

Such was the state of affairs in Italy.

# Book 28 Chapter 3: Pressure Put On Achaia and Aetolia

II. The war with Perseus.

At this time Aulus, serving as proconsul and wintering in Thessaly with the army, sent envoys to the regions of Greece, Gaius Popilius and Gnaeus Octavius.

They first came to Thebes, where they praised the Thebans and urged them to preserve their goodwill toward the Romans.

Then, as they went on through the cities of the Peloponnese, they tried to show the people the Senate's mildness and humanity by producing the decrees that had been issued a little earlier.

At the same time, through their remarks, they let it be understood that they knew the men in each city who were hanging back beyond what was proper, and likewise those who were pressing forward.

And it was plain to everyone that they were displeased with the laggards no less than with those who openly opposed them.

By this they drove many people into uncertainty and bewilderment about what words or actions would best meet the demands of the present moment.

Gaius and his colleague, when the Achaean assembly had been convened, were said to have intended to accuse Lycortas, Archon, and Polybius,

and to show that these men were out of sympathy with Roman policy and, though quiet for the moment, were not naturally so disposed, but were watching events and waiting for their opportunity.

They did not dare to do this, however, because they had no reasonable pretext against the men just named.

So when the council was assembled for them at Aegium, they addressed the Achaeans in a speech at once cordial and exhortatory, and then sailed away to Aetolia.

# Book 28 Chapter 4: Discussion of the Aetolian Congress

And again, when an assembly had been convened for them at Thermon, they came before the people and delivered speeches full of exhortation and professions of goodwill.

But the real object for which they had the assembly in hand, they said, was that the Aetolians must give them hostages.

After they had stepped down, Proandrus rose and wanted to set forth certain services he had rendered that were useful to the Romans, and to accuse those who were slandering him. Thereupon Gaius rose again,

and although he knew perfectly well that Proandrus was no friend of Rome, he nevertheless praised him and confirmed everything he had said.

After him Lyciscus came forward and, though he brought no accusation against anyone by name, cast suspicion on many.

For he said that the Romans had acted rightly in dealing with the ringleaders by taking them off to Rome—meaning Eupolemus and Nicander—

but that their associates and supporters were still left in Aetolia, and that all of them ought to meet with the same correction, unless they surrendered their children to the Romans as hostages.

His hints were aimed especially at Archedamus and Pantaleon.

When he yielded the floor, Pantaleon rose and briefly denounced Lyciscus, saying that he shamelessly and basely flattered those who held the upper hand.

Then he turned against Thoas, thinking that he was the man who gave the accusations against them their greatest credibility, since no quarrel between the two of them was thought to exist.

Reminding him partly of what had happened in the time of Antiochus, and partly reproaching him for ingratitude because, after he had been surrendered to the Romans, he had unexpectedly been saved through the embassy of Pantaleon and Nicander,

he quickly worked the crowd up so far that they were ready not only to shout Thoas down whenever he tried to speak, but even to stone him with one accord.

After this had happened, Gaius briefly reproached the Aetolians for stoning Thoas, and at once departed with his fellow-envoy for Acarnania, saying no more about the hostages. Meanwhile affairs in Aetolia were full of mutual suspicion and complete disorder.
