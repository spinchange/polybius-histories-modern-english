# Book 8 Chapter 29: Gaius Livius Has a Party

Once the entrance had been safely made as planned and without any disturbance at all, the Carthaginians, thinking that the greater part of the enterprise was already accomplished, now advanced boldly toward the marketplace by the broad street that rises from the district called the Deep Road.

The cavalry, however, numbering not fewer than two thousand, they left outside the walls, wishing to keep this force in reserve against any appearance from outside or against the unexpected turns that commonly arise in such undertakings.

When they came near the marketplace, they halted the force in marching order, while they themselves waited for Philemenus, anxious to see how this part too of their enterprise would succeed.

For when they had lit the fire and were about to move toward the gate, they had also sent off Philemenus, carrying the boar on a litter and accompanied by about a thousand Libyans, toward the neighboring gate, not wishing, as from the beginning, to hang the whole enterprise upon a single hope, but upon several.

Philemenus, when he came to the wall and gave his accustomed whistle, was immediately met by the guard coming down to the wicket.

When he called from outside to open quickly because they were carrying a heavy load, namely a wild boar, the guard gladly and hurriedly opened, hoping that some share of this successful hunt would also come to him, since he had always received a portion of what Philemenus brought in.

Philemenus himself, carrying the front end of the litter, entered first; with him came another man dressed as a Numidian, as though he were one of the country people, and after him came two more carrying the beast from behind.

When all four were inside the wicket, they struck down and killed the man who had opened it, as he harmlessly looked over and felt the boar, and then quietly let in through the little gate the Libyans following close behind them, about thirty in number.

Once this had been done, some were engaged in cutting the bars, others in killing the men on the tower, and others in summoning the Libyans outside by means of agreed signals.

When these too had entered safely, they advanced toward the marketplace according to the arrangement.

And when this force as well joined him, Hannibal, delighted that the business was proceeding exactly as he wished, pressed on with the plan he had set before himself.

# Book 8 Chapter 30: Hannibal Enters Tarentum

He selected about two thousand Celts, divided them into three bodies, and assigned to each body two of the young men who had managed the plot.

He also sent some of his own officers with them, ordering them to occupy the most favorable points along the roads leading into the marketplace.

When they had done this, he ordered the local young men to single out and spare any Tarentine citizens they encountered, crying from a distance that Tarentines should stay where they were, since they were safe.

But to the Carthaginian and Celtic officers he gave orders to kill any Romans they met. With that, they split up and carried out what had been commanded.

As the enemy's entry now became plain to the Tarentines, the city filled with shouting and confusion of every kind.

Gaius, when the enemy's entrance suddenly came upon him, realized that in his drunken state he was incapable of action. He at once rushed out of the house with his servants, came to the gate leading to the harbor, and after the guard had opened the wicket for him, slipped through and, seizing one of the little boats at anchor, was conveyed with his servants to the citadel.

At this same moment Philemenus and his people, having prepared Roman trumpets and some men able to sound them from familiarity with their use, stood in the theater and gave the signal.

The Romans, responding in arms by force of habit to the trumpet-call and hurrying toward the citadel, made the affair turn out exactly as the Carthaginians intended.

For as they came through the streets scattered and without formation, some ran into the Carthaginians and others into the Celts, and by being cut down in this manner in separate groups, a very great number perished.

At daybreak the Tarentines stayed quietly in their houses, still unable to make sense of what was happening.

For because of the trumpet and because no outrage or plundering was taking place in the city, they thought the disturbance had arisen from the Romans themselves.

But the sight of many Romans lying dead in the streets, together with some Gauls visibly stripping the bodies, brought to mind the presence of the Carthaginians.

# Book 8 Chapter 31: Philemenus and his Retinue Get In Too

By now Hannibal had drawn up his forces in the marketplace, and the Romans had withdrawn into the citadel, since they had long since secured it with a garrison. As the daylight became clear, Hannibal proclaimed that all Tarentines were to assemble in the marketplace without arms,

while the young conspirators went around the city crying out for freedom and urging the people to take courage, since the Carthaginians had come on their behalf.

Those Tarentines who had remained attached to the Romans understood the situation and withdrew into the citadel; but the rest obeyed the proclamation and gathered without arms, and to these Hannibal spoke graciously.

Since the Tarentines, in the joy of their unexpected hope, signaled approval of all he said with one voice, Hannibal dismissed the crowd for the moment, ordering every man to return at once to his own house and write on the door, “A Tarentine's.”

Whoever wrote the same words on a Roman dwelling, he declared, would suffer death.

He then selected the most suitable men for action and sent them to plunder the Roman houses, giving as their watchword that every unmarked house should be treated as hostile. The rest he held in reserve in formation behind them.

# Book 8 Chapter 32: Massacre of Romans In Tarentum

After a great and varied mass of goods had been collected from the plundering, and the Carthaginians had gained a booty worthy of their expectations,

they bivouacked under arms for the night. On the following day Hannibal consulted with the Tarentines and decided to wall off the city from the citadel, so that the Tarentines might no longer live in fear of the Romans occupying the acropolis.

First, then, he undertook to set up a palisade parallel to the wall of the citadel and to the trench that already lay before it.

But knowing clearly that the enemy would not allow this quietly and would show their strength at that point, he prepared his best troops, thinking that for the future nothing was more necessary than to strike terror into the Romans and make the Tarentines confident.

Accordingly, when the first palisade was being set up and the Romans made a bold and daring attack on the enemy, Hannibal engaged only briefly and drew out the onset of the Romans, and when most of them had come beyond the trench, he gave the signal to his own men and attacked.

A fierce struggle followed, as was natural in a narrow and enclosed space.

In the end the Romans were forced back and put to flight. Many fell by enemy hands in the fighting itself, but the greater number, pushed and hurled together into the trench, perished there.
