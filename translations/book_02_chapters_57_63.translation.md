# Book 2 Chapter 57: The Treatment of Mantinea

The people of Mantinea, then, had at the outset abandoned their membership in the Achaean confederacy and of their own free will handed themselves and their homeland over to the Aetolians, and after that to Cleomenes.

Having committed themselves to this course and become participants in the Lacedaemonian polity, in the fourth year before the arrival of Antigonus they were taken by storm by the Achaeans, when Aratus carried off a stroke against their city.

On that occasion they were so far from suffering any harsh treatment for the offense just described that what happened actually became famous, because of the suddenness with which both sides reversed their attitude toward one another.

For the moment he secured the city, Aratus at once ordered the men under his command that no one was to touch anything belonging to another.

Next, gathering the Mantineans together, he urged them to take heart and to remain in possession of their own property; for their safety would be guaranteed if they continued as citizens within the Achaean confederacy.

To the Mantineans this prospect appeared so unsuspected and unexpected that they all instantly swung over to the opposite frame of mind.

The very men against whom they had been fighting a little while before—men through whom they had watched many of their kinsmen perish and not a few of their own people fall to grievous wounds—these they now brought into their own houses and made sharers of their hearths along with their families, leaving out nothing of mutual goodwill.

And they had good reason to act so. For I do not know whether any men ever encountered more reasonable enemies, or whether any ever came through what seemed the greatest of calamities with less harm than the Mantineans did, thanks to the humanity of Aratus and the Achaeans toward them.

# Book 2 Chapter 58: The Mantineans Turn Over their City to the Lacedaemonians

But afterward, foreseeing the factional strife within their own community and the schemes of the Aetolians and Lacedaemonians, they sent envoys to the Achaeans and requested that a protective garrison be provided for them.

The Achaeans agreed and selected by lot three hundred men from their own number. Those on whom the lot fell set out, leaving behind their own cities and livelihoods, and took up residence in Mantinea, guarding the freedom and at the same time the security of its people.

Along with these they also sent out two hundred mercenaries, who together with the Achaeans helped maintain the established order there.

Not long afterward, however, the Mantineans fell into civil strife, drew in the Lacedaemonians, handed over the city to them, and slaughtered the men stationed among them by the Achaeans—a graver and more dreadful breach of faith than one could easily describe.

For even granted that they had decided to renounce altogether their gratitude and friendship toward the confederacy, they surely ought at least to have spared the men just mentioned and allowed every one of them to depart under a truce.

This much, after all, it is customary to grant even to enemies, by the common laws of mankind.

But they, in order to give Cleomenes and the Lacedaemonians a sufficient pledge of their commitment to the present design, transgressed the common rights of mankind and deliberately carried out the most monstrous act of impiety.

For to become the murderers and avengers of men who had earlier taken them by storm yet released them unharmed, and who at that very moment were guarding their freedom and security—how great an indignation does this deserve?

What would these men have to suffer before they would be thought to have paid a fitting penalty? Perhaps one might say: to be sold into slavery along with their wives and children, once they had been overcome in war.

But this is a fate that, by the laws of war, awaits even those who have committed no act of impiety at all. So they deserved to meet with some more thorough and heavier punishment;

and therefore, even if they had suffered what Phylarchus says, it would not have been natural for pity toward them to follow from the Greeks. Rather, praise and approval would have been due to those who carried out the punishment of their impiety.

Yet, although nothing worse befell the Mantineans in their downfall than the plundering of their goods and the selling of the free into slavery, this writer, for the sake of sensationalism, not only introduced an outright falsehood,

but a falsehood that is implausible as well; and through the excess of his ignorance he could not even take account of what lay right beside it—namely, how these same men, who at the very same period took Tegea by storm, did nothing of the kind there.

And yet, if the cruelty of the perpetrators had been the cause, it would have been natural for the people of Tegea too to suffer the same fate as those who had fallen under their power at the same time.

But if the difference in treatment concerned the Mantineans alone, it is obvious that the cause of the anger directed at them must also have been something distinctive.

# Book 2 Chapter 59: Execution of Aristomachus

Again, Phylarchus says that Aristomachus of Argos—a man from a most distinguished family, who had himself been tyrant of the Argives and was descended from tyrants—after falling into the hands of Antigonus and the Achaeans, was led off to Cenchreae and tortured to death, having suffered the most unjust and dreadful treatment of any man alive.

In this episode too the writer keeps to his characteristic manner and invents certain cries supposedly heard through the night as the man was being racked, reaching the people living nearby; of whom some, he says, were appalled at the impiety, others incredulous, others outraged at what was happening, and all came running to the house.

As for this sort of sensationalism, let it pass; enough has been said about it.

For my own part, even if Aristomachus had committed no other crime against the Achaeans, I judge that, by reason of the way he chose to live and his lawless treatment of his own country, he deserved the heaviest punishment.

And although the writer, wishing to magnify the man's reputation and to dispose his audience to share more deeply in his indignation at what Aristomachus suffered, says that he was not only a tyrant himself but also descended from tyrants—

no one could easily bring a graver or more biting accusation than this. For the very word carries with it the most impious connotation and embraces every injustice and lawlessness found among men.

And if Aristomachus did endure the most terrible tortures, as this man claims, even so he did not pay an adequate penalty for a single day—

the day on which, after Aratus had broken into the city with the Achaeans and undergone great struggles and dangers for the freedom of the Argives, and had in the end been driven out because none of those inside who had arranged to support him stirred, out of fear of the tyrant—

Aristomachus, using this as a pretext and excuse, on the ground that certain men had been complicit in the Achaeans' entry, tortured and slaughtered the eighty foremost citizens, who had done no wrong, in front of their own relatives.

those of his ancestors, for that would be a long story. And so one should not regard it as a terrible thing if he met with some such fate himself; far more terrible would it have been if he had died scot-free without experiencing any of this.

Nor should any lawlessness be charged against Antigonus or Aratus because they took a tyrant captive in war, tortured him, and put him to death—a man for killing and punishing whom, even in time of peace, praise and honor would have followed from all who reason rightly.

And when, on top of the crimes already mentioned, he also betrayed the Achaeans, what did he deserve to suffer?

He had laid down his tyranny only a short time before, hemmed in by circumstances on account of the death of Demetrius, and had unexpectedly obtained his safety, protected by the mildness and generosity of the Achaeans;

for they not only made him exempt from punishment for the crimes committed during his tyranny, but even admitted him into their commonwealth and clothed him with the highest honor, appointing him their leader and general.

Yet he forgot the favors just described in an instant: as soon as his hopes for the future were a little raised by Cleomenes, he tore both his native city and his own allegiance away from the Achaeans at the most critical moment and attached them to the enemy.

Such a man, once he had been taken captive, ought not to have died being tortured at night in Cenchreae, as Phylarchus says, but rather to have been led around the Peloponnese and made an example of under punishment, and so to have departed from life.

And yet, for all that he was such a man, he met with no harsh treatment except being drowned in the sea by those stationed at Cenchreae.

# Book 2 Chapter 61: The Loyalty of the Megalopolitans

Besides this, he recounted the misfortunes of the Mantineans with exaggeration and dramatic effect, evidently supposing that it is the duty of historians to call attention to lawless deeds;

yet of the nobility of the Megalopolitans, which they displayed at the very same period, he made not even the slightest mention—

as though it were more proper to history to enumerate the offenses of the wicked than to mark out the fine and just things that men do, or as though readers of such records were less improved by serious and admirable deeds than by lawless and despicable ones.

How Cleomenes took the city, and how, having kept it intact, he at once sent letter-carriers to the Megalopolitans in Messene, inviting them to recover their homeland undamaged and to share in his own cause—this he did relate to us, wishing to display the magnanimity and moderation of Cleomenes toward his enemies.

And further, how the Megalopolitans, while the letter was being read aloud, would not let it be read to the end and nearly stoned the letter-carriers—this much he made clear. But the natural sequel, the very thing proper to history, he left out:

the praise and the honorable record of their admirable resolve.

And yet it lay ready at hand. For if we count as good men those who endure war on behalf of friends and allies merely by their words and votes, while to those who go so far as to take upon themselves the devastation of their land and a siege we award not only praise but also gratitude and the greatest rewards,

what estimate must we form of the Megalopolitans? Surely the most honorable and the highest?

First they surrendered their land to Cleomenes; then again they suffered utter ruin for their homeland because of their loyalty to the Achaeans;

and finally, when an unhoped-for and unexpected opportunity was given them to recover it undamaged, they chose instead to lose their land, the tombs of their ancestors, their temples, their homeland, their possessions—everything, in a word, that men hold most necessary—rather than betray their faith to their allies.

What nobler deed has ever been done, or could be done? To what could a historian better draw the attention of his audience? By what deed could he more strongly urge men toward keeping faith and toward genuine and lasting partnership? Yet of none of this did Phylarchus make any mention,

being blind, as it seems to me, to the finest things and those most fitting for a historian to treat.

# Book 2 Chapter 62: The Wealth of Megalopolis

Furthermore, in what follows he says that from the spoils of Megalopolis six thousand talents fell to the Lacedaemonians, of which two thousand were given to Cleomenes according to custom.

Here, in the first place, who would not be amazed at his inexperience and his ignorance of the common understanding of the resources and power of Greek affairs—a knowledge that historians above all ought to possess?

For I am not even speaking of those earlier times in which the resources of the Peloponnesians had been utterly ruined, both by the kings in Macedonia and, still more, by the unbroken succession of their wars against one another;

but in our own day, in which everyone, speaking with one and the same voice, is thought to be enjoying the greatest prosperity, nevertheless from the whole Peloponnese, from its movable property alone—leaving slaves out of account—it would be impossible to gather so great a sum of money.

That I make this claim now not at random but on solid reasoning is clear from the following.

For who has not read concerning the Athenians that at the time when, together with the Thebans, they entered upon the war against the Lacedaemonians and sent out ten thousand soldiers and manned a hundred triremes,

they then decided to base their war contributions on a property assessment and had the entire territory of Attica valued, along with the houses, and likewise the rest of their property—yet the whole valuation of their wealth fell short of six thousand talents by two hundred and fifty?

From this, what I have just said about the Peloponnesians will appear not unreasonable.

And at that period, from Megalopolis itself, not even the most extravagant estimate would dare to claim that more than three hundred talents were obtained,

seeing that it is agreed that most of the bodies, both free and slave, managed to escape to Messene. And the strongest proof of what has been said is this:

the Mantineans, who were second to none of the Arcadians in either power or wealth, as this very writer says, and who were captured through siege and surrender, so that no one could escape and nothing could easily be smuggled away,

nevertheless yielded as their entire spoil, captives included, only three hundred talents at this same period.

# Book 2 Chapter 63: Cleomenes Invades Argolis

But who would not marvel still more at what comes next? For in his account he states that about ten days before the battle the ambassador from Ptolemy came to Cleomenes announcing that Ptolemy refused to continue supplying funds and urged him to make terms with Antigonus.

And he says that, on hearing this, Cleomenes decided he must stake everything on a throw as quickly as possible, before his forces learned what had happened, since he had no hope in his own resources of being able to pay his troops.

But if he had at that very time become master of six thousand talents, he could have outdone Ptolemy himself in supplies;

and as for war against Antigonus, if he had been master of only three hundred talents, he would have been more than able to hold out safely and wear the war down.

To declare at once that all of Cleomenes' hopes rested on Ptolemy because of the supplies, and at the same time to assert that he had become master of so vast a sum at the very same period—how is this not a sign of the greatest absurdity and, moreover, of want of reflection?

Many other things of this kind have been set down by the writer, both for the present period and throughout his whole work; but concerning these I think that what has now been said is sufficient for my original purpose.
