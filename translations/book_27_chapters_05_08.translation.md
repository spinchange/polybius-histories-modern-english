# Book 27 Chapter 5: Perseus Sends Alexander to Boeotia

Perseus learned that some of the cities in Boeotia were still holding fast to their goodwill toward him, and he sent Antigonus, the son of Alexander, as envoy.

When he arrived in Boeotia, he passed over the other cities because he found no opening for closer dealings there,

but he entered Coronea, Thisbae, and also Haliartus, and urged the people to hold fast to their goodwill toward the Macedonians.

They gladly accepted what he said and voted to send envoys to Macedonia, so he sailed away and, on meeting the king, gave him a full report on the situation in Boeotia.

A little later the envoys also arrived and asked him to send help to the cities that had chosen the Macedonian side,

for the Thebans, they said, were pressing them hard and harassing them because they would not agree with them or choose the side of Rome.

When Perseus heard this, he said that because of the truce he could not send military help to anyone at all,

but he urged them generally to defend themselves against the Thebans as best they could, while not making war on the Romans, but remaining quiet.

# Book 27 Chapter 6: The Boeotians and Rhodians

When the Romans had heard the envoys who had come from Asia speak about Rhodes and the other cities, they summoned the envoys sent by Perseus.

Solon and Hippias and their party tried to say something about the whole situation and to appease the senate, but for the most part they defended themselves against the charge of the plot against Eumenes.

When they had finished their defense, the senate, having long since resolved on war, ordered them to leave Rome at once, and all other Macedonians who happened to be staying in Italy to depart within thirty days.

After this they called in the consuls and urged them to seize the moment and not delay.

# Book 27 Chapter 7: War With Perseus Begun

While Gaius was still lying at anchor off Cephallenia, he sent the Rhodians a letter about dispatching ships, entrusting the letter to a certain athletic trainer named Socrates.

When the letter reached Rhodes, Stratocles was serving as prytanis in the second half-year of his term.

When the matter was brought forward for debate, Agathagetes, Rhodophon, Astymedes, and many others thought the ships should be sent and that they should join the war at once from the beginning, without making any pretext.

But Deinon, Polyaratus, and their party, though already displeased by the friendly attitude that had been shown toward the Romans, now put forward King Eumenes as a pretext and began to corrupt the inclination of the masses.

For the Rhodians had long felt suspicion and hostility toward Eumenes, first dating from the war against Pharnaces, when Eumenes was blockading the mouth of the Hellespont to keep ships from sailing into the Black Sea, and the Rhodians intervened and stopped him,

and then, in more recent times, the quarrel had been stirred up again by the Lycian disputes over certain forts and a tract of land that lay on the border of the Rhodian Peraea and was continually being ravaged by troops under Eumenes.

Because of all this they were ready enough to listen to anything said against the king.

So Deinon's party seized on this opportunity and tried to discredit the letter, saying that it had come not from the Romans but from Eumenes, who wanted by every possible means to drag them into the war and saddle the people with needless expense and suffering.

As proof of what they said, they pointed to the fact that the man who had brought the letter was just such a person as this, whereas the Romans were not accustomed to act in that way, but sent communications of this kind with great seriousness and official protection.

They said this even though they knew perfectly well that the letter had in fact been written by Lucretius, because they wanted to teach the masses not to do anything readily for the Romans, but in every matter to be difficult and to provide occasions for suspicion and resentment.

For their purpose was to estrange the people from their goodwill toward Rome and, so far as they could, entangle them in friendship with Perseus.

These men were devoted to that course because Polyaratus, being more boastful and vain, had burdened his estate with debt, while Deinon, being greedy and reckless, had from the beginning been drawn to the profits that came from powerful lords and kings.

At this point Stratocles the prytanis rose and, after speaking at length against Perseus and much in favor of the Romans, urged the majority to ratify the decree for dispatching the ships.

They immediately made ready six quadriremes, sent five of them to Chalcis with Timagoras appointed to command them, and the remaining one to Tenedos under a commander named Timagoras.

When he reached Tenedos, he came upon Diophanes, who had been sent by Perseus to Antiochus; he did not get possession of Diophanes himself, but he did capture his crew.

Lucretius received all the allies who had come by sea with kindness and then released them from this service, saying that the situation did not require naval assistance.

# Book 27 Chapter 8: Perseus Summons a Council

After the Macedonian victory, when a council had been gathered around Perseus, some of his friends pointed out that the king ought to send an embassy to the Roman commander,

and even now declare himself willing to pay the Romans the tribute that his father, when defeated, had previously promised, and to give up the same places.

For if the Romans accepted the settlement, they said, the war would end honorably for the king after his success in the field, and in general the Romans would in future be more cautious, now that they had had experience of Macedonian valor, not to impose anything unjust or oppressive on the Macedonians.

But if they refused to accept out of angry pride over what had happened, then heaven would justly be offended with them, while he, because of his moderation, would have both gods and men fighting on his side.

This view was approved by most of his friends, and Perseus agreed, so envoys were sent at once: Pantauchus son of Balacrus and Midon of Beroea.

When they arrived before Licinius, the commander immediately called a council. After the envoys had explained the message they had been instructed to deliver, Pantauchus and his colleague were asked to withdraw, and the Romans deliberated on the situation before them.

They unanimously decided to give the harshest answer possible.

For among the Romans this is in every way an established ancestral custom: in times of setback they show themselves most headstrong and severe, but in times of success most moderate.

Everyone would agree that this is a noble trait; but whether it is always practicable in certain circumstances, one might reasonably question.

At any rate, on that occasion they gave this answer: Perseus must place himself and all that concerned him in their hands, and in general give the senate authority to decide, as it thought best, about Macedonian affairs.

When Pantauchus and his colleague heard this, they returned and reported it to Perseus and his friends.

Some of them were shocked by such arrogance, grew angry, and advised Perseus to send no more embassies or messages about anything whatever.

Perseus, however, was not that sort of man. Instead he kept adding to the amount of money offered and sent to Licinius again and again.

But he made no progress, and most of his friends reproached him, saying that though he was the victor he was acting like the defeated party, as if everything had gone wrong for him.

So in the end, abandoning hope in further embassies, he was forced to move his camp back again to Sicyrium.
