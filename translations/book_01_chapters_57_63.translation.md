# Book 1 Chapter 57: The Two Sides Evenly Matched

Take the case of two outstanding boxers, men matched in both their natural gifts and their physical conditioning. When they have entered the decisive moment of the contest for the crown and are fighting it out, landing blow upon blow without pause, it is impossible—for the combatants and the spectators alike—to keep a precise account of, or to anticipate, each individual feint and stroke.

Yet from the overall vigor of the men, and from the competitive drive of each, one can form an adequate impression of their skill, their strength, and their courage as well. The same holds for the two generals I am now describing.

The reasons for, and the methods by which, they daily set ambushes and counter-ambushes, attacks and assaults against one another could never be set down in full by a writer who tried to enumerate them; and for an audience the recital would be endless and, into the bargain, the reading of it pointless and unprofitable.

But from a general statement about them and about the outcome of their rivalry, one will arrive at a clearer understanding of the events described.

For nothing was left untried—neither the stratagems that could be learned from history, nor the devices prompted by the moment and the immediate situation, nor the undertakings that called for hazardous and forceful daring.

A decisive engagement, however, could not be brought about, and for several reasons. The forces on both sides were evenly matched; the entrenched camps were alike unapproachable because of their strength; and the distance between the two armies was extremely short.

This last was the chief reason why minor clashes occurred ceaselessly day after day, while nothing decisive was ever accomplished.

For it always turned out that the only men killed in these encounters were those who fell into hand-to-hand fighting; while those who once gave way were at once entirely out of danger, safe behind their own defenses, and could then turn about and renew the fight.

# Book 1 Chapter 58: Hamilcar Besieges the Romans at Eryx

Nevertheless, Fortune, like a good umpire of the games, shifted them by a daring move out of the position just described and the struggle they had been engaged in, and shut them up in a more dangerous contest within a narrower space.

For while the Romans were holding Eryx both at its summit and at its base, as I have said, Hamilcar seized the town of the Erycinians, which lay between the summit and the men encamped at the foot.

The result was that the Romans holding the summit endured a siege and faced danger with reckless courage, while the Carthaginians held out with almost incredible tenacity—with the enemy pressing them on every side, and their supplies brought in only with difficulty, since they clung to the sea at a single point by a single approach.

Here once again both sides used against each other every device and force of siege-craft; they bore every kind of privation and made trial of every assault and battle.

In the end—not, as Fabius claims, because they were exhausted and worn down, but like men untouched and unconquerable—they let the crown stand undecided.

For before either side could overcome the other, though they fought on in this place for two more years, the war came to its decision by another means.

Such, then, was the situation at Eryx and among the land forces. The two states were like high-bred fighting birds battling to their last breath.

Such birds, when through exhaustion they have often lost the use of their wings yet hold on by sheer spirit, keep dealing out blows until at last, by chance closing with each other, they seize one another at a vital moment, and once this happens it falls out that one of the two drops first.

So too the Romans and Carthaginians, worn out by their labors through the unbroken succession of dangers, were utterly past feeling, their strength sapped and their resolve undone—

# Book 1 Chapter 59: The Romans Once More Build a Fleet

—by the long years of taxes and expenses. The Romans were fighting on with the same desperate spirit. For nearly five years now they had entirely withdrawn from operations at sea, both because of their reverses there and because they had become convinced they would decide the war by their land forces alone.

But seeing now that the matter was not going forward as they had reckoned—chiefly because of the daring of the Carthaginian commander—they resolved for a third time to stake their hopes on naval power,

believing that by this plan, if they seized the right moment for the attempt, they could in this way alone bring the war to a successful close. And so in the end they did.

The first time, they had quit the sea, yielding to the blows of fortune; the second time, they had been worsted in the sea-battle off Drepana.

Now they made this third attempt, and by it they were victorious; cutting off the Carthaginian armies around Eryx from their supplies by sea, they brought the whole struggle to its conclusion.

The undertaking was, for the most part, an act of sheer determination. The public treasury held no funds for the purpose; nevertheless, through the patriotism and generosity of the leading men toward the commonwealth, the means were found to carry it through.

According to the resources at their command, men came forward singly, in pairs, or in threes to furnish a fully fitted quinquereme, on the understanding that they would recover the cost if affairs went well.

In this way two hundred quinqueremes were quickly made ready, built on the model of the Rhodian's ship. The Romans then appointed Gaius Lutatius as commander and sent him out at the start of summer.

He appeared unexpectedly off the coasts of Sicily and seized both the harbor at Drepana and the anchorages near Lilybaeum, since the entire Carthaginian fleet had withdrawn home.

He threw up works around the city of Drepana and made his other preparations for the siege; and while he pressed on with this, doing everything in his power,

he also kept watch for the arrival of the Carthaginian fleet, and, mindful of his original purpose—that only through a sea-battle could the whole issue be decided—he let no time be wasted or left idle.

Instead, each day he held practice drills and exercises for the crews suited to his plan, and by attending carefully to the rest of their daily regimen, he turned his sailors, in a very short time, into trained athletes ready for the task before them.

# Book 1 Chapter 60: Battle of the Aegusian Islands

The Carthaginians, when news reached them—contrary to all expectation—that the Romans had put to sea with a fleet and were once again contesting the mastery of the sea, at once fitted out their ships.

Loading them with grain and other provisions, they dispatched the fleet, determined that their forces around Eryx should lack none of the necessities.

They appointed Hanno commander of the naval force. He put out to sea and made landfall at the island called Holy Isle, intent on slipping across to Eryx unobserved by the enemy. There he meant to unload his cargo and lighten his ships, then take on board as marines the suitable men from among the mercenaries, along with Barcas, and only then to engage the enemy.

But Lutatius grasped the arrival of Hanno's force and worked out their intention. Taking the best men from his land army, he sailed to the island of Aegusa, which lies off Lilybaeum.

There he addressed his forces with words fitting the occasion and informed the helmsmen that a sea-battle would take place the next day.

Toward dawn, as day was just breaking, he saw that the wind was bearing down fresh and full astern of the enemy, while for himself the run out against the wind would be difficult, the sea being hollow and rough. At first he was at a loss as to what to do in the circumstances.

But he reasoned that if he risked battle while the storm held, he would be fighting only against Hanno, against the bare naval crews, and against ships still heavily laden;

whereas if he waited for fair weather and held back, letting the enemy cross over and join their land forces, he would be fighting against ships that were nimble and lightened, against the best men of the land armies, and—most formidable of all—against the daring of Hamilcar, than which nothing at that time was more to be feared.

He therefore decided not to let the present opportunity slip; and seeing the enemy's ships running under sail, he put out in haste.

His crews, with their fine physical condition, rode the swell with ease, and he quickly drew up his fleet in a single line, prow facing—

# Book 1 Chapter 61: The Battle of Aegusa

—the enemy. The Carthaginians, seeing that the Romans were blocking their crossing, lowered their masts and, after each ship's company had exhorted one another, closed with their opponents.

Since the state of preparation on each side was the exact opposite of what it had been in the sea-battle off Drepana, the outcome of the fight for each side naturally turned out the opposite as well.

For the Romans had remodeled their shipbuilding and had stripped their vessels of all encumbrances except what was needed for the battle; their crews, well drilled and working together, served them with outstanding effect; and their marines were picked men from the legions, men who would never give ground.

With the Carthaginians the reverse was true. Their ships were laden and so unwieldy for the engagement; their crews were utterly untrained and had been thrown aboard for the emergency; and their marines were newly levied recruits, raw to all hardship and danger.

For because they had never expected the Romans to dispute the sea again, they had grown contemptuous and let their naval forces fall into neglect.

And so, the moment battle was joined, they were worsted at many points of the fighting and quickly gave way. Fifty of their ships were sunk, and seventy captured with their crews.

The rest of the host hoisted their sails and, running before the wind, made off again to Holy Isle, the wind luckily and unexpectedly shifting to serve their need at just the right moment.

The Roman commander, for his part, sailed back to Lilybaeum and busied himself with the army over the disposal of the captured ships and prisoners—a considerable task, for those taken alive in the battle fell little short of ten thousand.

# Book 1 Chapter 62: End of the First Punic War

When the defeat fell upon them unexpectedly, the Carthaginians, as far as their fighting spirit and ambition went, were still ready to carry on the war, but in their reckoning they were at a complete loss.

For they could no longer supply their forces in Sicily, since the enemy controlled the sea; yet to abandon those forces—and so, in a sense, to betray them—would leave them without the men or the leaders to keep fighting.

They therefore quickly sent word to Barcas and entrusted him with the decision over the whole matter. And he indeed acted thoroughly as a good and prudent commander should.

For as long as there was any reasonable hope in the existing circumstances, he left untried nothing that seemed hazardous or daring, but, if any commander ever did, he tested to the full every hope of victory in the war.

But once the situation had turned and no reasonable means was left to save the men under his command, he yielded to circumstances with great good sense and practical judgment, and sent envoys to negotiate a truce and a settlement.

For it is the mark of one and the same commander to be able to see both the moment for victory and, equally, the moment for giving way.

Lutatius readily accepted the overtures, since he was well aware that his own side was worn down and already exhausted by the war; and so it came about that the conflict was brought to an end, on terms drawn up roughly as follows:

"On these conditions there shall be friendship between the Carthaginians and the Romans, provided the Roman people also approve. The Carthaginians shall withdraw from the whole of Sicily, and shall not make war on Hiero, nor bear arms against the Syracusans or against the allies of the Syracusans.

The Carthaginians shall give back to the Romans all prisoners without ransom. The Carthaginians shall pay to the Romans, over twenty years, two thousand two hundred Euboic talents of silver."

# Book 1 Chapter 63: The Longest and Harshest War in History

When these terms were referred back to Rome, the people did not accept the treaty, but sent out ten commissioners to investigate the matter.

On their arrival they made no change in the substance of the terms, but they tightened them somewhat against the Carthaginians.

They cut the time allowed for paying the tribute in half, added a thousand talents to the sum, and further required the Carthaginians to withdraw from all the islands lying between Italy and Sicily.

Such, then, were the terms, and such the conclusion, of the war that arose between the Romans and Carthaginians over Sicily—a war fought continuously for twenty-four years, the longest, the most unbroken, and the greatest war we know of by report.

In it, apart from the other battles and preparations I have described above, the two sides fought against one another at sea once with more than five hundred quinqueremes between them, and a second time with not far short of seven hundred.

In this war the Romans lost, counting those destroyed in shipwrecks, close to seven hundred quinqueremes, and the Carthaginians about five hundred.

So that those who marvel at the sea-battles and fleets of Antigonus, Ptolemy, and Demetrius, had they looked into these events, might well have been astounded at the overwhelming scale of these operations.

And if one cared to weigh the difference in size between quinqueremes and the triremes with which the Persians fought the Greeks, and again the Athenians and Spartans fought one another, one would find that nowhere in all history have such great forces contended at sea.
