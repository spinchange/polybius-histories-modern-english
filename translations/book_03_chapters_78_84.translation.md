# Book 3 Chapter 78: Hannibal Marches Southward

During his time in winter quarters he also employed a certain stratagem that was thoroughly Phoenician in character.

Because he was anxious about the unreliability of the Celts and about possible plots against his life—the alliance between himself and them being so recently formed—he had a set of false hairpieces made, each one suited to the appearance natural to a different age of life.

These he wore constantly, changing them from one to another, and in the same way he kept changing his clothing as well, always to match whichever wig he was wearing.

By this means he was hard to recognize, not only for those who caught sight of him suddenly, but even for those who were on familiar terms with him.

He observed, however, that the Celts were resentful at the war dragging on in their own territory, and that they were eager and on edge to push into enemy land—ostensibly out of anger toward the Romans, but mostly for the sake of plunder. He therefore decided to break camp as quickly as possible and to satisfy the army's impulses.

And so, as soon as the season began to change, he made inquiries of those reputed to have the best knowledge of the country. He found that the other routes for invading enemy territory were long and obvious to the enemy, whereas the one leading through the marshes into Etruria, though difficult, was short and would catch Flaminius and his men completely off guard.

Since he was always by temperament drawn to this kind of approach, he resolved to make his march by this route.

But when word spread through the camp that the general intended to lead them through some marshes, every man grew wary of the march, fearful of pits and

# Book 3 Chapter 79: Hannibal Goes Through the Marsh

the swampy stretches of the terrain. But Hannibal had carefully reconnoitered the ground along the route, learning which parts were shallow and firm. He broke camp and placed the Libyans and Iberians—all the more serviceable part of his force—in the vanguard, mixing the baggage train in among them so that they would have ample supplies for the immediate present.

As for the longer term, he gave no thought at all to the whole baggage train, reasoning that once he reached enemy country, if he were defeated he would have no further need of supplies, and if he held the open field he would have no shortage of provisions.

Behind the troops just mentioned he set the Celts, and behind all of them the cavalry.

He left his brother Mago in charge of the rearguard, both for general reasons and especially because of the softness and aversion to hardship of the Celts, so that if they should suffer and try to turn back, Mago could stop them with the cavalry and lay hands on them.

Now the Iberians and Libyans, marching through the marshes while these were still untrampled, got through with only moderate hardship, since they were all hardy by nature and accustomed to such ordeals.

But the Celts advanced only with difficulty, since the marshes were now churned up and trodden into deep mire, and they endured the suffering painfully and miserably, being wholly unused to that kind of distress.

Yet they were prevented from slipping back to the rear by the cavalry stationed behind them.

All of them suffered, especially from sleeplessness, as they marched continuously through water for four days and three nights on end; but the Celts in particular labored and perished more than the rest.

Most of his pack animals slipped in the mud, fell, and died, providing a single service to the men in their fall:

for the soldiers sat on the carcasses and, piling the baggage in heaps, kept themselves above the water, and in this way snatched a little sleep during a short part of the night.

Not a few of the horses, too, lost their hoofs through the relentless march across the mud.

Hannibal himself barely came through, riding with great hardship on the one elephant that survived, in severe pain from a violent attack of ophthalmia that had struck him—through which he ultimately lost the sight of one eye, since the circumstances allowed him no time to halt or apply treatment, the situation making it impossible.

# Book 3 Chapter 80: Flaminius in Hannibal's Way

Having crossed the marshy terrain in this unexpected fashion, and finding Flaminius in Etruria encamped before the city of Arretium, Hannibal for the moment pitched camp there beside the marshes,

wishing to rest his army and to make a thorough study of the enemy and of the terrain that lay ahead.

He learned that the country in front of him was rich in plunder, and that Flaminius was a consummate rabble-rouser and demagogue but had no natural aptitude for the genuine conduct of military affairs, and on top of this was overconfident in his own resources.

He therefore calculated that if he marched past their camp and descended into the territory beyond, Flaminius—partly anxious about the jeers of the crowd, unable to bear watching the land being ravaged, and partly stung by personal feeling—would readily come along, following him into any region, eager to win the success on his own and not to wait for the arrival of his colleague, who held equal authority.

From all this he reckoned that Flaminius would hand him many openings for an attack.

# Book 3 Chapter 81: A General Must Know his Enemy's Character

He reasoned all this out with intelligence and practical sense. For it would not be wrong to say that anyone who supposes there is any more decisive element of generalship than knowing the purpose and character of the opposing commander is ignorant and deluded.

Just as in contests between individuals or between ranks the man who means to win must observe carefully how he can reach his goal and what part of his opponent appears exposed or insufficiently armored,

so too those in command of whole armies must look not for where some part of the body is unguarded, but for where some vulnerable point in the mind of the enemy commander shows itself.

For many men, through laziness and sheer idleness, utterly throw away not only the affairs of the state but their own private lives as well;

many, through their craving for wine, cannot even fall asleep without a stupefying bout of drunkenness;

and some, through their lust for sexual pleasure and their obsession with it, have not only brought ruin on cities and lives but have even forfeited their own existence in disgrace.

And indeed, cowardice and slackness bring reproach on the individuals who have them privately, but when they reside in a supreme commander they become a public matter and the greatest of calamities.

For such a commander not only renders his subordinates ineffective, but often brings the gravest dangers upon those who have placed their trust in him.

Recklessness, too, and audacity and irrational temper, and likewise vainglory and conceit, are easy prey for the enemy but most perilous for one's friends. For a man of that sort is an easy mark for every plot, ambush, and deception.

So if a man can discern the failings of those around him, and approach his adversaries precisely at the point and by the means through which the enemy's leader is most vulnerable, he would very quickly master the whole campaign. For just as, if you remove the helmsman from a ship,

the entire vessel with all its crew falls into the hands of the enemy, in the same way, if a man outmaneuvers the commander of an army through his designs and calculations, he often gains possession of the opposing force, men and all.

It was precisely by foreseeing and reasoning out these things about the enemy commander that Hannibal, on this occasion, did not fail in his design.

# Book 3 Chapter 82: Flaminius Enticed Out

For as soon as Hannibal broke camp from the region around Faesulae and, passing a little beyond the Roman encampment, made his raid into the country that lay ahead,

Flaminius at once became agitated and full of fury, imagining that he was being treated with contempt by the enemy.

Then, as the country was being plundered and the smoke rising everywhere signaled its devastation, he grew indignant, regarding what was happening as outrageous.

And so, when some advised that he should not rashly pursue or close with the enemy, but should be on his guard and beware of their large body of cavalry, and above all should wait for the other consul and engage in battle only when all the armies were combined together,

he not only paid no attention to what they said but could not even tolerate those who offered such advice.

He urged them rather to consider what the people back home were likely to say if the country were being ravaged almost up to the very walls of Rome while they sat encamped in Etruria behind the enemy.

Finally, with these words, he broke camp and advanced with his army, giving no thought to time or place, intent only on engaging the enemy, as though victory were already plainly theirs.

For he had instilled in the crowds such confident expectation that those who tagged along outside the ranks for the sake of booty—carrying chains and fetters and all such equipment—were more numerous than those bearing arms.

Hannibal, for his part, was at the same time advancing forward through Etruria toward Rome, keeping on his left the city called Cortona and its hills, and on his right the lake called Thrasymene.

As he advanced he set fire to the country and laid it waste, wishing to provoke the fury of his opponents. And when he saw that Flaminius was already drawing near,

he set about preparing for battle. Along the route there was a level defile, which on its two long sides had high, continuous hills, while at its short ends it was closed: on the side facing him by a steep, hard-to-climb height set across the way, and at the rear by the lake, which left only an extremely narrow passage into the valley along the foot of the hills.

Passing through the valley along the lake, Hannibal seized for himself the hill that faced the line of march, and encamped on it with the Iberians and Libyans.

The Balearic slingers and spearmen he led around by the vanguard and posted in concealment beneath the hills lying to the right of the valley, deploying them in a long extended line.

The cavalry and the Celts he likewise led around in a circuit under the hills on the left and stretched them out in a continuous line, so that the outermost of them were right at the entrance—the one running between the lake and the foot of the hills into the place described.

So Hannibal, having made these preparations during the night and having ringed the valley with his ambushes, kept quiet.

Flaminius followed close behind, eager to close with the enemy.

He had pitched camp the day before right beside the lake at a very late hour, and when day broke afterward he immediately, at dawn, led his vanguard along the lake into the valley below, intending to engage the enemy.

# Book 3 Chapter 84: The Disaster At the Thrasymene Lake

The day being exceptionally foggy, Hannibal—once the greater part of the marching column had entered the valley and the enemy's vanguard was already drawing close to him—gave the signals and sent word to the men in the ambushes, and launched his attack on the enemy from every side at once.

Flaminius and his men, taken aback by this sudden appearance, and with visibility poor because of the conditions in the air, and with the enemy bearing down and falling upon them from higher ground at many points, found that the Roman company-commanders and tribunes were not only unable to bring help wherever it was needed, but could not even grasp what was happening.

For they were being struck simultaneously—some in front, some from the rear, some on the flanks.

As a result, most of them were cut down still in their order of march, unable to defend themselves, as if they had been handed over to slaughter by the poor judgment of their commander.

For while they were still debating what to do, they were being destroyed before they realized it.

At that very moment some of the Celts fell upon Flaminius himself—who was at a loss and in utter distress over the whole situation—and killed him.

So of the Romans in the valley nearly fifteen thousand fell, unable either to give way to the circumstances or to do anything at all, but holding to this above all else, in keeping with their training: not to flee or abandon their ranks.

Those, however, who were caught on the march in the narrows between the lake and the foot of the hills were destroyed shamefully, and still more wretchedly.

For as they were forced back into the lake, some, in their frenzy of mind, plunged in to swim with their armor on and were drowned, while the great mass advanced into the lake as far as they could and stayed there with only their heads above the water.

When the cavalry rode in and destruction was plainly before them, they raised their hands and begged to be taken alive, uttering every kind of plea; but in the end they were killed, some by the enemy, and some at their own urging by their comrades.

About six thousand of those in the valley, having beaten the enemy directly in front of them, were unable to come to the aid of their own side or to get around behind their opponents, because they could see nothing of what was happening—though they could have rendered great service to the overall outcome.

Always pressing forward, they kept advancing, convinced they would fall in with some of the enemy, until without realizing it they emerged onto the higher ground. When they reached the heights,

and the fog had by now lifted, they understood the disaster that had occurred. No longer able to do anything—since the enemy now had the upper hand in the whole battle and already held all the ground—they closed ranks and withdrew to a certain Etrurian village.

After the battle, Maharbal was sent by the general with the Iberians and the spearmen and encamped around the village; and with manifold difficulties closing in on them, they laid down their arms and surrendered under a truce, on the understanding that their lives would be spared.

Such, then, was the course of the entire engagement that took place in Etruria between the Romans and the Carthaginians.
