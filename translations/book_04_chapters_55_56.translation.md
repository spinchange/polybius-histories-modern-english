# Book 4 Chapter 55: Polyrrhen and Lappa Join the Alliance

The people of Polyrrhenia and Lappa, together with all their allies, observed that the Cnossians were holding fast to their alliance with the Aetolians, and they saw that the Aetolians were at war with both King Philip and the Achaeans. They therefore sent envoys to the king and to the Achaeans to ask for military aid and for admission into alliance.

The Achaeans and Philip received them into the common alliance and dispatched a relief force: four hundred Illyrians under the command of Plator, two hundred Achaeans, and a hundred Phocians.

These troops arrived and, after no long time, sailed home again, having greatly strengthened the position of the Polyrrhenians and their allies.

For in a very short span of time they penned the Eleuthernaeans and the Cydonians, and the people of Aptera as well, within their walls, and forced them to abandon the alliance with the Cnossians and share in the same hopes as the Polyrrhenians. Once this had been accomplished,

the Polyrrhenians and the allies who stood with them sent five hundred Cretans to Philip and the Achaeans, while the Cnossians, a little earlier, had sent a thousand to the Aetolians. Both contingents fought alongside their respective sides in the war then under way.

Meanwhile the Gortynian exiles also seized the harbor of Phaestus, and likewise held the harbor of Gortyn itself by a daring stroke; and using these places as their bases of operations, they made war on the party within the city.

# Book 4 Chapter 56: Raid of Aetolians In the Peloponnese

Such, then, was the state of affairs in Crete. Around this same time Mithridates also opened war against the people of Sinope, and this became, as it were, the beginning and the pretext of the misfortune that was ultimately brought upon the Sinopeans.

When, with a view to this war, they had sent an embassy to the Rhodians and called upon them for aid, the Rhodians resolved to appoint three men and to give them a hundred and forty thousand drachmas, with which those appointed were to procure the supplies needed by the Sinopeans.

The men so commissioned made ready ten thousand jars of wine, three hundred talents of dressed hair, a hundred talents of prepared sinew, a thousand suits of armor, three thousand stamped gold pieces, and besides these four catapults together with the men to operate them.

The Sinopean envoys took these and returned home; for the Sinopeans were in dread that Mithridates might undertake to besiege them both by land and by sea, and so they were directing all their preparations to that end.

Sinope lies on the right-hand side of the Pontus as one sails toward Phasis, and it is settled on a peninsula that juts out into the sea. The neck of this peninsula, joining it to Asia, is no more than two stades across, and the city, set upon it, effectively closes it off from sea to sea.

The remainder of the peninsula projects out into the open sea; it is level ground and entirely easy to approach toward the city, but all around, on the seaward side, it is sheer, hard to land upon, and offers altogether very few points of access.

For this reason the Sinopeans were anxious that Mithridates might construct siege works along the side facing Asia, and likewise, on the side opposite to it, make a landing from the sea onto the level ground and the heights overlooking the city, and so attempt to besiege them.

They therefore set about fortifying the seaward circuit of the peninsula, staking off and entrenching the approaches from the sea, while at the same time placing missile weapons and soldiers at the points of advantage.

For the whole extent of it is not large, and is altogether easy to control and of modest size.
