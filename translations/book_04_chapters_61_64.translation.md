# Book 4 Chapter 61: Philip Arrives in Epirus

Such, then, was the state of affairs in the Peloponnese. King Philip, having passed through Thessaly, arrived in Epirus.

Taking up the Epirotes alongside the Macedonians in full force, together with the three hundred slingers from Achaia who had joined him, and in addition the five hundred Cretans sent by the Polyrrhenians, he advanced; and having passed through Epirus, he arrived in the territory of the Ambracians.

Now, if he had at once pressed straight on and thrust into the interior of Aetolia, falling upon it suddenly and unexpectedly with so heavy a force, he would have put an end to the whole business.

But as it was, persuaded by the Epirotes to reduce Ambracus first by siege, he gave the Aetolians a breathing space in which to make a stand, to take some forethought, and to prepare for what lay ahead.

For the Epirotes, placing their own interest above the common interest of the allies and being intensely eager to bring Ambracus under their own control, begged Philip to lay siege to the stronghold and to capture this first,

since they counted it of the greatest importance to recover Ambracia from the Aetolians, and hoped that this could be accomplished only if, having gained mastery of the aforementioned place, they should then settle down to besiege the city.

For Ambracus is a stronghold well fortified with both outworks and a wall, and it lies amid marshes, having a single narrow, raised causeway as its approach from the land; and it is conveniently situated so as to command both the territory of the Ambracians and the city.

Philip, then, won over by the Epirotes and having encamped around Ambracus, set about

the preparations for the siege. Scopas, meanwhile, at this same time took up the Aetolians in full force, and making his march through Thessaly, invaded Macedonia; he advanced and destroyed the grain throughout Pieria, and after gathering a great quantity of plunder, he set off back, making his march toward Dium.

When the inhabitants had abandoned the place, he entered it and razed the walls, the houses, and the gymnasium; and besides these he burned the colonnades around the sacred precinct and destroyed the rest of the offerings—all that served for adornment or for the use of those gathering for the festivals; and he overturned all the statues of the kings as well.

This man, then, having from the very outset of the war and in his very first action carried on war not only against men but also against the gods, made his way back.

And on arriving in Aetolia, he was honored and admired—not as one who had committed sacrilege, but as a man who had proved himself good in the public service—having filled the Aetolians with empty hopes and irrational arrogance.

For from these events they conceived the notion that no one would dare even to come near Aetolia, while they themselves would fearlessly plunder not only the Peloponnese, as had been their custom, but Thessaly and Macedonia as well.

# Book 4 Chapter 63: Philip's Aetolian Campaign

Philip, however, on hearing of the events in Macedonia—and having at once reaped the consequences of the Epirotes' ignorance and contentiousness—was besieging Ambracus.

Making vigorous use of his earthworks and the rest of his siege apparatus, he quickly cowed those within, and took the stronghold in forty days all told.

He let the garrison—some five hundred Aetolians—depart under truce, and so fulfilled the Epirotes' desire by handing Ambracus over to them.

He himself, taking up his force, advanced past Charadra, eager to cross the so-called Ambracian gulf where it is narrowest, near the Acarnanian temple called Actium.

For the aforementioned gulf issues from the Sicilian sea between Epirus and Acarnania through an altogether narrow mouth—less than five stades wide—

but as it advances inland it stretches to a breadth of a hundred stades, while in length it runs in from the sea about three hundred stades; and it forms the boundary between Epirus and Acarnania, having Epirus on the north and Acarnania on the south.

Having ferried his force across at the aforementioned mouth, and having passed through Acarnania, he came to the Aetolian city called Phoeteiae, having taken along on the way two thousand Acarnanian infantry and two hundred cavalry.

Having encamped around the aforementioned city and made vigorous and terrifying assaults upon it for two days, he took it by capitulation, releasing the Aetolians within under truce.

But on the following night, since the city was thought to be still untaken, five hundred Aetolians came to its aid; the king, learning of their approach beforehand, set ambushes at certain well-chosen spots, killed most of them, and took the rest captive, except for a very few.

After this, having measured out grain rations for thirty days to his force from the captured stores—for a great quantity had been found amassed at Phoeteiae—he advanced, making his march into the territory of Stratus.

At a distance of about ten stades from the city he encamped beside the river Achelous, and from there he set out and fearlessly ravaged the country, since none of the enemy dared to come out against him.

# Book 4 Chapter 64: Philip Marches Toward Metropolis

At this time the Achaeans, hard pressed by the war and learning that the king was close at hand, sent ambassadors asking him to come to their aid.

These men, meeting Philip while he was still near Stratus, discussed the rest of their business according to their instructions, and, pointing out to the army the spoils to be had from the enemy's country, tried to persuade him to cross the Rhium and invade Elis.

The king, having heard them out, detained the ambassadors, saying that he would deliberate about their requests; he himself broke camp and advanced, making his march toward Metropolis and Conope.

The Aetolians held the citadel of Metropolis but abandoned the town. Philip, having set fire to Metropolis, pressed straight on toward Conope.

When the Aetolian cavalry had gathered and dared to confront him at the crossing of the river, which lies twenty stades in front of the city, and were confident that they would either stop him altogether or do much harm to the Macedonians during the crossing,

the king, perceiving their design, ordered his peltasts to enter the river first and to make the crossing in a dense body, locked together unit by unit with shields interlocked.

His men obeyed; and as soon as the first company had crossed, the Aetolian cavalry made a brief trial against it, but since this company stood fast with shields locked, and the second and third, as they crossed, joined their arms to the one already in place, the cavalry, accomplishing nothing and finding themselves in difficulty, withdrew toward the city.

And from then on the spirit of the Aetolians, having taken refuge in their cities, held itself quiet.

Philip, having crossed over with his army and fearlessly ravaged this district too, advanced, making his march toward Ithoria. This is a stronghold that lies squarely on the pass and is remarkable for its strength, both natural and man-made.

As he drew near, the garrison, terror-stricken, abandoned the place; and the king, having gained mastery of it, razed it to the ground.
