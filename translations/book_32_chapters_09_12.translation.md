# Book 32 Chapter 9: Scipio the Younger and Polybius

Olympiad 155, year 3. I. Affairs in Italy.

Because the Issaeans had repeatedly sent embassies to Rome and made it clear that the Delmatae were injuring their territory and the cities allied with them,

namely Epetium and Tragurium, and because the Daorsi were making the same complaint,

the senate sent envoys under Gaius Fannius to inspect conditions in Illyria, and especially those concerning the Delmatae.

While Pleuratus was alive, these people had obeyed him; but after his death, when Gentius succeeded to the kingship, they broke away from him, made war on their neighbors, and subdued the cities next to them, some of which even paid them tribute. This tribute consisted of livestock and grain.

So Fannius and his party set out on this mission.

# Book 32 Chapter 10: Polybius Responds to Scipio's Speech

King Ariarathes arrived in Rome while it was still summer.

Then, after Sextus Julius and Lucius Aurelius had entered office as consuls, he conducted private interviews, shaping both his presentation and his demeanor to fit the situation before him.

Envoys from Demetrius were also present, headed by Miltiades, prepared for either line of argument; for they were ready both to defend themselves against Ariarathes and, out of hostility, to accuse him.

Orophernes too had sent envoys, headed by Timotheus and Diogenes, bringing a crown for Rome and intending to renew friendship and alliance, but above all to join in the case against Ariarathes, defending some points and attacking others.

In these private interviews, however, the party of Diogenes and Miltiades made the stronger impression, since they were several men set against one, and because in the rest of their presentation they had the advantage of seeming to represent successful men against one who had suffered a reverse.

In the explanation of the facts too they were far superior.

For they had the audacity to say absolutely anything on any subject, paying no regard to the truth, while what they said could not be challenged because there was no one present to answer them.

So, with falsehood winning the day almost without a struggle, they thought the affair was proceeding exactly as they wished.

# Book 32 Chapter 11: Character of the Younger Scipio

II. Affairs in Asia.

Many men, through their craving for ever more and their overbearing spirit, have been ensnared by wealth; Orophernes, king of Cappadocia, was caught in this trap, ruined, and driven from his kingdom.

But after summarizing his downfall, I shall bring the narrative back to the usual order that I follow throughout this work.

For just now I passed over events in Greece and anticipated the events in Asia, namely those in Cappadocia, because Ariarathes' departure from Italy and his return to take up affairs there did not admit of any reasonable division.

Therefore I return to describe what happened in Greece at the same time.

Among these events there occurred a peculiar and extraordinary affair connected with the city of the Oropians.

I shall summarize the whole business by going back over some points and anticipating others, so that, since the matter is not especially notable in itself, I may not make the narrative trivial and obscure by reporting it piecemeal in separate stretches of time.

For when even the whole story can scarcely seem worth close attention to the hearer, who among those eager to learn would pay attention to disconnected parts told at intervals?

For the most part, when they are successful, people are generally of one mind; but in failure, becoming irritated by events, they turn touchy and hard to deal with even toward their friends.

This is what happened in the case of Orophernes too: when events turned against him, he and Theotimus began blaming one another. [Text missing.]

Polybius says in Book 32 that after ruling Cappadocia for only a short time and casting aside the customs of his fathers, Orophernes introduced a life of revelry and theatrical extravagance.
