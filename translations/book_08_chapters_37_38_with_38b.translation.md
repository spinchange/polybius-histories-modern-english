# Book 8 Chapter 37: Fall of Syracuse, B. C. 212

He counted the courses of stone; for the tower was built of regular blocks, so that it was quite easy to calculate from the ground the height of the battlements.

Some days later a deserter reported that the people in the city had now for three days been holding a public sacrifice to Artemis, and that, though they were using scanty food because of scarcity, they had wine in abundance, a large supply having been provided both by Epicydes and by the Syracusans. Marcus then selected a section of wall where it was lower, and, thinking it likely that the men would be drunk because of the festivity and the lack of solid food, decided to test the hope he had formed.

Two ladders were quickly made, fitted to the height of the wall, and he then proceeded with the remaining arrangements, speaking openly to the men best suited for the climb and for facing the first and most conspicuous danger, and filling them with great hopes for the future.

He also chose the men who were to assist them and carry the ladders, explaining nothing except that they should be ready for what was ordered. When they had obeyed exactly as instructed, he roused the foremost men at the proper hour of night.

Sending ahead the men with the ladders, along with a standard, a military tribune, and the others assigned to the attempt, and reminding them again of the rewards awaiting brave conduct, he then roused the entire army and sent out the leading contingents, one by one, under their standards and at intervals.

When about a thousand had gone, he himself followed after a short delay with the rest of the force.

The men carrying the ladders managed to set them safely against the wall without being noticed, and those appointed to the ascent at once mounted without hesitation.

When these too had escaped notice and had gained a firm position upon the wall, all the rest, no longer preserving the original order but each as he could, came running up by the ladders.

At first, as they advanced along the wall, they found the guard-posts deserted. For the men gathered in the towers because of the sacrifice were either still drinking or had long since fallen asleep in drunkenness.

Therefore the Romans came suddenly upon the first and then the next groups and quietly killed most of them before they knew what was happening.

Then, when they came near the Hexapylae and descended, they broke open the first postern gate built into the wall, through which they admitted the general and the rest of the army. In this way the Romans took Syracuse.

None of the citizens realized what was happening, because of the distance, since the city was large.

And the Romans took courage because they held the high ground around Epipolae.
