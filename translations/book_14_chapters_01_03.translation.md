# Book 14 Chapter 1: Scipio Plans To Attack the Punic Camp

II. Scipio’s Actions in Africa

While the consuls were occupied with these matters,

Publius in Libya, during the winter quarters, learned that the Carthaginians were equipping a fleet. He therefore gave attention both to that preparation and no less to the siege of Utica.

Yet he had not completely abandoned hope in regard to Syphax. Since their forces were not far apart, he kept sending messages, convinced that he would draw him away from the alliance with the Carthaginians.

For he did not despair that the king had already begun to feel satiety with the girl for whose sake he had chosen the Carthaginian side, and in general with his friendship for the Phoenicians, because of the natural fickleness of the Numidians and their disregard alike for gods and men.

But since his mind was occupied with many concerns and he entertained shifting hopes about the future, while dreading the danger before him because the enemy were many times more numerous, he seized on the following opportunity.

Some of the men sent to Syphax reported to him that the Carthaginians in their winter camp had made their huts from all kinds of timber and leafy brush, without earth,

while the Numidians made theirs from reeds, and the reinforcements now gathering from the towns were for the present building shelters from foliage alone, some inside the ditch and stockade, but most of them outside.

Publius therefore concluded that an attack by fire would be, for the enemy, the most unexpected course possible, and for his own side the most practical. So he turned his attention to preparing this plan.

In his exchanges with Publius, Syphax always came back to the same proposal: that the Carthaginians should withdraw from Italy, the Romans likewise from Libya, and that each side should keep whatever lay between those regions as they then held it.

Previously Publius would not tolerate even hearing of this. But now, through the men sent to him, he gave the Numidian a slight indication that the proposal he was making was not impossible.

As a result, Syphax, greatly relieved, became much bolder in pressing the matter.

So the number of messengers passing back and forth increased, and they came more frequently; sometimes they even stayed several days with one another without exciting suspicion.

On those occasions Publius always sent along with the envoys certain practical and military men, rough-looking and lowly, dressed in slave clothing, so that they might safely reconnoiter and inspect the approaches and entrances to each camp.

For there were two encampments: one, held by Hasdrubal, with thirty thousand infantry and three thousand cavalry; the other, about ten stades away, was the Numidian camp, with ten thousand horse and about fifty thousand foot.

This latter camp was easier to approach and its huts were especially fit to be burned, because the Numidians, as I said just now, used neither timber nor earth in building them, but simply canes and reeds.

# Book 14 Chapter 1a: Preface

I. From the Preface

Polybius says of himself and of the plan of his books: perhaps, then, the summaries prefixed to each Olympiad bring readers to understanding both because of the number and because of the greatness of the events, since deeds from the whole inhabited world are being brought under one general view.

Yet I think the events of this Olympiad especially will make an impression on readers, first because in this period the wars in Italy and Libya reached their conclusion. And who would not wish to learn what sort of outcome and ending they had?

For by nature all people, even if they gladly follow each separate action and speech in detail, nevertheless long to know the end of each story.

Besides this, it happened that at the same time the intentions of the kings became especially plain. For what had previously been said about them as rumor was then clearly recognized by everyone, even by those who had no desire at all to pry into public affairs.

Therefore, wishing to make the exposition proportionate to the importance of the events, I have not arranged the actions of those two years into a single book, as I did in the earlier parts of the work.

# Book 14 Chapter 2: Scipio Prepares to Attack Utica by Sea

When the season of spring was already beginning to show itself, and Scipio had fully investigated everything concerning the enemy for the attack just mentioned,

he launched his ships and fitted them with engines as though he intended to besiege Utica from the sea.

With about two thousand infantry he once more occupied the hill above the city and strongly fortified it with elaborate trenchworks,

making the enemy think he was doing this for the sake of the siege, though in truth he wanted to place a reserve there for the crucial moment of the operation, so that when the camps were separated from the Roman position, the soldiers watching Utica might not dare to sally out from the city, attack the nearby palisade, and besiege those guarding it.

While he was making these preparations, he also sent repeatedly to Syphax, asking whether, if he accepted the proposals being made, the Carthaginians too would be content with them, or whether they would once again say that they must deliberate about the concessions.

At the same time he instructed the envoys not to return to him before they had received an answer on these points.

When they arrived and delivered the message, the Numidian was persuaded that Scipio really meant to bring the negotiations to completion, both because the envoys said they would not depart before receiving his reply, and because Scipio showed concern about the Carthaginians’ assent.

Accordingly Syphax at once sent to Hasdrubal, explaining what had happened and urging him to accept peace; and he himself grew careless, allowing the Numidians who were joining him to camp outside his entrenchment.

Publius, for appearances, behaved in much the same way; in reality, however, he was pressing his preparations with the utmost energy.

When the Carthaginians informed Syphax that they agreed to conclude matters according to the terms, and the Numidian, delighted, communicated this to the envoys, the envoys immediately departed for their own camp to report to Publius what the king had done.

On hearing this, the Roman commander at once sent another embassy to Syphax to tell him that Publius himself approved and was eager for peace, but that the members of his council disagreed and insisted on standing by the existing position.

When they arrived, they conveyed this message to the Numidian.

Scipio made this embassy in order not to seem to be violating the truce, if, while the heralded negotiations for peace were still continuing between the two sides, he should undertake some act of war.

Once this declaration had been made, he believed that whatever he went on to do would be beyond reproach.

# Book 14 Chapter 3: Syphax and Hasdrubal Are Deluded

Syphax heard this with irritation, because he had already formed strong hopes about the peace; but he went to confer with Hasdrubal and explained the message brought him from the Romans.

After much perplexed discussion, they considered how they ought to handle what came next, being very far in their thoughts and plans from what was actually about to happen.

For they had no suspicion at all that they needed to take precautions or that some terrible blow was hanging over them; rather, they were full of eagerness to do something themselves and to draw the enemy down onto level ground.

At this time Publius, by his preparations and his orders, led the mass of the army to suppose that he had some operation in hand against Utica,

but, summoning at midday the ablest and most trustworthy tribunes, he disclosed the plan to them and ordered them, after taking supper at the proper hour, to lead the forces outside the palisade as soon as all the trumpeters gave the usual signal together.

For among the Romans it is the custom at supper time for all the buglers and trumpeters to sound near the general’s tent, so that the night watches may then take up their posts in the usual places.

After this he called back the scouts whom he had been sending into the enemy camps and compared and examined their reports about the approaches and entrances to the encampments, using Massanissa, because of his knowledge of the country, as both judge of what they said and adviser.
