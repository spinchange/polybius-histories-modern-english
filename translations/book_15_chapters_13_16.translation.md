# Book 15 Chapter 13: Cowardice of the Carthaginians

Now that the struggle had become a hand-to-hand fight, man against man, the mercenaries at first had the advantage in skill and boldness and wounded many Romans.

But the Romans, trusting in the firmness of their formation and in their equipment, kept pressing steadily forward.

At the same time, the Romans were supported and encouraged by the troops behind them, whereas the Carthaginians neither came up to aid their mercenaries nor closed in behind them, but lost heart.

So in the end the foreign troops gave way, and thinking they had been openly abandoned by their own side, they turned on the men posted behind them as they retreated and began killing them.

This forced many Carthaginians to die bravely against their will, since, while being slaughtered by the mercenaries, they had to fight at once against their own men and against the Romans.

Fighting in this wild and disordered fashion, they killed quite a number on both sides, friend and enemy alike.

By charging in this way they threw the maniples of the hastati into confusion. But when the officers of the principes saw what had happened, they halted and brought up their own line.

Most of the mercenaries and Carthaginians were then cut down, some by one another and some by the hastati themselves.

As for those who survived and fled, Hannibal did not allow them to mix with his reserve. He ordered his officers to present their weapons and prevent the fugitives from entering.

So these men were forced to withdraw toward the wings and the open spaces there.

# Book 15 Chapter 14: Victory of Scipio and Flight of Hannibal

But the ground between the armies that still remained in position was full of blood, slaughter, and corpses, and this obstacle created a serious difficulty for the Roman commander during the rout.

For the slippery footing caused by the dead, lying in heaps and soaked with blood, and the confusion of weapons scattered everywhere among the bodies were bound to make orderly advance extremely difficult.

Even so, Scipio carried the wounded to the rear of the line, recalled the pursuing hastati by trumpet signal, and stationed them directly in front of the enemy center.

Then he closed up the principes and triarii on each wing and ordered them to advance through the corpses.

When they had come through and drawn level with the hastati, the lines clashed with the greatest fury and eagerness.

Since both sides were nearly equal in numbers, spirit, courage, and equipment, the battle remained undecided for a long time, men dying where they stood because of sheer determination.

At last Massanissa and Laelius, wheeling back from their pursuit of the cavalry, arrived at exactly the right moment.

When they fell on Hannibal’s troops from the rear, most of the Carthaginians were cut down in their ranks, and of those who turned to flee only a few escaped entirely, since the cavalry were upon them and the ground was flat.

More than fifteen hundred Romans fell, while over twenty thousand Carthaginians were killed, and not many fewer were taken prisoner.

# Book 15 Chapter 15: Hannibal Did All He Could

Such was the outcome of the battle that decided everything and, under the commanders already mentioned, assigned empire to the Romans.

After the battle Scipio followed up the pursuit, plundered the Carthaginian camp, and then returned again to his own.

Hannibal, meanwhile, withdrew continuously with a few horsemen and reached Hadrumetum safely, having done in the battle everything possible that a good general, and one with experience in many great affairs, ought to do.

First of all, by entering into conference he tried to settle the crisis by negotiation.

That is what one does who understands success, yet does not trust Fortune, and who foresees the unexpected turns to which battles may come.

Then, when he had accepted battle, he handled matters in such a way that, against Romans armed in similar fashion, no better plan of engagement could have been devised than the one Hannibal then arranged.

For since the Roman formation is hard to break, and since in general and in detail a Roman force can fight in every direction out of a single deployment, with the nearest standards always wheeling to the point of danger,

and since their equipment gives both protection and confidence, by reason of the great shield and the sword’s power to endure heavy blows, they are for these reasons difficult opponents and hard to master.

# Book 15 Chapter 16: Hannibal's Tactics Sound

Yet despite all this, Hannibal adapted himself to each of these difficulties as well as circumstances allowed, leaving nothing better to be desired.

His numerous elephants he collected and threw forward for the express purpose of throwing the enemy lines into confusion and breaking their order.

He placed the mercenaries in front and the Carthaginians behind them so that the enemy’s bodies might first be worn down by exertion, the edge of their weapons dulled by the number of men they killed, and the Carthaginians, standing in the middle, compelled to remain and fight, as the poet says, “even the unwilling man must battle under necessity.”

The most warlike and steadfast of his troops he posted at a distance, so that, seeing what happened from afar and preserving their bodies and spirits fresh, they might employ their courage at the proper moment.

And if, after doing everything possible to win, a man previously undefeated still failed, he deserves pardon.

For sometimes chance itself thwarts the designs of brave men, and sometimes, as the proverb says, a good man meets another stronger than himself. One might well say that this is what happened to Hannibal then.
