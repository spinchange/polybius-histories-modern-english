# Book 3 Chapter 71: Hannibal Prepares An Ambush

These were the matters that occupied Tiberius. Hannibal, meanwhile, had long since noticed a stretch of ground between the two camps that was flat and bare of trees, yet well suited for an ambush, because a watercourse ran through it with a raised bank along its edge, and along that bank grew a continuous thicket of thorns and brambles. There he set about laying a trap for the enemy.

He was confident the scheme would easily escape notice. For the Romans were suspicious of wooded ground, since the Celts always set their ambushes in such places, but they felt no distrust of level and open country—

not realizing that, both for staying concealed and for keeping the men in ambush from coming to any harm, open ground is in fact better suited than woodland. The reason is that those lying in wait can see everything that is going on from a long way off, while in most such places there is cover enough to hide behind.

A chance watercourse with a low bank, or sometimes reeds, ferns, and a kind of thorn-scrub, can conceal not only infantry but at times even cavalry, provided one takes the modest precaution of laying the conspicuous parts of the armor flat against the ground and tucking the helmets in under the shields.

In any case, the Carthaginian general, after conferring with his brother Mago and with his council about the coming engagement, found them all in agreement with his plans.

So, as soon as the army had taken its evening meal, he called for his brother Mago—a young man, but full of dash and trained in warfare from boyhood—and assigned to him a hundred horsemen and an equal number of foot soldiers.

While it was still daylight, he had picked out the most robust men from the whole army and ordered them, after their supper, to report to his tent.

There he encouraged them and roused in them the spirit the occasion demanded, and instructed each man to choose ten of the bravest soldiers from his own unit and bring them to a certain spot in the camp that had already been designated.

When they had carried out his orders, he sent these men—a thousand cavalry and as many infantry—off by night to the place of ambush, supplying them with guides, and gave his brother his instructions about the timing of the attack.

At first light he himself assembled the Numidian cavalry, who were outstanding for their stamina, encouraged them, and—promising rewards to those who fought well—ordered them to ride up to the enemy's rampart, then quickly cross the river and skirmish so as to provoke the enemy into action. His aim was to catch his opponents before they had breakfasted and while they were still unprepared for what lay ahead.

He then gathered the rest of his officers, urged them likewise toward the coming battle, and ordered them all to breakfast and to see to their weapons and horses.

# Book 3 Chapter 72: Preparations for Battle

As soon as Tiberius saw the Numidian cavalry approaching, he immediately sent out his own cavalry on their own, with orders to engage and close with the enemy.

Next he sent out the foot javelineers, about six thousand of them, and he also set the rest of his army in motion from the camp, as though the whole issue were going to be decided at first sight. He was emboldened both by his superior numbers and by his cavalry's success the day before.

It was now around the winter solstice, and the day was snowy and bitterly cold; and since nearly all the men and horses had marched out without breakfast, at the start the troops were carried along by their dash and eagerness.

But when it came to crossing the river Trebia, which had risen because of the rain that had fallen during the night on the high ground above the camps, the infantry could barely get across, immersed up to their chests.

As a result the army was in distress from the cold and from hunger, all the more as the day wore on.

The Carthaginians, by contrast, had eaten and drunk in their tents, had got their horses ready, and were all rubbing themselves down with oil and arming around their fires.

Hannibal, watching for his moment, as soon as he saw that the Romans had crossed the river, threw out his spearmen and Balearic slingers—some eight thousand of them—as a covering force and led out his army.

Advancing about eight stades in front of his camp, he drew up his infantry, about twenty thousand in number—Iberians, Celts, and Libyans—in a single straight line,

and dividing his cavalry, he stationed them on either wing—more than ten thousand, counting his Celtic allies—while he split the elephants and posted them in front of both wings.

Tiberius at this same moment was recalling his cavalry, since he saw they could make no headway against the enemy. For the Numidians would fall back easily and in scattered fashion, then wheel about and press the attack again boldly and recklessly—this being the characteristic feature of Numidian fighting.

His infantry he drew up in the formation customary among the Romans: about sixteen thousand citizens and twenty thousand allies.

For with them a full army for major operations consists of that many men, whenever circumstances bring both consuls together at once.

After this, placing his cavalry—about four thousand—on either wing, he advanced against the enemy in imposing style, in good order

and at a steady, deliberate pace. When the two sides were now close to one another, the light-armed troops posted in front of each army closed with each other.

When this happened, the Romans were at a disadvantage in many respects, while for the Carthaginians the engagement turned out greatly in their favor,

since the Roman foot javelineers had been under hard strain since dawn, had spent most of their missiles in the clash with the Numidians, and found the missiles they had left useless from being soaked through by the persistent damp.

Much the same was happening with their cavalry and with the army as a whole.

With the Carthaginians the situation was the very opposite: drawn up fresh and in their prime, they were always ready and eager for whatever was needed.

And so, as soon as the light troops who had fought the opening skirmish had been received back through the gaps in the line, and the heavy-armed troops on both sides had clashed, the Carthaginian cavalry at once began pressing the enemy hard from both wings, since they far outnumbered them and were superior in the freshness of both men and horses, owing to the unworn condition I mentioned, in which they had set out.

As the Roman cavalry gave way and the wings of the phalanx were left exposed, the Carthaginian spearmen and the mass of Numidians, sweeping past their own front ranks and falling upon the Roman flanks, did them great damage and prevented them from fighting the troops facing them.

But the heavy-armed troops on both sides, who held the front and center positions of the whole line, kept up a close, hand-to-hand fight for a long time, evenly matched,

# Book 3 Chapter 74: The Romans Retreat to Placentia

making the contest a fierce one. It was at this point that the Numidians lying in ambush rose up and suddenly fell on the rear of the troops fighting in the center, throwing the Roman forces into great confusion and difficulty.

In the end both of Tiberius's wings, pressed in front by the elephants and all around—on their flanks—by the light-armed troops, were routed and driven, as the pursuit went on, toward the river that lay behind them.

After this, of the Romans stationed in the center of the action, those positioned in the rear were destroyed and suffered terribly under the attack of the men from the ambush,

while those in the foremost ranks, forced to fight on, overpowered the Celts and a portion of the Libyans, killed many of them, and cut their way through the Carthaginian line.

Seeing that their own wings had been driven off the field, they gave up any thought of going to their aid or of returning to their own camp—wary of the enemy's superior numbers of cavalry, and hindered by the river and by the driving, swirling downpour beating down on their heads.

So, keeping their ranks, they withdrew in a body and in safety to Placentia, numbering no fewer than ten thousand.

Of the rest, the greater part perished near the river at the hands of the elephants and the cavalry,

while those of the infantry who escaped, and most of the cavalry, made their withdrawal toward the body of men just mentioned and were brought back together with them to Placentia.

The Carthaginian army pursued the enemy as far as the river but, unable to advance any farther because of the storm, returned again to its camp.

They were all overjoyed at the battle, feeling they had won a complete success; for it turned out that few of the Iberians and Libyans had fallen, and that the heaviest losses had been among the Celts.

But from the rain and the snow that followed, they were so badly affected that the elephants died—all but one—and many of the men and horses perished as well from the cold.

# Book 3 Chapter 75: Better Success in Spain

Tiberius, well aware of what had happened but wishing as far as possible to hide the disaster from the people in Rome, sent messengers to report that a battle had been fought but that the storm had robbed them of the victory.

For the moment the Romans believed the reports that reached them; but before long they learned that the Carthaginians were holding their own camp and that all the Celts had gone over to friendship with them,

while their own men had abandoned their camp, had withdrawn from the field, had all gathered together into the cities, and were being supplied with necessities by sea up the river Padus. They then understood all too clearly what had really happened in the battle.

And so, since the affair had come upon them as a complete surprise, they devoted themselves with special energy to their remaining preparations and to guarding the regions that lay exposed, sending legions to Sardinia and Sicily, and garrisons to Tarentum and to other places of strategic value; they also fitted out sixty quinqueremes.

Gnaeus Servilius and Gaius Flaminius, who happened to be the consuls newly installed at that time, were assembling the allies and enrolling their own legions.

They also forwarded supplies, some to Ariminum and some to Etruria, with the intention of taking the field from those positions.

They sent too to Hiero asking for help, and he dispatched to them five hundred Cretans and a thousand peltasts. In short, they were vigorously making every kind of preparation on every front.

For the Romans are at their most formidable, both collectively and as individuals, when they are gripped by genuine fear.

# Book 3 Chapter 76: Gnaeus Scipio in Spain

About this same time Gnaeus Cornelius, who had been left by his brother Publius in command of the naval forces (as I mentioned earlier), set sail from the mouths of the Rhone and put in with his whole fleet on the Iberian coast at the region called Emporium.

Starting from there he made a series of landings: those of the coastal inhabitants who would not submit, as far as the river Iber, he besieged, while those who came over to him he treated kindly, taking every possible care for their welfare.

Having secured the coastal peoples who had joined him, he advanced with his whole army, marching into the interior,

for by now he had also gathered a large allied contingent of Iberians. As he went forward he won some cities over and subdued others.

When the Carthaginians—those whom Hanno had been left in charge of in that district—encamped against him near a city called Cissa, Gnaeus engaged them in a pitched battle, defeated them, and gained a great quantity of property, since the whole baggage of the forces that had set out for Italy had been left behind with them.

He made allies and friends of all the peoples north of the river Iber, and took prisoner both Hanno, the Carthaginian general, and Andobales, the leader of the Iberians.

This Andobales was ruler of the inland regions and had always been exceptionally well disposed toward the Carthaginians.

Hasdrubal quickly grasped what had happened and came to the rescue, crossing the river Iber.

Learning that the men from the Roman fleet had been left behind and were carrying on carelessly and overconfidently because of the success of the land forces,

he took from his own army about eight thousand infantry and around a thousand cavalry, caught the men from the ships scattered about the countryside, killed many of them, and forced the rest to flee to their vessels.

He then withdrew, recrossed the river Iber, and set about preparing and garrisoning the regions south of the river, taking up his winter quarters at New Carthage.

Gnaeus, having rejoined his fleet and punished those responsible for the disaster according to their own customs, then brought his land and sea forces together in one place and took up his winter quarters at Tarraco.

By dividing the spoils fairly among his soldiers, he won great goodwill from them and made them eager for what lay ahead.

# Book 3 Chapter 77: Hannibal's Treatment of Roman Prisoners

Such was the state of affairs in Iberia. As the spring season set in, Gaius Flaminius took up his forces and advanced through Etruria, pitching camp before the city of the Arretines,

while Gnaeus Servilius went in the opposite direction, toward Ariminum, to watch there for the enemy's invasion.

Hannibal, wintering in Celtic territory, kept the Romans among the prisoners from the battle under guard, giving them only modest rations,

but their allies he at first treated with every kindness; and afterward he gathered them together and addressed them, declaring that he had not come to make war on them but on the Romans on their behalf. Therefore, he said, if they were sensible, they should

hold fast to friendship with him.

For he had come, first, to win back freedom for the peoples of Italy, and likewise to help each of them recover the cities and territory they had lost to the Romans.

With these words he released them all without ransom to their own homes, wishing in this way both to draw the inhabitants of Italy to his side and to alienate them from their goodwill toward Rome, and to stir up those who felt they had suffered any loss of cities or harbors under Roman rule.
