# Book 11 Chapter 4: A Plea For Union In Greece

"I think the facts themselves have made it clear, men of Aetolia, that neither King Ptolemy nor the city of Rhodes nor the peoples of Byzantium, Chios, and Mytilene treat a reconciliation with you as a minor matter.

For this is not the first time or the second that we have spoken to you about peace. Ever since you began this war, we have sat by you, seized every opportunity, and never stopped reminding you of these things,

having in view for the present the destruction of both yourselves and the Macedonians, and for the future the safety of our own countries and of the rest of the Greeks.

For just as with a fire, once someone has set the material alight, what follows is no longer under his control, but the blaze takes whatever course chance gives it, governed mostly by the winds and by the destruction of the fuel beneath it, and often in an irrational way rushes back upon the very man who first kindled it,

so too war, once some people have set it blazing, sometimes destroys them first and sometimes sweeps onward unjustly ruining all that lies in its path, always finding new fuel and being fanned, as if by winds, by the ignorance of neighboring peoples.

Therefore, men of Aetolia, since the islanders as a whole and the Greeks who dwell in Asia have sent us here to beg you to put an end to the war and choose peace, because what is happening concerns us as well, come to your senses, feel shame, and listen to those who urge you.

If by some chance you were fighting a war that was unprofitable, as most wars are, but still glorious both in its original purpose and in the reputation likely to follow from it, perhaps someone might forgive your eagerness.

But if it is the most disgraceful of all wars, full of dishonor and slander, does the matter not require the greatest reflection?

I will speak openly about what I think, and if you are wise you will bear it calmly.

For it is far better to be reproved at the right moment and be saved than, by hearing pleasing words, soon to perish yourselves and drag the rest of the Greeks down with you."

# Book 11 Chapter 5: Dangers of the Treaty With Rome

"Set your own blindness before your eyes. You claim to be making war on Philip for the sake of the Greeks, so that, being safe, they may not have to do what he commands; but in reality you are making war for the enslavement and ruin of Greece.

That is what your treaty with the Romans says—a treaty which formerly existed only on paper, but now is seen taking shape in events themselves.

At first the written terms merely brought shame on you; now by your actions that shame is made visible to everyone.

Philip is now only a name and a pretext for the war, for he is suffering no great harm. He has as allies most of the Peloponnesians, the Boeotians, Euboeans, Phocians, Locrians, Thessalians, and Epirotes; and it is against these peoples that you have made your treaty,

on the condition that their persons and movable goods belong to the Romans, while their cities and land belong to the Aetolians.

If you yourselves captured a city, you would not tolerate insulting free people or burning their towns, because you regard such conduct as cruel and barbaric.

Yet you have made treaties by which you have handed over all the other Greeks to barbarians for the most shameful outrages and lawless acts.

Formerly this was not understood. But now, because of the people of Oreus and the wretched Aeginetans, you have become plain to all, as if Fortune had deliberately set your folly up on a public stage.

Such are the origins of the war and such the things already happening. And if everything now turns out exactly as you wish, what sort of end should one expect? Surely the beginning of great evils for all the Greeks?"

# Book 11 Chapter 6: Philip In Aetolia Again

"For anyone can see that if the Romans put an end to the war in Italy—and that moment is close at hand now that Hannibal has been shut up within a very narrow corner of Bruttium—

they will next hurl their full force at Greece, claiming in words that they are helping the Aetolians against Philip, but in truth intending to bring everything there under their own power. I think this is perfectly plain.

And whether, once masters, they govern well or badly, the credit and title will belong to them if they act well, while if they act badly, the profits from those they ruin and the authority over those they spare will likewise belong to them.

Then you will call the gods to witness, when neither any god will still wish nor any human being be able to help you.

Perhaps from the beginning you ought to have foreseen all this,

for that would have been fitting for you. But since many future events escape human foresight, now at least, after seeing from these things what happens, you ought to take better counsel about the future.

In any case, for our part, we have left undone nothing that true friends ought to say or do in the present, and we have spoken our mind frankly about what is coming.

We ask and urge you not to begrudge either yourselves or the rest of the Greeks their freedom and safety."

This speech seemed to produce some change of mind among the majority. After him came the ambassadors from Philip, who passed over the arguments point by point and said they had come with two instructions.

If the Aetolians chose peace, they were to accept it at once; but if not, they were to call the gods and the ambassadors then present from the other Greek states to witness, and to depart after declaring that for whatever should happen afterward to the Greeks the Aetolians, not Philip, must be held responsible.

# Book 11 Chapter 7: Philip Vandalizes Thermus

Philip bitterly lamented that he had very nearly failed to get Attalus into his hands.

Then, after marching to Lake Trichonis and arriving at Thermus, where there was a sanctuary of Apollo, he defaced all the offerings that he had left untouched before, showing bad judgment in the former case and bad temper in the latter.

For when a man is angry with human beings and in his anger becomes impious toward the divine, that is a sign of utter irrationality.
