But when the Celtiberians met them near Abba—men who had been hired by the Carthaginians and numbered more than four thousand—they put confidence in this reinforcement, halted where they were, and took a little courage in spirit.

At the same time the girl—who was the daughter of the general Hasdrubal and the wife of Syphax, as I said above—begged and pleaded with him to remain and not abandon the Carthaginians at such a crisis. The Numidian was persuaded and agreed to what was asked.

The Celtiberians also gave the Carthaginians no small grounds for hope. Instead of four thousand, they reported them to be ten thousand, and said that in battle they were irresistible both in spirit and in equipment.

Lifted up by this report and by the coarse and public chatter that spread everywhere, the Carthaginians were twice as strongly encouraged to try once more for the open country.

Finally, within thirty days, they pitched a camp on the place called the Great Plains together with the Numidians and the Celtiberians, the whole force numbering no fewer than thirty thousand.

# Book 14 Chapter 8: Scipio Determines to Attack

When news of this reached the Roman camp, Publius at once set about marching out. After giving instructions to those besieging Utica and to the force operating by sea about what they ought to do, he advanced with the whole army in light marching order.

On the fifth day he arrived at the Great Plains and, after coming near the enemy, on the first day encamped on a certain hill about thirty stades away from them.

On the next day he descended into the plain and pitched camp seven stades away, posting the cavalry in front.

They remained there for the next two days, testing one another a little by skirmishing attacks; on the fourth day both sides deliberately led out and drew up their forces.

Publius arranged his line in the ordinary Roman fashion, placing first the standards of the hastati, behind them those of the principes, and lastly those of the triarii in the rear.

Of the cavalry he stationed the Italian horse on the right and the Numidians with Massanissa on the left.

Syphax and Hasdrubal placed the Celtiberians in the center opposite the Roman infantry formations, the Numidians on the left wing, and the Carthaginians on the right.

At the very first onset the Numidians gave way before the Italian cavalry, and the Carthaginians before the men under Massanissa, already utterly defeated in spirit by their repeated previous failures.

But the Celtiberians fought bravely when they came to close quarters with the Romans. For they had no hope of safety in flight, because they did not know the country, nor, if taken alive, any hope of being spared because of their faithlessness toward Publius.

For, though they had suffered no injury from him during the operations in Iberia, they seemed to have come unjustly and in violation of good faith to aid the Carthaginians against the Romans.

Nevertheless, as soon as the troops on the wings had broken, they were quickly surrounded by the principes and triarii and all cut down except for a very few.

Thus the Celtiberians perished, but not before rendering great service to the Carthaginians, not only during the battle itself but also during the retreat.

For if they had not hindered the Romans, and if the Romans had immediately pressed on after the fugitives, only a very few of the enemy would have escaped altogether.

But as it was, because the Romans were delayed by dealing with them, Syphax and his cavalry withdrew safely to their own country, while Hasdrubal escaped with those saved from the disaster to Carthage.

# Book 14 Chapter 9: Scipio's Victory On the Great Plains

When the Roman commander had made proper arrangements about the spoil and the prisoners, he summoned the council and deliberated about what ought to be done next.

It was resolved that the commander, Publius, should remain with part of the force and move through the cities, while Laelius and Massanissa, taking the Numidians and part of the Roman army, should pursue Syphax and give him no time to recover his wits or prepare.

Having reached this decision, they separated, the one party going against Syphax with the forces mentioned, while the commander himself went against the cities.

Some of these attached themselves voluntarily to the Romans out of fear; others he besieged and took by storm.

Indeed, the whole country was ripe for a change, since it had long been exposed without interruption to hardships and exactions, because the wars in Iberia had lasted so many years.

In Carthage, where there had already been great disorder, an even greater upheaval now followed, since they had suffered so severe a blow for the second time and had given up the hopes they had previously entertained.

Still, those among the advisers who seemed the most manly urged that they should now put to sea with the fleet against those besieging Utica, attempt to raise the siege, and fight a naval battle with the enemy, who were unprepared in that respect.

They also argued that men should be sent to Hannibal and that they should omit no extreme measure in testing even this hope, since both enterprises offered, on reasonable grounds, great opportunities for saving themselves.

Others said the times no longer allowed such measures, and that they must fortify the city and prepare for a siege; for chance, if only they remained united, would offer them many opportunities.

At the same time they advised deliberating about peace and treaty terms—on what conditions and by what means they might free themselves from their present troubles.
