# Book 4 Chapter 43: The Site of Byzantium

The mouth that joins the Pontus and the Propontis is, as I said just now, one hundred and twenty stades in length. Hieron marks its boundary toward the Pontus, while the narrows at Byzantium mark its boundary toward the Propontis.

Between these two points lies the Hermaeum, set on a kind of promontory of Europe that juts out into the strait. It lies about five stades from Asia and stands at the very narrowest point of the whole mouth. It was here, they say, that Darius bridged the channel at the time when he made his crossing to attack the Scythians.

Now along the rest of its course from the Pontus the flow of the current is fairly uniform, because the lands bordering the strait on either side are alike in form.

But when the stream, carried down from the Pontus and squeezed together, strikes with force against the European Hermaeum, where I said the narrowest point lies, it then turns aside, as if from a blow, and dashes against the regions of Asia on the opposite side.

From there, in turn, as if recoiling, it makes its return back toward the headlands of Europe called the Hestiae.

Setting out from there once again, it falls upon what is called the Cow, a place in Asia, the spot on which the myths say Io first set foot after she had crossed over.

Finally the current, starting from the Cow, runs straight toward Byzantium itself. Splitting around the city, a small part of it is cut off into the gulf called the Horn, while the greater part swerves away once more.

It is no longer strong enough, however, to reach the land opposite, on which Calchedon stands.

For having made its returns back and forth many times, and since the channel here is broad, the current has by now spent its force at this point and no longer makes short reflections at a sharp angle toward the far shore, but rather at an obtuse one.

down the middle of the channel. And the very thing that makes the city of the Byzantines so excellently situated, and that of the Calchedonians the opposite, is just what I have now described—even though, to the eye, the position of both seems equally favorable for advantage.

Yet, all the same, it is not easy to sail into the one even if you wish to, whereas to the other the current carries you of necessity, even against your will, as I said just now.

Here is the proof of this: those who want to cross from Calchedon to Byzantium cannot sail straight across because of the current between them, but instead skirt along toward the Cow and the place called Chrysopolis.

It was this place that the Athenians once seized, on the advice of Alcibiades, when they first set about levying tolls on those sailing into the Pontus; and from there they let their ships go forward with the current, which carries them of necessity to Byzantium.

The situation is similar for a voyage on the other side of the city of the Byzantines.

For whether a man runs from the Hellespont before the south winds, or toward the Hellespont from the Pontus before the Etesian winds, the voyage along Europe from the city of the Byzantines is straight and at the same time easily made, on toward the narrows of the Propontis at Abydos and Sestos, and back again likewise from there to Byzantium.

But from Calchedon along the Asian coast it is just the opposite, because the passage is full of bays and because the territory of the Cyziceni projects far out.

For a ship carried from the Hellespont toward Calchedon to make use of the voyage along Europe, and then, as it draws near the region of Byzantium, to bend and run across to Calchedon, is difficult on account of the current and the things already mentioned.

Likewise, again, for a ship sailing out, to run straight for Thrace is utterly impossible, both because of the current between them and because both winds blow against both attempts.

For the south wind carries ships into the Pontus and the north wind out of it, and one must use these very winds for each of the two voyages.
