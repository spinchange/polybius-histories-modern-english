# Book 38 Chapter 21: Chapter 21

[...] The text here is badly broken in the witness, but the sense is that Polybius is completing Scipio's remark about the danger of overconfidence and the importance of good judgment.

No clearer or more practical lesson than this is easy to state.

For to take account, in the midst of great achievements and the calamities of one's enemies, of one's own affairs and of the opposite condition, and in general to keep the instability of Fortune before one's eyes amid success, is the mark of a great and complete man, and one worthy of remembrance. —

# Book 38 Chapter 22: Chapter 22

When Scipio saw the city ... then utterly ending in complete destruction, it is said that he burst into tears and was plainly seen weeping over his enemies;

and for a long time he stood absorbed in thought, realizing that cities, nations, and all sovereignties must change, just as human beings do. So it happened to Ilium, once a fortunate city; so too to the empire of the Assyrians, Medes, and Persians, and to the Macedonian power that had lately shone so brightly — whether he said this willingly, or whether the verse came to his mind unbidden:

"The day shall come when sacred Ilium shall perish,

and Priam, and Priam's people of the spear-loving race."

Polybius asked him this boldly, for he had been his tutor;

and they say that, whatever he intended by the remark, he did not mention his own fatherland openly, though he was surely thinking of the fate of human affairs and was afraid for it. Polybius himself records these things as he heard them.
