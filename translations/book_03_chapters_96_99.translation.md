# Book 3 Chapter 96: Naval Success of the Romans In Spain

Hasdrubal and his men, having been warned well in advance by their scouts that the enemy was bearing down on them, drew up their land forces along the shore and at the same time ordered the crews to embark in the ships.

When the Romans were already close at hand, they gave the signal for battle and put out to sea, having resolved to fight. They engaged the enemy and for a short while contended for the victory, but before long they began to give way.

For the support of the infantry stationed along the shore did them less good in lending them courage for the danger than it did harm by holding out a ready hope of safety.

In the end, after losing two ships together with their crews, and the oar-banks and marines of four others, they fled, turning aside toward the land.

As the Romans pressed on against them with fury, they ran their ships up onto the beach and, leaping out of the vessels, made their escape to the troops drawn up there.

The Romans came boldly right up to the shore, took in tow such of the vessels as could still be moved, and sailed off in overwhelming joy, having beaten the enemy at the first onset, gained command of the sea, and captured twenty-five of their ships.

So in Iberia, as a result of this, the prospects of the Romans had grown more promising on account of the success just described.

The Carthaginians, when news of this setback reached them, at once manned seventy ships and sent them out, having judged it essential, for the sake of all their designs, to keep their hold on the sea.

These ships made first for Sardinia, and from there put in at the region around Pisa in Italy, since those aboard were convinced that they would link up there with Hannibal's forces.

But the Romans quickly put out against them from Rome itself with a hundred and twenty quinqueremes, and when the Carthaginians learned of this advance, they sailed back again to Sardinia and after that returned once more to Carthage.

Gnaeus Servilius, who commanded the fleet just mentioned, followed the Carthaginians for some distance, convinced that he would overtake them, but as he fell far behind he gave up the chase.

He first put in at Lilybaeum in Sicily; after that he sailed down to Libya, to the island of the Cercinitae, and after taking money from them on condition of not ravaging their territory, he departed.

On the voyage back he gained control of the island of Cossyrus and, after installing a garrison in its little town, he put in again at Lilybaeum.

went back to the land forces. Meanwhile the Senate, learning of the advantage that Gnaeus had won in the sea battle, and considering it useful—or rather essential—not to abandon affairs in Iberia, but to make a stand against the Carthaginians and to press the war forward,

made ready twenty ships and put Publius Scipio in command of them, in accordance with the plan formed at the outset, and dispatched him in haste to his brother Gnaeus, to manage the campaign in Iberia jointly with him.

For they were deeply anxious that the Carthaginians might gain control of those regions and, securing abundant supplies and manpower, lay claim to the sea more completely and join in the attack on Italy, sending armies and money to Hannibal.

For this reason, regarding this war too as of great importance, they sent out the ships and Publius. On reaching Iberia and joining his brother, he rendered great service to the common cause.

For never before had the Romans ventured to cross the river Iber—they had been content with the friendship and alliance of the tribes on the near side—but now they crossed it, and for the first time found the courage to lay claim to affairs on the far side, with chance, too, lending them considerable help in the circumstances that surrounded them.

For after overawing the Iberians living about the crossing, they came to the city of the Saguntines and pitched camp about forty stades away, near the temple of Aphrodite,

having taken up a position naturally well suited both for safety from the enemy and for the supply of provisions from the sea;

for it happened that their fleet was at the same time making its passage along the coast beside them. It was there that

# Book 3 Chapter 98: Treason of Abilyx

a reversal of affairs of the following kind took place. At the time when Hannibal was making his march to Italy, from all the cities of Iberia whose loyalty he distrusted he took as hostages the sons of the most prominent men, and he deposited them all in the city of the Saguntines, both because of its strength and because of the loyalty of the men left in charge of it. Now there was a certain Iberian,

Abilyx by name, second to none of the Iberians in standing and in the circumstances of his life, and reputed to surpass all the rest by far in his goodwill and loyalty toward the Carthaginians.

This man, observing how affairs stood and judging that the prospects of the Romans were the more promising, worked out in his own mind a calculation about betraying the hostages—a calculation thoroughly Iberian and barbarian.

For, convinced that he could rise to great power among the Romans if he offered, at the critical moment, a proof of loyalty together with a useful service, he set about breaking faith with the Carthaginians and handing the hostages over to the Romans.

He observed that Bostar, the Carthaginian general—who had been sent by Hasdrubal to stop the Romans from crossing the river, but, not daring to do this, had withdrawn and was encamped in the parts of Saguntum facing the sea—was a guileless man, gentle by nature,

and well-disposed and trusting toward himself; so he opened the matter of the hostages to Bostar, saying that, now that the Romans had crossed the river, the Carthaginians could no longer hold Iberia together by fear, but that the situation called for the goodwill of their subjects;

now therefore, with the Romans drawn near and laying siege to Saguntum, and the city in danger, if Bostar were to bring out the hostages and restore them to their parents and their cities, he would frustrate the ambition of the Romans—for this was the very thing they were most eager to accomplish, by getting control of the hostages—

and he would call forth the goodwill of all the Iberians toward the Carthaginians, since he had foreseen what was coming and had taken thought for the safety of the hostages. And he said that he would multiply the favor many times over by personally taking charge of the affair.

For, by restoring the boys to their cities, he would win over the goodwill not only of those who had fathered them but also of the people at large, setting before their eyes, through what was happening, the chosen policy and magnanimity of the Carthaginians toward their allies.

He urged Bostar also to expect a host of gifts privately from those who recovered their children; for each of them, unexpectedly regaining the dearest of their kin, would compete in showering benefits on the man in charge of the affair.

what he proposed. And for the time being Abilyx went away, having fixed a day on which he would come with everything needed for conveying the boys back.

Arriving by night at the Roman camp and falling in with some of the Iberians serving with the Romans, through them he came before the generals.

Reckoning up at length the surge of feeling and the change of allegiance toward them that would come about among the Iberians if they gained control of the hostages, he undertook to hand the boys over to them.

Publius and his colleague received the prospect with the greatest eagerness and promised large rewards, and for the moment Abilyx went off to his own country, having agreed on the day, the hour, and the place at which those who were to receive him would have to wait.

After this he took the friends he needed and came to Bostar, and when the boys had been handed over to him from Saguntum, he set out by night, as though wishing to escape notice, slipped past the enemy's stockade, and came at the appointed time and place and delivered all the hostages into the hands of the Roman commanders.

Publius and his colleague honored Abilyx exceptionally and made use of him for the restoration of the hostages to their homelands, sending along their own trusted men.

Going from city to city and, through the restoration of the boys, setting before everyone's eyes the mildness and magnanimity of the Romans in contrast to the faithlessness and harshness of the Carthaginians, and adding to this the example of his own change of sides, he spurred many of the Iberians on toward friendship with the Romans.

Bostar, having shown himself, in handing the hostages over to the enemy, more childish than befitted his years, fell into no ordinary dangers.

For the present, with the season now pressing, both sides broke up their forces into winter quarters, the affair of the boys having proved a considerable piece of luck for the Romans in view of the designs that lay before them. Such was the state of affairs in Iberia.
