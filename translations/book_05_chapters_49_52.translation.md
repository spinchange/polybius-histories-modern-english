# Book 5 Chapter 49: Hermeias Overruled

With his hopes now turned toward this campaign, the council met again, and the king called on them to speak about how the preparations against Molon ought to be used. Epigenes once more began the discussion by speaking about the present crisis.

He said that they ought long ago to have acted without delay on his advice, before the enemy gained such great advantages; but even now, he maintained, they still had to take the matter firmly in hand.

At this Hermeias again flew into a blind and reckless rage and began to insult him.

While lavishly praising himself and bringing charges against Epigenes that were both false and beside the point, he kept urging the king not to disregard the hopes they had in Coele-Syria so irrationally, nor to give them up.

This offended most of those present and displeased Antiochus too; and only with difficulty, because the king took great pains to reconcile them, was the quarrel brought to an end.

Since the majority thought that Epigenes had spoken more necessarily and more usefully, the decision was ratified: they were to campaign against Molon and devote themselves to that business.

Hermeias quickly put on a show of agreement and changed his tone, declaring that all ought to support the decision without excuse; and he himself at once made a great display of readiness.

# Book 5 Chapter 50: Death of Epigenes

When the forces had assembled at Apamea, a disturbance arose among the mass of the soldiers over the pay and rations still owed to them.

Seeing that the king was alarmed and frightened by the outbreak at so critical a moment, Hermeias promised to settle all the allowances due to the troops, provided the king would consent not to take Epigenes with him on the campaign.

For, he said, no orderly conduct of the expedition was possible when so much anger and faction had been stirred up among the men.

The king heard this with displeasure and was extremely eager, because of Epigenes' experience in warfare, to have him accompany the campaign.

But he was so hemmed in and already so dominated by Hermeias' scheming, watchfulness, and ingratiating service that he was no longer master of himself; so, yielding to the pressure of the moment, he granted what was demanded.

When Epigenes withdrew, as ordered, to his quarters, the men in the council were appalled at the spite behind it.

The troops, on the other hand, once they had received what they asked for, swung around and became well disposed toward the man responsible for setting their pay right, except for the Cyrrhestians.

These mutinied, and being nearly six thousand in number, went over to open revolt and caused a great deal of trouble for a considerable time. But in the end they were defeated in battle by one of the king's generals; most were killed, and the survivors surrendered themselves to the king's mercy.

Hermeias, having thus made the king's friends dependent on him through fear and the troops through the services he had rendered them, broke camp and marched on with the king.

As for Epigenes, he devised the following plot against him, taking as his accomplice Alexis, the commander of the citadel of Apamea.

He wrote a letter as though it had been sent from Molon to Epigenes, and by dazzling one of Epigenes' servants with great promises persuaded him to slip the letter into Epigenes' papers.

Once this had been done, Alexis immediately appeared and asked Epigenes whether he had received any letters from Molon.

When Epigenes indignantly denied it, Alexis demanded the right to search. Entering at once, he quickly found the letter, and using it as his pretext, killed Epigenes on the spot.

After this, the king was persuaded that Epigenes had justly perished; those about the court, however, suspected what had really happened, but kept silent out of fear.

# Book 5 Chapter 51: Antiochus Marches Through Mesopotamia

When Antiochus reached the Euphrates and took over the force stationed there, he set out again, and after marching as far as Antioch in Mygdonia he stayed there about the winter solstice, wishing to wait out the onset and height of the season.

After remaining there about forty days, he advanced to Libba.

There a council was held on the question of how they should advance against Molon and from where and in what manner they should obtain supplies for the march, since Molon happened to be in the region around Babylon.

Hermeias thought they should march along the Tigris, keeping that river, as well as the Lycus and the Caprus, in front of them.

But Zeuxis, with the fate of Epigenes before his eyes, hesitated to say what he really thought; still, because Hermeias' ignorance was so obvious, he at last found the courage to advise that they should cross the Tigris.

He pointed out both the general difficulty of marching along the river and the further fact that, after traversing a considerable distance and then passing through six days of desert, they would reach what was called the King's Canal.

If the enemy occupied that in advance, crossing would be impossible, while a retreat back through the desert would plainly be dangerous, especially because of the shortage of supplies they would then face.

By crossing the Tigris, on the other hand, he made it clear that the people of Apolloniatis would openly repent and incline toward the king, since even now they were obeying Molon not by choice but through compulsion and fear.

He also showed that the country was excellent and would furnish the army with abundant provisions.

Most important of all, he said, Molon would be cut off from returning to Media and from getting support from those regions.

As a result, Molon would be forced either to risk a battle, or, if he refused to do so, to see his troops quickly transfer their hopes to the king.

# Book 5 Chapter 52: Antiochus Advances Against Molon

When Zeuxis' advice was adopted, they immediately divided the army into three parts and ferried the men and baggage across the river at three separate places.

After this they marched in the direction of Dura and relieved that city from the siege by a sudden attack, for one of Molon's commanders happened to be besieging it.

Then, by continuing their marches without interruption, on the eighth day they crossed the pass called Oreicum and came down into Apollonia.

At the same time Molon learned of the king's arrival. Since he distrusted the populations of Susiana and Babylonia, because he had only recently gained control of them and that in an unexpected way, and since he also feared being cut off from a return to Media, he decided to bridge the Tigris and move his forces across.

He was eager, if possible, to seize the rough country of Apolloniatis first, because he had confidence in the large number of slingers known as Cyrtii.

Once this decision had been carried out, he pressed on with a rapid and strenuous march.

Thus it happened that, just as Molon was approaching the places just mentioned and the king was setting out from Apollonia with all his forces, the light troops sent ahead by both sides ran into one another on some heights.

At first they engaged and tested each other; but when the armies on both sides came up, they broke off. For the moment both sides withdrew to their own camps and encamped about forty stades apart.

When night fell, Molon reflected that for rebels fighting kings, a battle in daylight and face to face was dangerous and a disadvantage; so he resolved to attack Antiochus by night.

Selecting from the whole camp the men best suited for the task and in the prime of strength, he made a circuit by certain routes, intending to launch his attack from higher ground on the right.

But during the march he learned that ten young men had deserted in a body to Antiochus, and so he abandoned this plan.

Quickly reversing course, he returned to his own entrenchment and arrived around dawn, filling the whole camp with noise and confusion.

For the men posted by the palisade, startled from sleep by the rush of those approaching, nearly bolted from the camp.
