# Book 10 Chapter 25: Philip's Increasing Deterioration

For the present situation, he said, is very like the arrangement and handling of troops in battle.

In battle it is usually the light-armed and most active part of the force that first runs the risk and is first destroyed, while the phalanx and the heavily armed take the credit for the result.

So now in the same way the Aetolians and those Peloponnesians allied with them are the ones who run the danger, while the Romans remain in reserve, standing like a phalanx.

And if these allies fail and are destroyed, the Romans will simply turn away from the affair and go off unharmed.

But if they win—which may the gods prevent!—then together with them the Romans will bring the rest of the Greeks also under their power.

For every democratic alliance has great need of friendship and support because of the irrationality that arises among multitudes.

# Book 10 Chapter 26: Philip V

When King Philip of Macedon had finished presiding over the Nemean games, he returned again to Argos and laid aside the diadem and the purple robe, wishing to present himself as a man equal to the crowd, mild, and democratic.

But the more democratic the dress he put on, the greater and more monarchical was the power he claimed.

For he was no longer merely trying widows, nor content with seducing married women; any woman who pleased him he now summoned by direct order, and those who did not readily obey he insulted by leading drunken revels to their houses.

He would summon some women's sons and others' husbands on absurd pretexts and bully them, displaying great licentiousness and lawlessness.

And so, by abusing the freedom he enjoyed while staying among them, he deeply offended many of the Achaeans, and especially the respectable men among them.

Yet because war hemmed them in on every side, they were compelled to endure and bear what was against their nature.

No previous ruler, I think, possessed greater natural advantages for kingship than Philip, nor greater faults than this same king.

And it seems to me that his good qualities belonged to him by nature, while his bad ones came upon him as he advanced in age, just as some horses acquire faults as they grow old.

Though I do not, like other historians, put such judgments in prefaces,

but rather fit the appropriate remarks to the events themselves whenever occasion calls and pronounce on kings and notable men there, because I think that is the more suitable method both for writer and reader.

# Book 10 Chapter 27: Medea and the Palace at Ecbatana

Media, then, is among the most important of the Asiatic kingdoms both in the size of its territory and in the number and quality of its men, and likewise of its horses.

For this country supplies almost all Asia with these animals, and the royal breeding establishments for horses are entrusted to the Medes because of the natural excellence of the land.

It is surrounded by Greek cities founded at Alexander's direction, to guard it against the neighboring barbarians, with the exception of Ecbatana.

This city is built in the northern parts of Media, opposite those regions of Asia that face the Maeotis and the Euxine.

It had originally been the royal residence of the Medes, and in wealth and magnificence of construction it seems to have greatly surpassed the other cities.

It lies under the foothills by Mount Orontes and has no wall, but within it possesses an artificial citadel, built with remarkable strength for defense.

Below this stands the royal palace, about which both speaking in detail and passing over in silence present certain difficulties.

For to those who like to produce astonishing narratives and are accustomed to describing things with amplification and show, the city offers a splendid subject; but to those who approach with caution anything that goes beyond common belief, it causes hesitation and embarrassment.

Still, the palace is in size almost seven stades in circuit, and in the costly splendor of its individual structures shows clearly how rich its original founders were.

For since all the timber was cedar and cypress, none of it was left bare: the beams, the ceiling-panels, and the columns in the porticoes and peristyles were sheathed, some with silver plates and others with gold, and all the roof-tiles were of silver.

Most of these had been stripped off during Alexander's invasion with the Macedonians, and the rest under the rule of Antigonus and Seleucus Nicator.

Yet even when Antiochus arrived, the temple itself, called that of Aene, still had the surrounding columns gilded, and a number of silver tiles were piled up within it; a few gold bricks remained, and more of silver.

From all these objects the coined royal money that was struck came to a sum little short of four thousand talents.

# Book 10 Chapter 28: Antiochus the Great In Media

Arsaces expected that Antiochus would come as far as these regions, but would not dare to push across the desert adjoining them with so large an army, especially because of the lack of water.

For no water appears on the surface in the district just mentioned, but there are many underground channels with well-shafts sunk through the desert, places unknown to those who lack experience of the country.

The true account handed down by the natives about these works is that in the days when the Persians ruled Asia, they granted to those who brought spring water into districts previously unirrigated the right to enjoy the land for five generations.

So, since Taurus sends down many large streams, men undertook every expense and hardship and from great distances built these underground channels, with the result that even in our own day those who use the water do not know where the channels originally receive their flow.

But when Arsaces saw that Antiochus was determined on the march through the desert, he then tried to fill in and destroy the wells.

The king, once this had been reported to him, sent out Nicomedes again with a thousand horse. They found that Arsaces had withdrawn with the main force, though some cavalrymen were destroying the mouths of the channels. Charging them at once, they routed them and forced them to flee, then returned to Antiochus.

The king crossed the desert and came to the city called Hecatompylos, which lies in the middle of Parthia; and because the roads leading to all the surrounding regions meet there, the place has received its name from that fact.
