# Book 30 Chapter 1: Attalus Comes to Rome

Olympiad 153, year 1. Affairs in Italy.

At this time Attalus, the brother of King Eumenes, came from him to Rome.

His stated reason was that, even if the Galatian disturbance in the kingdom had not occurred, he would still have come to Rome to congratulate the Senate and receive some public mark of distinction for having fought on their side and shared all their dangers in a friendly spirit.

But as things stood, the Galatian crisis also forced him to come to Rome at that moment.

Since everyone received him graciously, both because of the familiarity formed during the campaign and because he seemed to be well disposed toward them, and since the welcome he met with was greater than he expected, his hopes rose high, because he did not know the true reason for his reception.

So he came close to ruining his own position and the whole kingdom.

For most Romans had become estranged from King Eumenes and his goodwill, and were convinced that during the war he had played a crooked game, talking with Perseus and watching for opportunities against them.

Therefore some of the prominent men took Attalus in hand and urged him to put aside the embassy on his brother's behalf and speak instead about his own interests.

For, they said, the Senate wished to establish for him a separate power and kingship because of its estrangement from his brother.

As a result Attalus was greatly elated and in private conversations gave assent to those who were pushing him in that direction.

At last he even came to an understanding with certain men of standing that he would enter the Senate and speak on these matters.

# Book 30 Chapter 2: Stratius Sent to Attalus

With opinion at Rome standing this way about Attalus, the king, foreseeing what was coming, sent Stratius the physician to Rome, a man in whom he had the greatest confidence,

showing him some things and instructing him in others to use every device to keep Attalus from following those who wanted to destroy their kingdom.

When Stratius arrived in Rome and got Attalus into his hands, he used many different arguments, for he was a man of good sense and persuasive power.

But only with difficulty did he accomplish his purpose and call Attalus back from his irrational impulse, setting before his eyes the fact that for the present he shared the kingship with his brother, differing from him only in not wearing a diadem and not being styled king, while in every other respect he possessed equal and identical authority.

And for the future he was universally acknowledged as heir to the throne, with that hope not far off, since the king, because of bodily weakness, was always expecting death, and because he had no children could not leave the realm to anyone else even if he wished.

For no natural son had yet been recognized by him, the one who later succeeded to the throne.

Above all, he said, he was astonished that Attalus was doing such harm to the needs of the present moment.

They ought to give great thanks to all the gods if, by standing together and acting with one mind, they could drive off the Galatian danger and the threat now hanging over them.

But if he should now fall into faction and quarrel with his brother, it was obvious that he would bring down the kingdom and deprive himself both of his present authority and of his future hope, while also depriving his brothers of their rule and influence within it.

By pressing these points and others like them, Stratius persuaded Attalus to remain with the existing arrangement.

# Book 30 Chapter 3: Attalus At Rome

Accordingly, when the man already mentioned entered the Senate, he congratulated it on what had happened and defended the goodwill and zeal he had personally shown in the war against Perseus.

In the same way he also urged at length that envoys should be sent to check the Galatians' recklessness and restore them to their former disposition.

He also spoke about Aenus and the city of Maronea, asking that these should be granted to him as a gift.

But he kept silent to the end about any attack on the king and about dividing the kingdom.

The Senate, however, supposing that he would later approach them privately on these questions, promised to send the envoys, honored him lavishly with the customary gifts, and also promised that it would give him the cities just mentioned.

But when, after immediately receiving these courtesies, he set out from Rome without doing any of the things they expected, the Senate, disappointed in its hopes, had nothing else it could do.

So, while he was still in Italy, it declared Aenus and Maronea free cities, thereby withdrawing its promise, and sent Publius Licinius and his colleagues as envoys to the Gauls.

What instructions it gave them is not easy to say, but it is not hard to infer them from what happened afterward.

This will become clear from their actions.

# Book 30 Chapter 4: Rhodian Ambassadors Argue Against War

Envoys also came from Rhodes, first the party led by Philocrates, and after them those led by Philophron and Astymedes.

For when the Rhodians received the answer that Agesipolis and his colleagues had obtained immediately after the battle, and saw from it the Senate's anger and threatening posture toward them, they at once sent the embassies just mentioned.

But Astymedes and Philophron, perceiving from their interviews, both public and private, the suspicion and estrangement people felt toward them, fell into complete discouragement and helpless distress.

And when one of the praetors mounted the Rostra and called on the crowd to make war on Rhodes,

then indeed, utterly losing their composure because of the danger threatening their country, they went so far as to put on dark clothing and in their appeals no longer tried to persuade or make demands of their friends, but begged them with tears not to decide on any irreparable measure against them.

Some days later Antony the tribune introduced them, the same man who had dragged down from the Rostra the praetor who was urging war; and Philophron spoke first, followed by Astymedes.

Then, after uttering what the proverb calls a swan-song, they received such an answer that they seemed released from their total fear of war,

but regarding the specific charges against them the Senate rebuked them harshly and bitterly.

The sense of the reply was this: if it were not for a few men who were their own friends, and especially for these envoys themselves, the Senate well knew how it ought, justly, to deal with them.

Astymedes thought that he had spoken well on behalf of his country, but he pleased neither the Greeks then staying at Rome nor those living at home.

For afterward he published in writing the speech he had composed in defense of Rhodes, and to most people who took it in hand it seemed bizarre and altogether unconvincing.

For he framed his defense not only from the just claims of his own country, but still more from accusations against others.

By setting benefits and services side by side and comparing them, he tried to make those of others seem false and small, while he magnified those of the Rhodians as much as he could.

And conversely, the faults of others he reproached bitterly and maliciously, while those of the Rhodians he tried to cover over, so that by the comparison their own might appear slight and deserving of pardon, whereas those of their neighbors might appear grave and wholly beyond excuse, though, as he said, all who had gone wrong had already obtained pardon.

This kind of defense, however, would never seem fitting for a statesman.

Even in cases where men have acted together in certain secret matters, we do not praise those who, out of fear or for profit, turn informer against their accomplices; rather,

we approve those who accept every torture and punishment and do not make any of their fellow-plotters share the same disaster. These are the men we accept; these are the men we consider noble. But the man who, because of some vague fear, lays all other people's errors before those in power and revives matters that time was carrying off into forgetfulness among the rulers—how could he fail to offend all who hear of it?
