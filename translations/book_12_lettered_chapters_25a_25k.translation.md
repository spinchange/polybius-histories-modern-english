# Book 12 Chapter 25a: Chapter 25a

What Polybius of Megalopolis says about Timaeus. Just as the proverb has it that a single drop from the largest vessel is enough to tell us the character of the whole contents, so we must judge in the matter now before us.

For once one or two falsehoods are found in a man's writings, and these turn out to have been deliberate, it is clear that nothing said by such a writer can any longer be regarded as certain or reliable.

But in order to convince even those who are more inclined to take his part, I must speak about his method and the pains he takes with his public speeches and exhortations, and further with the speeches of ambassadors—in short, with everything of that kind, which are, as it were, the summary headings of events and hold the whole history together.

For that Timaeus has set these down in his account contrary to the truth, and has done so on purpose, what reader can fail to notice?

He has not written what was actually said, nor reported it as it really was said. Instead, having first decided how it ought to have been said, he reels off all the supposed speeches and the circumstances attending the events as a man might do who, in a rhetorical exercise, were attempting to argue a set theme *** as though he were making a display of his own ability, rather than giving a true account of what was said. —

# Book 12 Chapter 25b: Chapter 25b

It is the special property of history first to learn the words that were actually spoken, whatever they may have been, and second to find out the reason why the action or speech in question either failed or succeeded.

For the bare statement of what happened, while it may entertain us, is of no profit; but when the reason is added, the study of history becomes fruitful.

For when we transfer parallels from the past to our own circumstances, we gain starting points and grounds for forming judgments about what is to come, so that we may at one time take precaution and at another, imitating what has gone before, set about facing present dangers with greater confidence.

But the man who passes over in silence both the words that were spoken and the reason for them, and instead gives false arguments and long-winded speeches in their place, destroys the very thing that is proper to history. This is exactly what Timaeus does, and we all know that his books are full of this kind of thing.

# Book 12 Chapter 25c: Chapter 25c

One might perhaps be puzzled how a man of the character we are describing has won such acceptance and credit with some readers.

The reason for this is that, since his work overflows with censure and abuse of others, he is judged not by his own work nor by his own statements, but by his attacks on his neighbors; and to this end he seems to me to have brought a quite exceptional industriousness and a natural talent.

Something very much like this happened in the case of Strato the natural philosopher too. For he likewise, whenever he sets out to pick apart and refute the opinions of others, is admirable; but whenever he advances something of his own and expounds one of his own ideas, he appears to those who know the subject far sillier and duller than himself.

And it seems to me that what happens with writers is exactly like what happens in our life as a whole.

For here too it is easy to find fault with one's neighbors, but hard to keep oneself free from error; and one might say, almost as a rule, that those who are quickest to censure their neighbors are the very ones who make the most mistakes in their own lives.

# Book 12 Chapter 25d: Chapter 25d

Besides what has already been described, something else applies in the case of Timaeus. Having settled in Athens for nearly fifty years and devoted himself to the writings of earlier authors, he supposed that he possessed the greatest resources for writing history—mistakenly, in my view.

For history has something in common with medicine, in that each of them, in its broadest divisions, falls into three parts; and it follows that the dispositions of those who take them up are likewise comparable.

Take medicine straightaway: one part of it is the theoretical, the next the dietetic, and the third the surgical and pharmaceutical, of the kind *** entirely. ... by misrepresenting the profession *

** Now the theoretical branch, which has its chief origin from Alexandria, among those called there the followers of Herophilus and Callimachus, occupies one part of medicine; but by its outward appearance and its grand professions it draws to itself such a reputation that no one else seems to have a grasp of the subject.

Yet when you bring these men to the test of reality and put a sick patient in their hands, they prove to be as far from being of use as those who have never read a medical treatise at all. Some patients, having entrusted themselves to such men on account of their fluency in argument, though there was nothing seriously wrong with them, have often put themselves in extreme danger.

For they are truly like men who navigate from a book. And yet these men, traveling about the cities with much show, when they have gathered crowds *** by reputation, bring those who have given true proof of themselves in actual practice into the utmost difficulty and contempt with their audience, since the plausibility of words often overcomes the verdict of experience on the actual facts.

But the third kind—the man who brings to each profession a genuine, practiced competence—is not only rare, but is also often overshadowed by glibness and audacity, owing to the lack of judgment in the many.

# Book 12 Chapter 25e: Chapter 25e

In the very same way, practical history too is threefold. One of its parts concerns the diligent study of documents and the comparison of the material drawn from them; another concerns the survey of cities and places, of rivers and harbors, and in general of the distinctive features and distances by land and by sea; and the third concerns political affairs.

And in the same manner many set their hearts on history because of the reputation it has previously enjoyed, yet most of those who take up the task bring to it nothing that does them credit—nothing but facility, audacity, and unscrupulous workmanship.

Like the quack drug-sellers, they court popularity and always say what will please the moment, in order to make a living by this means; and they are not worth saying more about.

But some of those who seem to approach history on reasonable grounds, like the theoretical physicians, spend their time in libraries and acquire in general a wide range of experience from the documents, and so persuade themselves that they are competent for the task, and seem to outsiders to be adequately equipped—yet this is only one part, as it seems to me, of practical history.

For it is true that the examination of earlier records is useful for knowing the views of the ancients and the conceptions they once held about situations, places, peoples, and constitutions and actions, and further for understanding the circumstances and fortunes that each people experienced in earlier times.

For the past fittingly draws our attention toward the future, provided one inquires truthfully into what is past in each case.

But to be persuaded—as Timaeus is—that, starting from this capacity alone, one is equipped to write well the history of later events is utterly foolish, and much as if a man who had looked at the works of the ancient painters should think himself a competent painter and a master of the art.

# Book 12 Chapter 25f: Chapter 25f

What I am saying will become still clearer from what follows—for instance, straightaway from what happens to Ephorus in certain passages of his history. For in military matters he seems to me to have some notion of operations at sea, but to be entirely without experience of land engagements.

Accordingly, when one fixes attention on the sea-battles off Cyprus and off Cnidus, which the King's generals fought against Evagoras of Salamis and again against the Spartans, it is reasonable to admire the writer both for his ability and for his expertise, and to carry away much that is useful for similar situations.

But when he describes the battle of Leuctra between the Thebans and the Spartans, or again that of Mantinea between these same peoples, in which Epaminondas lost his life, then, if one pays close attention to the details and studies the formations and maneuvers made at the very crises of the action, he appears ridiculous and altogether inexperienced and ignorant of such matters.

Now the engagement at Leuctra, which was straightforward and fought with the army in a single body, does not make the writer's inexperience very conspicuous; but the one at Mantinea has, in his presentation, a varied and generalship-like appearance, yet is untenable and entirely beyond the writer's grasp. This will become clear

if one accurately lays out the ground and measures against it the movements he describes.

The same thing happens with Theopompus too, and most of all with Timaeus, who is our present subject.

For wherever such writers treat their subject in summary outline, they pass undetected; but wherever they choose to elaborate and demonstrate some point in detail, they show themselves to be exactly such men as Ephorus. —

# Book 12 Chapter 25g: Chapter 25g

That it is impossible to write well about events in war for the man who has no experience of military operations, or about affairs in the various states for the man who has had no practical experience of such actions and circumstances.

It follows that, when nothing is written either with experience or vividly by these bookish authors, the work proves to be of no practical use to its readers. For if one takes out of history the element that can benefit us, what is left of it becomes utterly worthless and useless.

Furthermore, when men set out to write in detail about cities and places while being untrained in such experience, it clearly must follow that the like result occurs: they leave out many things worthy of mention, and make much of many things not worth it. This happens most of all to Timaeus, owing to his want of direct observation.

# Book 12 Chapter 25h: Chapter 25h

Timaeus says, in his thirty-fourth book: "Having spent fifty unbroken years living as a foreigner in Athens, I admittedly had no experience of warfare, nor, moreover, of the firsthand inspection of places."

Consequently, whenever in his history he comes upon one of these subjects, he is ignorant of much and falsifies much; and even when he occasionally touches the truth, he is like those painters who make their sketches from stuffed dummies.

For with them too the outer outline is sometimes preserved, but the vividness and the energy of living creatures is absent—which is precisely what is proper to the art of painting. And the same thing happens with Timaeus and in general with those who set out from this bookish habit.

For the vivid impression of events is absent from them, because this comes only from the writer's own firsthand experience; and so those who have not themselves gone through the events do not implant in their hearers any genuine enthusiasm.

It is for this reason that our predecessors thought such vivid touches ought to be present in their accounts; so that, when the discussion was about political affairs, they would add that of necessity the writer had taken part in public life and had experience of what happens in that sphere; and when it was about military affairs, that he had likewise served in campaigns and faced danger; and again, when about private life, that he had raised children and lived with a wife. And the like for the other departments of life.

This is reasonably to be found only among those writers who have themselves gone through the events and have made this department of history their own. To be, as it were, an active doer and performer of everything is perhaps difficult; but of the greatest and most general matters it is essential.

# Book 12 Chapter 25i: Chapter 25i

That what I say is not impossible, the poet is sufficient proof to win belief, for in him one may see this vividness present in abundance.

From all this everyone would reasonably agree that the diligent study of documents is the third part of history and holds the third rank.

That what I am now saying is true would become most evident in the case of the deliberative and hortatory speeches, and further the speeches of ambassadors, which Timaeus employs.

For few occasions admit of presenting all the arguments available, while most admit only a few of those at hand—and of these, some are used by the men of today, others by those of former times; and some the Aetolians put forward, some the Peloponnesians, and some the Athenians.

Now to run through all the available arguments idly and out of season, applied to everything and to everyone—which is what Timaeus does, contriving arguments for every subject—is utterly untruthful, juvenile, and fit only for the lecture-room; and indeed this has already been the cause of failure and contempt for many. But to choose always the fitting and timely arguments—that is essential.

And since the application is variable—both how many and what kind of the available arguments one should use—a rather different sort of practice and rule is needed if we are not to harm but to benefit our readers.

Now the right occasion is in all cases hard to teach by rule, yet it is not impossible to be brought within the range of intelligent guess through observations drawn from firsthand experience and practice; and in the present case what I am saying may best be inferred from the following.

For if writers, having indicated the occasions and the impulses and dispositions of those deliberating, and then having set out the speeches actually delivered, were to make clear to us the reasons why the speakers either hit the mark or missed it, we would arrive at some true understanding of the matter, and would be able—at once by discriminating and by transferring the parallel to similar cases—always to hit the mark in the questions before us.

But the trouble is, I think, that to explain causes is hard, while to coin speeches in books is easy; and to say a few words to the point, and to find the rule for this, is within reach of few, whereas to spin out a great deal of empty matter lying ready to hand is common and easy.
