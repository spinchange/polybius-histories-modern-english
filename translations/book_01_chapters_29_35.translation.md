# Book 1 Chapter 29: The Siege of Aspis

After this the Romans again took on fresh supplies, fitted out the captured ships, and gave the crews the care their success deserved. Then they put to sea and made their voyage toward Libya.

Their leading ships came to land below the headland called Hermaeum, which lies at the mouth of the whole gulf by Carthage and projects out into the open sea toward Sicily. There they waited for the ships sailing up behind them, and when they had gathered the whole fleet together they sailed along the coast until they reached the city called Aspis.

There they landed, hauled the ships ashore, and enclosed them with a trench and palisade. Since the people holding the city would not submit willingly, they began to besiege it.

The Carthaginians who had escaped the danger of the sea battle sailed back as well. Convinced that the enemy, elated by the advantage they had won, would immediately sail against Carthage itself, they stationed their land and naval forces at the points lying in front of the city.

But when they learned that the Romans had disembarked safely and were besieging Aspis, they gave up trying to watch for an attack by sea and instead concentrated their forces and busied themselves with guarding both the city and the countryside.

The Romans, after taking Aspis and leaving a garrison to hold the city and its district, also sent envoys to Rome to report what had happened and to ask what they should do next and how they should handle the situation. After this they broke camp with all their forces in eager haste and set out to ravage the country.

Since no one opposed them, they destroyed many country houses built with exceptional luxury, seized a great quantity of livestock, and carried off to the ships more than twenty thousand people.

Meanwhile messengers arrived from Rome explaining that one of the commanders must remain with an adequate force, while the other was to bring the fleet back to Rome.

So Marcus remained behind with forty ships, fifteen thousand infantry, and five hundred cavalry.

Lucius took the crews and the mass of prisoners aboard, sailed safely along the coast of Sicily, and arrived at Rome.

# Book 1 Chapter 30: Regulus in Africa

The Carthaginians, seeing that the enemy intended to remain in the country for a considerable time, first chose two generals from among themselves, Hasdrubal son of Hanno and Bostar, and then sent to Hamilcar at Heraclea, summoning him to come with all speed.

He took with him five hundred cavalry and five thousand infantry and came to Carthage. Having been appointed a third general, he consulted with Hasdrubal and the others about how they should deal with the present situation.

They decided to come to the aid of the countryside and not to stand by while it was plundered with impunity.

A few days later Marcus advanced, seizing by assault and looting the strongholds that were unwalled, and laying siege to those that were fortified.

When he came to a notable city called Adys, he encamped around it and energetically set about siege works and an assault.

The Carthaginians, eager to relieve the city and judging that they must contest control of the open country, led out their army.

They occupied a hill that stood above the enemy, but it was unsuitable for their own forces, and there they pitched camp.

Though they placed their greatest hopes in their cavalry and elephants, they abandoned the flat ground and shut themselves up in steep and difficult terrain. In doing so, they were about to teach the enemy exactly what ought to be done against them. And that is exactly what happened.

For the Roman commanders, recognizing with practical good sense that the most effective and most formidable part of the enemy's strength had been rendered useless by the ground, did not wait for them to come down into the plain and draw up for battle.

Instead, taking advantage of the opportunity before them, they advanced at daybreak from both sides against the hill.

The Carthaginian cavalry and elephants were therefore completely useless. The mercenaries, however, came out bravely and with great spirit, and at first forced one Roman division to give ground and flee.

But when they pushed too far forward and were surrounded by the troops ascending from the other side, they were routed, and after that the whole army was immediately driven out of the camp.

The elephants and cavalry, as soon as they reached level ground, made their withdrawal safely.

The Romans pursued the infantry only a short distance, then plundered the camp, and after that moved through the whole countryside and sacked the cities without resistance.

They also gained possession of the city called Tunes. Since it was well suited for the operations before them and was conveniently placed both with regard to Carthage itself and the neighboring countryside, they encamped there.

# Book 1 Chapter 31: Distress at Carthage

The Carthaginians, after suffering a setback at sea not long before and now another on land—not because of any cowardice among the rank and file, but because of the incompetence of their leaders—had fallen into difficulties of every kind.

At the same time the nation of the Numidians was attacking them as well, and was doing the country damage no less than the Romans, indeed even greater damage.

As a result, because the people from the countryside fled in fear and crowded into the city, there was complete discouragement and famine: discouragement because of the panic, and famine partly because of the numbers gathered there and partly because they expected a siege.

Marcus, seeing that the Carthaginians had failed both by land and by sea and thinking that he would very soon take the city, but anxious that the commander coming from Rome might arrive first and take the credit for the whole achievement, invited the Carthaginians to negotiate a settlement.

They gladly listened and sent out their leading men. But when they met with him, they were so far from inclining toward any of his proposals that they could hardly bear even to listen to the weight of his demands.

For Marcus, as though he already had complete control of everything, thought that whatever concessions he granted ought to be accepted by them as a favor and an act of generosity.

But the Carthaginians saw that even if they became his captives, nothing harsher could follow than the commands he was now imposing. So they not only came back rejecting the proposals, but were also deeply offended by Marcus's severity.

When the Carthaginian council heard what the Roman general was demanding, although it had almost given up hope of safety, it nevertheless took so manly and noble a stand that it chose to endure anything and to try every action and every opportunity rather than submit to anything dishonorable or unworthy of its past deeds.

# Book 1 Chapter 32: Xanthippus of Sparta

About this time one of the recruiting officers previously sent to Greece sailed into Carthage, bringing a large number of soldiers, among them a certain Xanthippus, a Lacedaemonian, a man trained in the Spartan discipline and possessed of solid experience in warfare.

When he heard about the defeat, how it had happened, and in what way, and when he considered the rest of the Carthaginian resources as well as the number of their cavalry and elephants, he quickly concluded and told his friends that the Carthaginians were being beaten not by the Romans but by themselves, through the inexperience of their commanders.

Because the situation was critical, what Xanthippus said was soon spread among the crowd and among the generals, and the men in authority decided to summon him and test what he could do.

When he appeared before them, he explained to the magistrates why they had now met with defeat, and showed that if they followed his advice and made use of flat ground in marching, encamping, and drawing up for battle, they would easily be able both to secure their own safety and to defeat the enemy.

The generals accepted what he said, were persuaded by him, and immediately entrusted the forces to him.

Even before this, there had already been among the masses an encouraging murmur and hopeful talk based on what Xanthippus was saying.

But once he led the army out before the city, drew it up in proper order, began to maneuver some units in formation, and gave commands according to military rules, he showed such a contrast to the incompetence of the previous generals that the multitude responded with loud cheers and were eager to engage the enemy at once, convinced that so long as Xanthippus led them, they would suffer nothing disastrous.

When the generals saw that the spirits of the masses had been restored so unexpectedly, they addressed them in a way suited to the moment, and after a few days set out with the army.

Their force consisted of twelve thousand infantry, four thousand cavalry, and nearly a hundred elephants.

The Romans, seeing that the Carthaginians were now making their marches through level country and placing their camps on flat ground, were puzzled and disturbed by this new development, but all the same they were eager to come to close quarters with the enemy.

After advancing, they spent the first day encamped about ten stades from their opponents.

On the next day the Carthaginian leaders were considering what should be done and how they ought to act in the present circumstances.

But the rank and file, full of eagerness for battle, gathered in groups and shouted Xanthippus's name one by one, convinced that he ought to lead them out at once.

The generals, seeing the army's rush of enthusiasm and eagerness, and with Xanthippus also insisting that they must not let the moment pass, ordered the troops to prepare themselves and gave Xanthippus authority to manage affairs as he thought best.

Once he had this power, he brought out the elephants and stationed them in a single line at the front before the whole army. Behind them, at a suitable distance, he placed the Carthaginian phalanx.

Of the mercenaries, he posted some on the right wing, while he placed the most mobile troops together with the cavalry in front of each wing.

When the Romans saw the enemy drawing up for battle, they advanced out readily to meet them.

Alarmed and anticipating the charge of the elephants, they put the light-armed troops in front and behind them stationed the maniples in many successive lines, while they divided the cavalry between the two wings.

By making their whole formation narrower than before but deeper, they had taken reasonable measures against the elephants, but against the cavalry—who greatly outnumbered their own—they had completely misjudged the situation.

When both sides had arranged, both generally and in detail, each part in the positions appropriate to it, they stood in order watching for the moment to attack one another.

# Book 1 Chapter 34: Roman Defeat

As soon as Xanthippus gave the order to the men on the elephants to advance and break up the enemy's line, and to the cavalry on each wing to wheel around and attack the enemy,

then the Roman army too, according to their custom, clashed their weapons together, raised their battle cry, and charged the enemy.

The Roman cavalry on both wings quickly fled, because the Carthaginian cavalry were so much more numerous.

Of the infantry, those posted on the left wing, partly avoiding the elephants' charge and partly despising the mercenaries, attacked the Carthaginian right. After routing them, they pressed on and pursued them as far as the camp rampart.

But among those stationed opposite the elephants, the front ranks, once engaged, were forced back and trampled underfoot by the sheer power of the animals, and perished in heaps where they stood. Still, because of the depth of the formation behind them, the main body remained unbroken for some time.

But when the men in the rear ranks found themselves surrounded on every side by cavalry and were forced to turn and fight them, while those who had forced their way through the elephants to the front, with the beasts now behind them, ran into the Carthaginian phalanx still intact and in good order, they were cut down.

Then, hard pressed from every side, most of the Romans were trampled to death by the overwhelming force of the animals, while the rest were shot down by the mass of cavalry on the very ground of the battle line. Only a very few broke away and tried to flee.

But since the routes of retreat lay over open plain, some of these too were killed by the elephants and cavalry, and perhaps five hundred who fled with the general Marcus were soon overtaken and captured alive together with him.

On the Carthaginian side about eight hundred mercenaries fell, namely those posted opposite the Roman left.

Of the Romans, about two thousand survived—those who, by pursuing the troops mentioned above, had stayed outside the main danger. All the rest perished except the general Marcus and those who fled with him.

The Roman units that escaped made their way with difficulty back to Aspis.

The Carthaginians stripped the dead, took the general along with the prisoners, and returned to the city overjoyed at the turn events had taken.

# Book 1 Chapter 35: Lessons from the Event

At this point a thoughtful observer could rightly note many things that contribute to the correction of human life.

For the need not to trust in Fortune—especially in times of success—was then made perfectly clear to all by what happened to Marcus.

The man who a little earlier had shown no pity or indulgence to the fallen was himself at once led away to beg those very things for his own safety.

And indeed the old saying of Euripides, that one wise plan overcomes many hands, was then confirmed by the facts themselves.

For one man and one mind brought down forces that seemed unconquerable and effective in action, and led back to a better state a government that appeared plainly ruined beyond recovery, along with the utterly disheartened spirits of its armies.

I have mentioned these things for the sake of improving those who read these memoirs.

For there are two ways by which all people may change for the better: through their own misfortunes and through those of others. The lesson drawn from one's own experience is more vivid, but the lesson drawn from other people's is safer.

Therefore one should never deliberately choose the first, since it brings correction only through great suffering and danger, but one should always seek the second, since through it one may perceive the better course without harm.

From this one ought to conclude that the experience gained from practical history is the finest education for real life.

For history alone, without causing us injury, produces true judges of what is better in every situation and circumstance. Let this be enough on these matters.
