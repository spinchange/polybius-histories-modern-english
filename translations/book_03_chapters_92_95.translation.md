# Book 3 Chapter 92: Hannibal Selects Winter Quarters

Reasoning along these lines, then, Hannibal marched out of Samnite territory through the pass by the hill called Eribianus and encamped beside the river Vulturnus, which divides the plains just described into roughly two halves.

He kept his camp on the side facing Rome, and with his foraging parties he ranged over the whole plain and plundered it without hindrance.

Fabius was thoroughly alarmed at the enemy's daring stroke, but for that very reason he held all the more firmly to the course he had decided upon.

His colleague Marcus, however, along with all the military tribunes and company commanders in the army, believed they had caught the enemy in an excellent position, and thought they ought to press on, close with them on the plain, and not stand by while the most celebrated part of the country was being ravaged.

Fabius pressed forward only as far as reaching the area, going along with those who were eager and ready to take risks,

but once he had drawn near to Falernum, he showed himself along the lower slopes and kept pace with the enemy, so as not to seem to his own allies to be surrendering the open country.

He would not, however, bring his army down into the plain, taking care to avoid an all-out engagement, both for the reasons already mentioned and because the enemy was plainly far superior in cavalry.

As for Hannibal, when he had tested the enemy and ravaged the entire plain, gathering an immense quantity of plunder,

he set about breaking camp, intending not to squander the booty but to deposit it in a place where he could also pass the winter, so that the army might have not merely a present feast but a steady abundance of supplies.

Fabius understood his plan—that he was preparing to make his return by the same route by which he had entered—and seeing that the ground was narrow and exceptionally well suited for an attack,

he stationed about four thousand men at the defile itself, urging them to use their courage at the right moment together with the natural advantage of the terrain, while he himself, with the greater part of his force, took up a position on a commanding hill in front of the narrows.

...he encamped. When the Carthaginians arrived and pitched their camp on the level ground right at the foot of the slope, he expected to carry off their plunder without a fight, and even, in all likelihood, to put an end to the whole affair, thanks to the favorable lie of the land.

So he was occupied with these deliberations and bent upon them, considering where and how he should make use of the terrain, and which men were to begin the attack on the enemy, and from what quarter.

But Hannibal, while the enemy was making these preparations for the coming day, reasoned out the likely course of events and gave them no time or opportunity to carry out their designs.

He summoned Hasdrubal, the officer in charge of the work details, and ordered him to bundle together torches from dry brushwood of every kind, as many as possible and with all speed, and to pick out from the whole of the plunder the sturdiest of the draft oxen, about two thousand of them, and collect them in front of the camp.

When this was done, he gathered the work crews together and pointed out to them a certain ridge lying between his own camp and the narrows through which he intended to march; toward this ridge he ordered them to drive the oxen hard and by force, once the order was given, until they reached the heights.

After this he told them all to take their supper and rest at the proper hour.

As soon as the third part of the night had passed, he at once led out the work crews and ordered them to fasten the torches to the oxen's horns.

This was quickly done because of their numbers, and he gave the order to light them all and directed the men to drive the oxen forward and force them up toward the ridge tops,

while he posted the spearmen behind them, telling them to help the drivers up to a certain point, but, once the animals had taken their first headlong rush, to run out along the flanks and, raising a din, to make for the high ground and seize the ridge tops in advance, so that they might lend support and engage the enemy if they should meet any at the heights.

# Book 3 Chapter 94: Fabius Returns to Rome

...he came to the narrows and the defiles. As for the Romans guarding the narrows, the moment they saw the lights advancing toward the heights, they supposed Hannibal was making his push that way, and so they abandoned the difficult ground and hurried to the help of the heights.

But as they drew near the oxen they were thrown into confusion by the lights, imagining and expecting something greater and more terrible than what was actually happening. Then, when the Carthaginian spearmen arrived,

after the two sides had exchanged a few long-range shots, with the oxen now charging in among them, both parties drew apart and held to the heights, waiting expectantly for the coming of day, since they could not make out what was going on.

Fabius, for his part, was partly baffled at what was taking place and, in the poet's words, "suspected it was some trick," and partly, in keeping with his original resolve, by no means judged it right to throw the dice or stake everything on a general engagement; so he kept quiet within his palisade and waited for daylight.

Meanwhile Hannibal, his plan succeeding as he had intended, brought his force safely through the narrows along with the booty, since those guarding the difficult ground had abandoned their posts.

At daybreak, observing the men stationed on the heights facing his spearmen, he sent over some of the Iberians, who closed with the Romans, cut down about a thousand of them, and easily relieved their own light troops and brought them down.

So Hannibal, having made his exit from Falernum in this fashion, now camped in safety and proceeded to survey and plan where and how he should pass the winter, having instilled great fear and much bewilderment in the cities and peoples of Italy.

Fabius, meanwhile, was spoken of badly by the masses, on the grounds that he had unmanfully let the enemy slip away from such ground; yet he did not abandon his purpose.

And being compelled, a few days later, to go to Rome for certain sacrifices, he handed the legions over to his colleague, and as he departed he charged him repeatedly not to be as eager to harm the enemy as to see that his own men suffered no serious hurt.

But Marcus took not the slightest of this to heart, and even while Fabius was still speaking he was wholly and utterly bent on hazarding battle and risking all.

# Book 3 Chapter 95: Hasdrubal Equips a Fleet

Such, then, was the situation in Italy.

Around the same time as the events just described, Hasdrubal, the general appointed over Iberia, had during the winter refitted the thirty ships left him by his brother and manned ten more; and at the beginning of summer he put to sea from New Carthage with forty decked vessels, having appointed Hamilcar admiral of the fleet.

At the same time he assembled his land force out of its winter quarters and set off: with the ships he made his voyage close along the shore, and with the infantry his march along the beach, hastening to bring both forces together at the river Ebro.

Gnaeus, reckoning up the designs of the Carthaginians, at first intended to come out of his winter quarters and meet them both by land and by sea.

But on hearing of the size of their forces and the scale of their preparations, he gave up the idea of meeting them by land; instead he manned thirty-five ships and, taking from his land army the men best suited for service as marines, he put to sea and on the second day made the run from Tarraco to the region of the river Ebro.

Coming to anchor about eighty stades from the enemy, he sent ahead two fast-sailing Massiliot ships to reconnoiter; for these men took the lead and the first risks, and rendered every service to the Romans without stint.

For the people of Massilia, if any others ever did, nobly shared in the fortunes of Rome—often on later occasions, but most of all during the war with Hannibal.
