# Book 9 Chapter 13: Scientific Strategy

Success demands that everything be adequately provided for; for that reason commanders must neglect nothing in undertakings of this kind.

The first principle of all is silence: a man must not, whether from delight at some unexpected hope, or from fear, or from familiarity, or from affection, share the plan with anyone outside the circle,

but only with those without whom the design cannot be brought to completion—and not even with them before the moment when the need for each person's part compels disclosure.

And one must keep silence not only with the tongue, but even more with the mind.

For many men, though they concealed their words, have betrayed their designs by their very expression, or even by the actions they took.

The second requirement is to know the capacities of marches by day and by night, and the distances that can be covered in each, not only on land but also at sea.

Third, and most important, one must understand the opportunities provided by the seasons and be able to adapt the plan rightly to them.

Nor should the place of action be treated as a small matter, since it often happens that because of the ground what seems impossible becomes possible, while what seemed possible turns out impossible.

Finally, one must not neglect signals and counter-signals, or the choice of the persons through whom and with whom the decided action is to be carried out.

# Book 9 Chapter 14: Generals Also Need to Know Science

Of the matters just mentioned, some are learned through practice, some from history, and some by systematic experience.

Best of all is for the commander himself to know the roads, the place to which he must come, the nature of the ground, and in addition the men through whom and with whom he is going to act.

Second best is to make careful inquiry and not trust random people; indeed, the reliability of guides in such matters should always be secured in some definite way.

These things, and others like them, perhaps can be acquired by commanders through military practice itself, some by direct experience and some by history.

But what comes from scientific knowledge requires instruction and principles, especially from astronomy and geometry. The actual labor of these sciences is not great for the purpose in view, yet their value is great and they can contribute greatly to the operations I have described.

Their most necessary use lies in reckoning night and day. If those periods were always equal, the matter would require no study and the knowledge would be common to all.

But since they differ not only from each other, but also vary in themselves, it is clearly necessary to know the increase and decrease of each.

For how can anyone correctly estimate a march and the distance possible by day or by night unless he has understood those differences?

And indeed it is impossible to arrive at the proper moment without such knowledge; one must otherwise either be late or arrive too early.

In these operations, arriving too early is an even greater mistake than being late.

For the man who passes beyond the appointed moment merely misses his opportunity, since he can learn from a distance what has happened and withdraw again safely.

But the man who comes too early, by approaching before the time and being noticed, not only fails in the enterprise but risks everything.

# Book 9 Chapter 15: Mode of Calculating Time

In all human actions the critical moment has power, but most of all in war.

Therefore a general ought to have ready at hand the summer and winter solstices, the equinoxes, and the intervals between them in which days and nights increase and diminish.

Only in this way can he properly measure the distances that can be covered, whether by land or by sea.

He must also know the divisions of time both by day and by night, so as to understand when reveille should be sounded and when a march should begin.

For no one can attain the end if he has failed to get the beginning right.

The times of day can be observed without difficulty from the shadow, from the sun's course, and from the distances it traverses across the sky.

Those of night are harder to judge unless one can follow, from what is visible, the arrangement and order of the twelve signs of the zodiac; yet even this is easy for those who have carefully studied the heavens.

For although nights are unequal in length, it nevertheless happens that in every night six of the twelve signs rise above the horizon. It is therefore clear that in corresponding parts of every night equal portions of the zodiac must rise.

And since the sun's position is known each day, it is clear that the sign lying opposite it must be the one rising.

What remains is that whatever part of the zodiac appears to have risen after that point indicates how much of the night has elapsed.

And once the signs of the zodiac are known both in number and in size, the particular divisions of the night also become known accordingly.

On cloudy nights one must pay attention to the moon, because by reason of its size its light is almost always visible wherever in the sky it may be.

Sometimes one must estimate from the times and places of its rising,

and sometimes again from those of its setting, provided one has enough understanding in this matter to keep pace with the daily changes in those risings.

For this reason one may praise the poet for representing Odysseus, that most capable of leaders, as drawing inferences from the stars not only for navigation, but also for operations on land.

For unexpected events, which cannot be fully anticipated by exact foresight, are enough by themselves to produce great and repeated difficulties,
