# Book 2 Chapter 15: Grain Production in Cisalpine Gaul

...of the ten thousand stades. As for the fertility of the land, it is not even easy to put into words. Grain is so abundant in these regions that in our own day the Sicilian medimnus of wheat has often gone for four obols, of barley for two, and the metretes of wine for the same price as the barley.

There is, moreover, an utterly overwhelming abundance of panic-grass and millet among them. And as for the quantity of acorns produced by the oak woods scattered at intervals across the plains, one may best judge it from this:

although a very great number of pigs are slaughtered in Italy, both for private households and for the provisioning of the armies, by far the fullest supply of them comes from these plains.

As to the cheapness and plenty of the various articles of food in detail, one could grasp it most precisely in this way:

travellers passing through the country take their lodging at the inns, and do not haggle over the individual provisions, but simply ask how much the host charges per person.

Now for the most part the innkeepers take in their guests—on the understanding that everything needful is provided—for half an as, which is the fourth part of an obol; and only rarely do they exceed this.

As for the numbers of the men, the size and beauty of their bodies, and beyond that their boldness in war, all this will be plain to learn from the events of their history themselves.

On each of the two flanks of the Alps—the one inclining toward the river Rhone, the other toward the plains already mentioned—the hilly, fertile regions are inhabited: those facing the Rhone and the north by Gauls called Transalpine, and those facing the plains by the Taurisci and the Agones and several other barbarian peoples.

The Transalpine are so named not from a difference of race but from a difference of location: for "trans," translated, means "beyond"; and so they call those who live on the far side of the Alps the Transalpine.

# Book 2 Chapter 16: Rivers and Mountains in Northern Italy

...of the snow that lies there always, [the peaks] are utterly uninhabitable. The Apennine, from its beginning above Massalia and its junction with the Alps, is inhabited by the Ligurians—both the flank of it inclining toward the Tyrrhenian Sea and the flank toward the plains—

along the sea as far as the city of Pisa, which lies first in Etruria toward the west, and inland as far as the territory of the Arretini.

Next come the Etruscans; and continuous with these, on both slopes of the mountains just mentioned, dwell the Umbrians.

After that the Apennine, keeping a distance of about five hundred stades from the Adriatic Sea, leaves the plains behind, bending off to the right, and running through the middle of the rest of Italy stretches down to the Sicilian Sea.

The remaining flat part of that flank runs down to the sea and the city of Sena.

The river Padus—celebrated by the poets as the Eridanus—has its sources in the Alps, toward the apex rather of the figure just described, and descends into the plains, making its course toward the south.

But when it reaches the level country, it turns aside with its stream toward the east and flows through the plains; it makes its outlet by two mouths into the region of the Adriatic, and the larger part of the plain country is cut off by it toward the Alps and the head of the Adriatic.

It carries a volume of water inferior to none of the rivers in Italy, because all the streams that flow down toward the plains from the Alps and the Apennine mountains fall into it from every side.

It runs with its greatest and most beautiful flow around the rising of the Dog Star, swollen by the mass of snows melting on the mountains just mentioned.

From the sea it is navigable upstream, by the mouth called Olana, for nearly two thousand stades.

For at first, from its sources, it has a single channel, but it divides into two parts at the place called Trigaboli; of these, the one mouth is named the Padoa, the other the Olana.

On the latter lies a harbour that offers anchorage as safe as any on the Adriatic to those who put in there. Among the local people the river is called the Bodencus.

As for the other tales told about this river among the Greeks—I mean the stories of Phaethon and his fall, and the tears of the poplars, and the black-clad people living along the river, who, they say, still to this day wear such garments out of mourning for Phaethon—

and indeed all such tragic material of this sort—we shall pass over for the present, because a detailed treatment of such matters does not really suit the character of this introductory survey.

But when a fitting occasion comes, we shall give them the notice they deserve, especially in view of Timaeus's ignorance about the regions just described.

# Book 2 Chapter 17: Gallic Settlements in the Valley of the Po

...except that these plains were in ancient times occupied by the Etruscans, at the same period as the so-called Phlegraean plains around Capua and Nola—plains which, just because they lay in many people's way and so became known, have won a great reputation for fertility.

For this reason those who record the dominions of the Etruscans should not direct their account to the land now held by them, but to the plains just mentioned and to the resources they drew from these places.

The Celts, mingling with them because they lay alongside one another, and casting envious eyes on the beauty of the country, on a slight pretext unexpectedly attacked with a great army, drove the Etruscans out of the country around the Padus, and took possession of the plains themselves.

So the first districts, those lying toward the eastern part of the Padus, the Laevi and the Lebecii settled; after these the Insubres, who were the greatest of their peoples; and next to these, along the river, the Cenomani.

The districts already adjoining the Adriatic were held by another, very ancient people: they are called the Veneti, differing little from the Celts in customs and dress, but using a different language.

About them the tragic poets have made much talk and dealt out much marvel-mongering.

The districts beyond the Padus, around the Apennine, were settled first by the Anares, and after them by the Boii; next to these, toward the Adriatic, the Lingones; and the last, on the sea, the Senones.

These, then, were the most notable of the peoples that took possession of the regions just described.

They lived in unwalled villages, without any other furnishings of life.

For because they slept on beds of straw, ate meat, and practiced nothing besides war and farming, they led simple lives, with no other science or craft known among them at all.

Each man's property consisted of livestock and gold, since these alone could, whatever the circumstances, be readily carried about everywhere and moved according to their own wishes.

They set the greatest store by their bands of companions, because among them the most feared and most powerful man was the one who was reckoned to have the most attendants and followers in his train.

# Book 2 Chapter 18: Early Conflicts between Gauls and Romans

In the beginning, then, they not only mastered the territory, but also made many of the neighbouring peoples their subjects, having overawed them by their daring.

Some time later, having defeated the Romans and those drawn up in battle alongside them, and pursuing the fugitives, they occupied Rome itself, except for the Capitol, three days after the battle.

But when a diversion arose—the Veneti invading their country—they then made terms with the Romans, handed back the city, and returned home.

After this they were caught up in internal wars; and some of those who lived in the Alps kept making raids and often gathered against them, comparing their own situation with the prosperity that had come to the others.

During this period the Romans recovered their own strength and once more set in order their affairs with the Latins.

When the Celts came again to Alba with a great army, in the thirtieth year after the capture of the city, the Romans did not then dare to lead out their armies against them, because—the assault having come unexpectedly—they were caught off guard and had no time to assemble the mustered forces of their allies.

But again, on a further expedition twelve years later, when the Celts were advancing with a great army, the Romans, having learned of it beforehand and gathered their allies, went out to meet them with great eagerness, hastening to engage and to risk all in a decisive battle.

But the Gauls, dismayed at their approach and falling into discord among themselves, when night came on made their withdrawal homeward in something very like a flight.

# Book 2 Chapter 19: Gallic Wars

...[peace] and a treaty. Having kept firmly to it for thirty years, when a fresh movement arose among the Transalpine Gauls, the [Cisalpine Gauls], fearing that a heavy war might be stirred up against them, turned the onslaught of the would-be invaders away from themselves by giving gifts and pleading their kinship, but incited them against the Romans and joined in the campaign themselves.

On this campaign they made their attack through Etruria, with the Etruscans joining forces with them, and after gathering a great quantity of plunder they returned safely from the Roman province.

But when they reached home and fell into strife over the greedy division of what they had taken, they destroyed the greater part both of the booty and of their own force.

This is the customary thing for the Gauls to do whenever they have seized something of their neighbours', and it comes about especially because of their senseless bouts of drunkenness and overeating.

After this, again in the fourth year, the Samnites and the Gauls came to an agreement together and drew up against the Romans in the territory of the Camertini, and destroyed many of them in the engagement.

On this occasion the Romans, stung to rivalry by the setback they had suffered, marched out a few days later and, engaging with all their armies in the territory of the Sentinates against the aforesaid enemy, killed most of them and forced the rest to flee headlong, each to his own land.

When ten years had again passed, the Gauls arrived with a great army to besiege the city of the Arretini.

The Romans came to the rescue and, engaging before the city, were defeated. In this battle Lucius the commander was killed, and they appointed Manius Curius in his place.

When he had sent envoys into Gaul concerning the prisoners, the Gauls broke the truce and killed the envoys.

When the Romans, in their anger, marched against them at once, the Gauls called the Senones met them and joined battle.

The Romans, prevailing over them in a pitched battle, killed most of them and drove out the rest, and became masters of all the country.

Into it they sent the first colony of Gaul, the city called Sena, named after the Gauls who had formerly inhabited it,

about which we explained a little earlier, saying that it lies on the Adriatic at the edge of the plains around the Padus.

# Book 2 Chapter 20: The Boii Attack the Romans and Lose

The Boii, seeing the Senones driven out, and fearing for themselves and their land that they might suffer the same fate, marched out in full force, having summoned the Etruscans.

Mustering near the lake called Vadimon, they drew up against the Romans.

In this battle most of the Etruscans were cut down, and only very few of the Boii escaped.

Nevertheless, in the very next year the same peoples came to an agreement once more, and, arming even those of their young men who had only just reached manhood, drew up against the Romans.

Utterly defeated in the battle, they barely brought themselves to yield, and by sending envoys to ask for a truce and a settlement they made a treaty with the Romans.

These events happened to fall in the third year before Pyrrhus's crossing into Italy, and the fifth before the destruction of the Gauls at Delphi.

For at this period fortune set, as it were, a kind of plague of war upon all the Gauls.

From the struggles just described two excellent advantages fell to the Romans: for, having grown accustomed to being cut down by the Gauls, they could neither see nor expect anything more dreadful than what had already been done to them;

and so, having become perfect athletes in the works of war, they faced Pyrrhus,

and, having crushed the daring of the Gauls at the right moment, thereafter fought without distraction—first against Pyrrhus for Italy, and after that against the Carthaginians for the supremacy over Sicily.

# Book 2 Chapter 21: War with Insubres and Boii and Gaesatae

After the defeats just described, the Gauls kept quiet for forty-five years, maintaining peace with the Romans.

But when those who had been eyewitnesses of those terrors had passed out of life with the passage of time, and a younger generation had come up—full of unreasoning passion, and with no experience or sight of any evil or any reverse—

they began once again to disturb the established order—as naturally tends to happen—being roused to anger against the Romans on trifling pretexts and calling in the Gauls from the Alps.

At first these designs were carried on apart from the common people, by the leaders themselves, in secret.

And so, when the Transalpine Gauls arrived as far as Ariminum with an armed force, the masses of the Boii grew suspicious and fell into discord both against their own leaders and against the newcomers; they put to death their own kings, Atis and Galatus, and cut one another to pieces in pitched battles.

It was then that the Romans too, alarmed at the invasion, went out with an army; but on learning of the self-inflicted destruction of the Gauls, they withdrew home again.

In the fifth year after this alarm, when Marcus Lepidus was consul, the Romans parcelled out among their citizens the territory in Gaul called Picene, from which they had defeated and expelled the Gauls called the Senones,

Gaius Flaminius having introduced this demagogic measure and policy—which indeed we must say became for the Romans, so to speak, the first cause of the people's turn for the worse, and also the cause of the war that afterward arose for them against the aforesaid Gauls.

For many of the Gauls slipped into that undertaking, and especially the Boii, because their lands bordered on Roman territory, having come to believe that the Romans were now making war on them no longer for leadership and dominion, but for their total uprooting and destruction.
