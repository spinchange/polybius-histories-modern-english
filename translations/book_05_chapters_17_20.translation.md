# Book 5 Chapter 17: Philip Comes to Corinth

About this time Lycurgus returned from Messenia, having accomplished nothing worth mentioning there. Afterward he set out again from Sparta and seized the city of the Tegeans.

But when the inhabitants withdrew into the citadel, he undertook to besiege it. Since he was unable to make any progress at all, he once more withdrew to Sparta.

The Eleans made a raid into the territory of Dyme, and by drawing into an ambush the cavalry who had come to help, they routed them easily.

They killed quite a number of the Gallic troops, and among the citizens they took prisoner were Polymedes of Aegium and the Dymaeans Agesipolis and Diocles.

Dorimachus had made his first expedition with the Aetolians because he was convinced, as I said above, that he could ravage Thessaly without danger to himself and would force Philip to raise the siege of the Paleis.

But when he found Chrysogonus and Petraeus ready in Thessaly to risk battle with him, he did not dare come down into the plain, and instead kept close to the foothills.

Then, when word reached him of the Macedonian invasion of Aetolia, he gave up his operations in Thessaly and hurried to go to their aid. But when he arrived and found that the Macedonians had already withdrawn from Aetolia, he was left behind and proved too late in every respect.

Meanwhile the king sailed from Leucas, ravaged the territory of the Oeantheans as he passed along the coast, and came down with his whole fleet to Corinth.

After mooring his ships at Lechaeum, he disembarked his troops and sent messengers to the allied cities in the Peloponnese, naming the day on which everyone was to be in Tegea with arms by nightfall.

# Book 5 Chapter 18: Philip Appears At Sparta

After making these arrangements and spending no time at all in Corinth, he gave the Macedonians orders to break camp. Marching by way of Argos, he reached Tegea on the second day.

There he took with him the Achaeans who had assembled and advanced by the mountain road, eager to enter Laconian territory without the Lacedaemonians noticing.

After going around through the deserted country, on the fourth day he came upon the hills opposite the city and passed along toward Amyclae itself, keeping the Menelaeum on his right.

The Lacedaemonians, watching from the city as the army moved along, were struck with panic and fear, marveling at what was happening.

For their minds were still unsettled by the reports that had come in about Philip's destruction of Thermus and, in general, about his actions in Aetolia; and there was even a rumor among them that Lycurgus would be sent out to aid the Aetolians.

But that the danger would come upon them so quickly from so great a distance had never entered any of their minds at all, especially since the king's youth made him seem in some measure easy to despise. So, because events were turning out contrary to all expectation, they were naturally dumbfounded.

For Philip, handling his enterprises with more daring and more practical skill than one would expect from his age, reduced all his enemies to helplessness and confusion.

For, as I said above, he had sailed from the middle of Aetolia and crossed the Ambracian Gulf by night, then put in at Leucas.

He stayed there two days, and on the third, putting out before dawn, reached Lechaeum on the second day after that, ravaging the Aetolian coast as he sailed.

After this he continued his marches without interruption, and on the seventh day came upon the hills above the city near the Menelaeum, so that most people who saw what had happened could hardly believe it.

So the Lacedaemonians, thrown into fear by the unexpected nature of the event, were at a loss and in a difficult position for the moment.

# Book 5 Chapter 19: Philip Marches Through Laconia

Philip, on the first day, encamped around Amyclae.

Amyclae is a place in Laconia, richly wooded and very fruitful, about twenty stades from Sparta.

There is also there a sanctuary of Apollo, one of the most famous among the sacred places in Laconia. The place lies in the part of the country that slopes toward the sea from the city.

On the following day, ravaging the countryside as he went, he descended to the stockade called Pyrrhus.

After raiding and laying waste the neighboring districts for the next two days, he encamped near Carnium. Setting out from there, he marched on Asine; and after making assaults on it but accomplishing nothing of importance, he broke camp again. From that point on, as he continued his advance, he devastated all the country facing the Cretan Sea as far as Taenarum.

Then, changing direction once more, he marched past the Lacedaemonian naval station, which is called Gythium, has a secure harbor, and lies about two hundred and thirty stades from the city.

Leaving this on his right as he marched, he encamped near Helos, which, viewed as a district, is the largest and finest stretch of land in Laconia.

From there he sent out raiding parties and burned and devastated the whole region and destroyed the crops in it; and his foragers reached as far as Acriae and Leucae, and also the territory of Boeae.

# Book 5 Chapter 20: Philip's Return Opposed

When the Messenians received Philip's letters concerning the campaign, they were no less eager than the other allies in their initial zeal, but set out promptly and sent their men in the prime of life: two thousand infantry and two hundred cavalry.

But because of the length of the journey they were delayed and missed Philip at Tegea, and at first they were at a loss what they should do.

Then, anxious not to seem to be hanging back because of the suspicions previously entertained about them, they marched through Argolis into Laconia, wanting to join Philip.

When they reached a place called Glympes, which lies on the borders of Argolis and Laconia, they encamped there both awkwardly and carelessly.

For they neither dug a trench nor put up a palisade around their camp, nor did they choose a naturally advantageous site; instead, trusting in the goodwill of the people who lived in the place, they encamped unsuspectingly in front of its wall.

But when the arrival of the Messenians was reported to Lycurgus, he took his mercenaries and some of the Lacedaemonians, advanced to the place, and at daybreak boldly attacked their camp.

The Messenians had made bad decisions in every respect, and especially in marching out from Tegea without a force strong enough in numbers and without relying on experienced advisers; yet when the danger actually came upon them, they still did the best that circumstances allowed for their own safety.

For as soon as they saw the enemy appearing, they abandoned everything and rushed for refuge to the place.

As a result, Lycurgus got possession of most of the horses and the baggage, but he took not a single man alive, and of the cavalry he killed only eight.

So the Messenians, after meeting with this reverse, returned home again through Argos.

Lycurgus, elated by what had happened, went to Sparta, made his preparations, and conferred with his friends, determined not to let Philip get out of the country again without danger and a clash.

But the king, breaking camp from Helos, advanced while ravaging the country as he went, and on the fourth day again reached Amyclae with his whole army about midday.
