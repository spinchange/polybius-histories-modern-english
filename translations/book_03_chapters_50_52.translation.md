# Book 3 Chapter 50: Hannibal Harassed By the Natives

After marching for ten days along the river, covering some eight hundred stades, Hannibal began the climb up toward the Alps, and it was now that he fell into the gravest dangers.

For as long as the army was on level ground, all the local chieftains of the Allobroges held back from attacking, partly out of fear of the cavalry and partly because of the barbarians who were escorting the column. But once the escort had withdrawn to their own country,

and Hannibal's forces began to push forward into the difficult terrain, the Allobrogian chieftains then mustered a sizable body of men and seized in advance the strategic positions through which Hannibal's troops were compelled to make their ascent.

Now if they had kept their plan hidden, they would have utterly destroyed the Carthaginian army. As it was, since they were spotted, they did indeed do serious harm to Hannibal's men, but they suffered no less themselves.

For once the Carthaginian general realized that the natives had occupied the advantageous positions ahead of him, he encamped at the foot of the pass and waited there,

and he sent ahead some of the Gauls who were serving as his guides to scout out the enemy's intentions and their whole plan.

When these men had carried out their orders, the general learned that during the day the enemy kept careful watch and held the positions in force, but at night they withdrew to a nearby town. Adapting himself to this state of affairs, he devised the following scheme.

He took up his army and advanced openly, and on coming near the difficult ground he encamped not far from the enemy. When night fell, he ordered the watch-fires to be lit and left the greater part of his army where it was; then, taking the men best suited for the task and equipping them lightly, he passed through the narrow defiles during the night and occupied the positions the enemy had seized earlier, the barbarians having withdrawn to the town as was their custom.

# Book 3 Chapter 51: The Gauls Attack the Baggage Train

When this had happened and day broke, the barbarians saw what had occurred and at first gave up their attempt.

But afterward, watching the great mass of pack animals and the cavalry struggling to wind their way slowly through the difficult ground, they were drawn by the opportunity to fasten onto the column.

When they did so and the barbarians attacked at many points, the Carthaginians suffered heavy losses—not so much from the enemy as from the terrain itself, and above all among the horses and pack animals.

For the approach was not only narrow and rough but also flanked by sheer drops, so that at every disturbance or panic many of the pack animals were sent tumbling over the cliffs along with their loads.

It was the wounded horses most of all that caused this kind of chaos. Some of them, panicked by their wounds, would collide head-on with the pack animals, while others, charging forward, shoved aside everything in their path on the narrow ground, and so they created enormous confusion.

Watching all this, and reckoning that even those who survived the present danger could not be saved if the baggage train were destroyed, Hannibal took the men who had seized the heights during the night and rushed down to help those who had gone ahead on the march.

In doing so, since Hannibal was charging from higher ground, many of the enemy were killed—but no fewer of his own men perished as well,

for the uproar along the column was now mounting on both sides because of the shouting and the clash described above.

But once he had killed most of the Allobroges and routed the rest, forcing them to flee back to their own land, the remaining mass of pack animals and horses then made its way through the difficult ground, slowly and with great hardship.

Hannibal himself rallied as many men as he could after the engagement and assaulted the town from which the enemy had launched their attack.

Finding it nearly deserted, since everyone had been lured out after plunder, he took control of the town. From this he gained many things useful both for the moment and for the future.

For the immediate present he recovered a quantity of horses and pack animals along with the men captured with them, and for the future he secured a supply of grain and livestock enough for two or three days.

But the most important result was the fear he instilled in the tribes that came next, so that none of those living near the passes would readily dare to attack him.

# Book 3 Chapter 52: Treachery of the Gauls

For the time being, then, he made his camp on the spot, stayed for one day, and then set out again.

Over the following days he led the army on in safety for a while; but by the fourth day he once more found himself in great danger.

For the people living along his route, having conspired together with treacherous intent, came out to meet him carrying branches and garlands—which among nearly all the natives is a token of friendship, just as the herald's staff is among the Greeks.

Wary of trusting such assurances, Hannibal carefully questioned them about their intentions and their whole purpose.

They claimed to be well aware both of the town's capture and of the destruction of those who had tried to do him wrong, and they made clear that this was precisely why they had come: they wished neither to inflict nor to suffer any harm, and they even promised to hand over hostages from among themselves.

For a long time Hannibal remained cautious and distrustful of what they said. But reasoning that if he accepted their offer he might perhaps make these new arrivals more cautious and more peaceable, whereas if he refused he would have them as open enemies, he assented to their proposal and pretended to enter into friendship with them.

When the barbarians had handed over the hostages, supplied cattle in abundance, and altogether put themselves into his hands without reserve, Hannibal's men came to trust them to a certain extent—so much so that they even used them as guides for the difficult ground that lay ahead.
