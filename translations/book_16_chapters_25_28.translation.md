# Book 16 Chapter 25: King Attalus At Athens

The people of Athens sent envoys to King Attalus both to thank him for what had already happened and to urge him to come to Athens so that they could consult together about the present crisis.

A few days later the king learned that Roman ambassadors had arrived at the Piraeus, and since he thought it necessary to meet them, he sailed there in haste.

When the Athenian people heard that he was coming, they passed lavish decrees about meeting him and about the whole manner of receiving the king.

After Attalus landed at the Piraeus, he spent the first day in business with the Roman envoys, and he was delighted to see that they remembered their former common cause and were ready for war against Philip.

On the next day he went up to the city with the Romans and the Athenian magistrates amid great ceremony. Not only those in office and the cavalry came out to meet them, but all the citizens as well, with their children and wives.

When they met, the goodwill shown by the crowd to the Romans, and still more to Attalus, left nothing beyond it.

When he entered by the Dipylon gate, priestesses and priests stood in lines on both sides. Then all the temples were opened, victims were placed beside every altar, and they invited him to offer sacrifice.

Finally they voted him such honors as they had scarcely ever granted so quickly to any of their earlier benefactors.

Among other things they created a tribe named after Attalus and enrolled him among their eponymous founders.

# Book 16 Chapter 26: Athens Votes for War Against Philip

After this they summoned an assembly and invited the king to address it.

But he declined, saying that it would be vulgar to come forward in person and recount his own benefactions to those who had received them.

So they gave up asking him to appear, but requested him to write down what he thought best for the present circumstances and to hand it over.

He agreed and wrote it, and the presiding magistrates then introduced the letter.

Its main points were a reminder of the earlier benefactions he had shown the people, an account of what he had done against Philip in the present crisis,

and finally an appeal to join the war against Philip, together with a solemn warning that if they did not now choose to share nobly in the hostility together with the Rhodians, the Romans, and himself, but later, after letting the occasion pass, wanted to share in the peace once others had won it, they would miss what was truly best for their country.

When this letter was read, the people were ready to vote for war, both because of what was said and because of their goodwill toward Attalus.

Then the Rhodians also came forward and spoke at length to the same effect, and the Athenians resolved to declare war on Philip.

They also received the Rhodians magnificently, crowned their state with a crown for valor, and granted all Rhodians equal civic rights, because, among other services, they had restored to them the ships that had been captured, together with the men on board.

After accomplishing this, the Rhodian envoys sailed away with the fleet to Ceos and the islands.

# Book 16 Chapter 27: The Romans Warn Philip Not to Attack Greece

At the time when the Roman ambassadors were staying in Athens, Nicanor, acting for Philip, was raiding Attica as far as the Academy.

The Romans sent a herald ahead to him, met with him, and told him to report to Philip that the Romans called on the king to make war on none of the Greeks, and to submit the wrongs done to Attalus to judgment before an impartial tribunal.

They added that if he did this, he could remain at peace with Rome, but if he refused to obey, the opposite would follow. Nicanor heard this and withdrew.

The Romans delivered the same message about Philip to the Epirotes as they sailed past Phoenice, and to Amynander when they went up into Athamania; similarly they addressed the Aetolians at Naupactus and the Achaeans at Aegium.

Having thus made these terms known to Philip through Nicanor, they themselves sailed on to Antiochus and Ptolemy to arrange a settlement.

# Book 16 Chapter 28: Activity and Energy of Philip

It seems to me that many men have managed to make a good beginning and to keep their enthusiasm strong enough for the growth of their enterprises,

but to carry their purpose through to the end, and even when fortune stands against them to make reason supply what impulse lacks, belongs to only a few.

So in this case one might rightly blame Attalus and the Rhodians for lack of effort, while admiring in Philip what was kingly and high-spirited and his persistence in his purpose—not praising his whole character, but noting the force of his energy at that moment.

I make this distinction so that no one may think I am contradicting myself, since just now I was praising Attalus and the Rhodians and blaming Philip, and now I seem to do the reverse.

For this is exactly why I said at the beginning of my work that it is necessary sometimes to praise and sometimes to blame the same men.

Circumstances often change men's purposes for the worse, and often for the better,

and sometimes, by their own natural character, men are at one time moved toward what is right and at another toward the opposite. One of these things, I think, happened in Philip's case here.

For stung by his losses, and acting largely out of anger and passion, he adapted himself to the needs of the moment with a kind of fierce, almost inspired energy. In this way he rose up against the Rhodians and King Attalus and carried through the next series of actions.

I was led to say this because some men, like bad runners in a race, give up their purpose near the finish, while others win over their rivals most of all at that very point.
