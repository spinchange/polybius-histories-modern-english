# Book 10 Chapter 17: Scipio's Treatment of the Prisoners

This is the thing that most often harms other armies. Since most men endure hardship and face danger for the sake of gain, it is obvious that when such an opportunity appears, those left in reserve or in camp can scarcely be expected to hold back, because among most peoples the common belief is that whatever a man seizes belongs to the one who took it.

For even if a king or careful general orders the profits to be brought into the common stock, everyone still thinks whatever he can conceal is his own.

So when the mass of soldiers rushes after plunder and cannot be controlled, whole armies are put in danger.

Indeed, many who have successfully carried out their attack—sometimes storming an enemy camp, sometimes taking a city—have not only been driven out again, but completely ruined, for no reason other than this.

That is why commanders ought to show more care and foresight about nothing else so much as this: that whenever such an occasion arises, the many should feel that the hope of profit stands equally open to all.

At that time, then, while the tribunes were occupied with the disposal of the spoils, the Roman general, when the whole number of prisoners had been assembled—it came to a little less than ten thousand—ordered them first to be separated into the citizen men and women and their children, and second, the craftsmen.

Once this had been done, he addressed the citizens, urged them to be well disposed toward Rome and remember the favor done them, and released them all to go back to their homes.

They departed weeping and rejoicing together at their unexpected deliverance, after bowing in reverence before the general.

To the craftsmen he said that for the present they were public property of Rome; but if each showed goodwill and zeal in his own craft, he promised them freedom once the war against the Carthaginians had been brought to a successful conclusion.

He ordered these men to be enrolled with the quaestor and appointed one Roman overseer for every thirty of them, their total number being about two thousand.

From the rest of the prisoners he selected the strongest, those in the prime of age and appearance, and assigned them to his crews.

In this way he increased all the sailors by one half and manned the captured ships too, so that on each vessel the number of men was now only a little short of double what it had previously been.

For the captured ships numbered eighteen, while those he had from the beginning were thirty-five.

To these men too he promised freedom on similar terms, if they showed loyalty and zeal until they had defeated the Carthaginians in the war.

By handling the prisoners in this way he inspired the citizens with strong goodwill and confidence both toward himself and toward Roman policy, while he stirred great eagerness in the craftsmen by the hope of freedom.

# Book 10 Chapter 18: Scipio's Treatment of Women

After this he separated Mago and the Carthaginians who were with him. Of these, two belonged to the council of elders and fifteen to the senate.

He entrusted them to Gaius Laelius, ordering him to take proper care of the men.

Next he summoned the hostages, who were more than three hundred in number. He brought the children forward one by one,

stroked them with his hand, and told them to take courage, because in a few days they would see their parents.

He urged all the rest together to be cheerful and to write letters to their own cities and relatives, first saying that they were safe and well treated, and second that the Romans wished to restore them all safely to their homes if their kinsmen chose alliance with Rome.

After saying this, and having previously selected from the spoils whatever would be most useful for his purpose, he distributed to each suitable gifts according to family and age: to the girls earrings and bracelets, to the young men daggers and swords.

Among the captive women, the wife of Mandonius—who was brother of Andobales, king of the Ilergetes—fell at his feet and with tears begged him to show more regard for their honor than the Carthaginians had done. Moved with sympathy, he asked what necessities they lacked.

For the woman was older and bore herself with a certain noble dignity.

When she said nothing, he called those who had been assigned to care for the women.

When they came and explained that all necessary provisions were being supplied to them in abundance, the woman again clasped his knees and repeated the same plea. Publius, now still more perplexed and suspecting that the appointed guardians were neglecting their duty and falsely reporting otherwise for the moment, told the women to take courage.

He himself, he said, would appoint others to look after them, so that they should lack nothing necessary.

After pausing a little, she said, "General, you do not understand what we are asking if you think it is food we now beg from you."

Then at last Publius grasped the woman's meaning, and as he saw before his eyes the youthful bloom of Andobales' daughters and of many other noble maidens, he was compelled to weep, while the woman in a few words made clear the danger of their situation.

So he showed plainly at once that he understood what had been said, and taking her by the right hand told both her and the others to be of good courage.

He would take thought for them as though they were his own sisters and daughters, and would appoint trustworthy men to care for them in accordance with what he had promised.

# Book 10 Chapter 19: The Money

After this he handed over to the quaestors whatever public money of the Carthaginians had been captured.

This amounted to more than six hundred talents, so that when it was added to the four hundred he had brought with him from Rome, the entire sum of funds at his disposal came to more than a thousand talents.

At about this time some young Romans came upon a maiden in the full bloom of age and beauty, far surpassing the other women. Knowing that Publius was fond of women, they brought her to him and, placing her before him, said they gave the girl to him as a gift.

He was struck and amazed by her beauty, and said that if he had been a private man, he could have received no gift more pleasing than this; but as a general, none could have been less welcome.

By this answer, as it seems to me, he meant to suggest that while in times of rest and leisure such pleasures and pastimes may be among the sweetest delights for young men, in times of action they become very great hindrances both to body and mind.

So he thanked the young men, but called the girl's father and, giving her back into his hands, told him to marry her to whichever of her fellow citizens he chose.

Thus, by displaying both self-command and moderation, he won great admiration from those under his authority.

After settling these matters and handing over the rest of the prisoners to the tribunes, he sent Gaius Laelius to Rome on a quinquereme, entrusting to him the Carthaginians and the most distinguished of the other prisoners, to report at home what had taken place.

For since most people there had already given up hope of success in Iberia, he knew clearly that once this news was announced they would take heart again and support the enterprise with much greater vigor.

# Book 10 Chapter 20: Scipio's Training Regimen

He himself stayed for some time at Carthage and continually trained the naval forces, while he also laid down for the tribunes something like the following method for exercising the infantry camps.

On the first day he ordered them to run thirty stades in full armor; on the second, all were to clean, maintain, and inspect their equipment openly; on the next they were to rest and take their ease.

On the day after that some were to fence with wooden swords covered in leather and tipped with buttons, while others were to cast javelins with blunted heads; on the fifth day they were to return again to the same runs and begin the cycle from the start.

And so that there should be no lack either of weapons for practice or of those needed for actual war, he devoted the greatest care to the craftsmen.

He had assigned careful men over this work in divisions, as I said before; but he himself also made the rounds every day and personally saw to the supplies furnished to each group.

So while the infantry around the city were busy with drills and exercises, the naval forces with practice cruises and rowing at sea, and the people in the city sharpening, forging, carpentry work, and in general every sort of effort in the making of arms,

no one who saw that city then, to use Xenophon's phrase, would have hesitated to call it a workshop of war.

And when he thought that everything had been well prepared and adequately exercised for the tasks ahead, he secured the city by garrisons and repairs to the walls, then broke camp with both army and fleet and advanced on the march toward Tarraco, taking the hostages with him.
