# Book 15 Chapter 17: Scipio Unmoved by Carthaginian Laments

Displays of emotion that go beyond what is customary among a people, when they appear to spring genuinely from overwhelming disaster, call forth pity in those who see and hear them; the strangeness of the spectacle moves us all in some degree.

But when such behavior seems adopted theatrically and for effect, it produces not pity but anger and disgust. That was what happened in the case of the Carthaginian envoys.

Scipio began briefly by saying that for their own sake the Romans owed them no kindness at all, since they themselves admitted that they had first brought war upon Rome by enslaving Saguntum in violation of treaty, and had only lately broken sworn oaths and written agreements once again.

Any mildness now shown them, he said, was for the Romans’ own sake, out of regard for Fortune and for the human condition, and because they had decided to behave with magnanimity.

And this, he said, would become clear to the envoys too if they judged the present position rightly. They ought not to count it terrible if they were ordered to suffer, to do, or to surrender certain things; rather,

they should regard it as remarkable if any act of humanity at all were granted them, since Fortune, by reason of their own injustice, had taken away any claim they had to pity or indulgence and had made them subject to their enemies.

After saying this, he set out what leniencies were being granted and, in turn, what penalties they would have to endure.

# Book 15 Chapter 18: Terms Made After the Battle of Zama

These were the main points of the proposals. The Carthaginians were to keep the cities in Libya that they had possessed before making war on Rome, as well as the land they had formerly held, together with their livestock, slaves, and other property.

From that day onward the Carthaginians were to remain free from molestation, to use their own customs and laws, and to live without a garrison.

These were the favorable terms. The opposite clauses were that the Carthaginians were to make full restitution to the Romans for all injuries committed during the armistice, to return all prisoners and deserters from every past period, and to hand over all warships except ten triremes, along with all their elephants.

They were to wage war on no people outside Libya at all, and on none within Libya without Roman consent.

They were to restore to King Massanissa every house, tract of land, city, and whatever else had belonged either to him or to his ancestors within the boundaries that would be assigned to him.

They were to furnish the Roman army with provisions for three months and to pay it until a reply came from Rome regarding the treaty.

The Carthaginians were to pay ten thousand talents of silver in fifty years, paying two hundred Euboic talents each year.

And as security for good faith they were to give one hundred hostages selected by the Roman general from among the young men, none younger than fourteen and none older than thirty.

# Book 15 Chapter 19: Hannibal Persuades Carthage to Accept These Terms

Such was what the Roman commander said to the envoys. After hearing it, they hurried home and explained everything to those in Carthage.

At that point, it is said, when one of the senators was about to speak against accepting the terms and had just begun, Hannibal stepped forward and pulled the man down from the platform.

When the rest were angered because he had done something contrary to established custom, Hannibal rose again and said that he admitted he did not understand such usages, but they ought to excuse him if he acted against their habits, since he had left his country when he was only nine years old and had returned to it only after more than forty-five years.

Therefore, he said, they should not focus on whether he had failed in matters of custom, but far more on whether he truly felt for the condition of his fatherland. That, he said, was what had driven him into this breach of decorum.

For it seemed to him astonishing, indeed utterly irrational, if any man who was a Carthaginian and knew what policies they had pursued against Rome, both collectively for the city and individually for themselves, did not now bow before Fortune in gratitude when, having become subject to Rome, they were receiving such mild terms.

Only a few days before, if anyone had asked what they expected their country to suffer if the Romans prevailed, none of them could even have answered, so great and excessive were the evils that seemed likely to come upon them.

So now, he said, they should not even bring the matter into debate, but unanimously accept the proposed terms, offer sacrifice to the gods, and pray with one voice that the Roman people would confirm them.

Since he was seen to be advising wisely and in a way suited to the crisis, it was decided to make peace on the stated terms.

The council immediately sent envoys to agree formally to them.

# Book 15 Chapter 20: League Against Ptolemy Epiphanes

II. Affairs of Macedonia and Greece

Who would not marvel at this: while Ptolemy himself was alive and did not need their help, these kings were ready to assist him,

but when he died and left behind a mere infant, whose kingdom both of them were naturally bound to preserve, then they invited one another to divide the child’s dominion between themselves and remove the heir he had left.

And they did not, like tyrants, put forward even some small pretext to veil their shame; rather they acted openly and brutally, in a way so savage that it calls to mind the life attributed to fish, among whom the destruction of the smaller becomes food and sustenance for the larger, though they are of the same kind.

Who, looking into this treaty as into a mirror, would not think himself an eyewitness of these kings’ impiety toward the gods, their cruelty toward human beings, and their excessive greed?

And yet who would not reasonably cease blaming Fortune for the conduct of human affairs in this case, seeing that she afterward inflicted on them the punishment they deserved and offered to later generations the finest example of correction in the chastisement of those kings?

For while they were breaking faith with each other and carving up the child’s kingdom, Fortune brought the Romans upon them, and what those men had unlawfully planned against their neighbor she justly and fittingly brought about against them.

For both of them were soon defeated in war and not only prevented from seizing what belonged to another, but were themselves shut in under tribute and made to do Rome’s bidding.

In the end, within a very short time, Fortune restored Ptolemy’s kingdom, while the dynasties and successors of those two monarchs she either utterly overthrew and destroyed or else involved in almost the same disasters.
