# Book 3 Chapter 108: Aemilius Paulus Harangues the Army

For this reason they summoned Aemilius and his colleague, set vividly before their eyes the magnitude of what would follow from the battle in either direction, and sent them off with instructions to bring the whole matter to a decision at the right moment, with courage and in a manner worthy of their homeland.

When the two men reached the army and assembled the troops, they made the Senate's decision clear to the rank and file and urged upon them what the present circumstances demanded, Lucius delivering his words out of genuine personal feeling.

Most of what he said was directed toward this single aim: the recent disasters that had befallen them. For it was on this account, more than anything, that the men had become demoralized and stood in need of encouragement.

He therefore tried to establish that, as for the defeats in the earlier battles, one could find not just one or two but several causes that had brought about such an outcome,

but that under present conditions no excuse remained—if only they proved themselves men—for failing to defeat the enemy.

For at that earlier time, he said, the two commanders had never fought side by side with the legions, nor had they made use of well-trained forces; instead the troops had been newly levied and had never set eyes on any danger at all.

And, most important of all, the men had been so ignorant of the enemy's situation that they had drawn up for battle and descended into all-out combat practically without ever having laid eyes on their opponents.

"For the men who were beaten at the Trebia River had arrived from Sicily only the day before, and at first light on the very next day they were drawn up for battle.

And as for those who fought in Etruria, not only had they not seen the enemy beforehand, but they could not even make them out during the battle itself, because of the misfortune that occurred with the weather.

Now, at any rate, everything is the opposite of what has just been described."

# Book 3 Chapter 109: Paulus's Speech Continued

"For in the first place, both of us consuls are here, not only to share the dangers with you ourselves, but we have also persuaded last year's commanders to remain on hand and take part in these same struggles.

You yourselves, moreover, have not only seen the enemy's weaponry, their formations, and their numbers, but you have now spent a second year fighting against them almost daily.

Since, then, every detail is the opposite of what obtained in the previous battles, it is reasonable to expect that the outcome of the present contest will likewise be the opposite.

For it would be strange—indeed, one might say, impossible—that men who in piecemeal skirmishes, engaging on equal terms, have for the most part come out on top should, when drawn up all together and outnumbering the enemy by more than two to one, be defeated.

Therefore, men, since everything has been prepared for you to win, the situation needs only one thing: your will and your eagerness—and on that point I do not think it fitting to exhort you further.

For those who serve someone else for pay, or who as allies are about to risk their lives on behalf of others—men for whom the moment of battle itself is most fearful, while the consequences make little difference to them—for such men a manner of exhortation is necessary.

But for men like you now, whose danger is not for the sake of others but for yourselves, your homeland, your wives, and your children, and for whom what follows afterward makes a difference many times greater than the dangers of the moment—for such men only a reminder is needed, not exhortation.

For who would not wish, above all, to win as he fights, and, if that should prove impossible, to die fighting first rather than live to look upon the outrage and ruin of those I have named?

Therefore, men, apart from anything I say, take it upon yourselves to set before your eyes the difference between losing and winning and all that goes with each, and so present yourselves for the battle as though it is not merely the legions that your country now risks, but its very existence.

For if the present situation should somehow be decided otherwise, she has nothing left to add to her current resources that would let her prevail over the enemy.

She has staked all her eagerness and strength upon you, and she holds all her hopes of survival in your hands. Do not now disappoint her of them,

but render to your homeland the thanks that are its due, and make it clear to all mankind that the earlier defeats came about not because Romans are lesser men than Carthaginians, but because of the inexperience of those who fought at the time and because of the circumstances arising from the conditions then."

Having delivered these and similar words of encouragement, Lucius then dismissed the troops.

# Book 3 Chapter 110: Skirmishes Before Cannae

The next day they broke camp and led the army to where they heard the enemy was encamped. Arriving on the second day, they pitched camp about fifty stades from the enemy.

Now Lucius, observing that the surrounding terrain was flat and bare, said they ought not to engage where the enemy was superior in cavalry, but should instead draw the enemy on and advance into ground of a kind where the battle would be decided more by the infantry forces.

But since Gaius, out of inexperience, held the opposite view, there was disagreement and friction between the commanders—which is the most dangerous thing of all.

When command fell to Gaius for the following day—since the consuls take over authority on alternate days, according to custom—he broke camp and advanced, wanting to draw near the enemy, despite Lucius's many protests and attempts to stop him.

Hannibal took up his light-armed troops and his cavalry, went out to meet them, and, falling upon them unexpectedly while they were still on the march, came to grips with them and threw them into great confusion.

The Romans withstood the first onslaught by putting forward some of their heavy-armed troops; then, sending in their javelin-men and cavalry, they gained the upper hand all through the engagement, because the Carthaginians had no significant reserves on hand, whereas the Romans had some maniples mixed in with their light troops to share the fighting.

For the moment, then, with night coming on, the two sides separated, the attack having turned out for the Carthaginians otherwise than they had hoped.

But on the next day Lucius, judging it best neither to fight nor any longer able to lead the army away safely, encamped with two-thirds of it beside the river called the Aufidus—the only river that cuts right through the Apennines—

(this being a continuous mountain range that separates all the watercourses of Italy, sending some to the Tyrrhenian Sea and others to the Adriatic; the Aufidus runs through it, having its sources in the regions of Italy facing the Tyrrhenian side and discharging into the Adriatic)—

while with the remaining third he set up a fortified camp across the river, eastward from the crossing, about ten stades from his own camp and a little more from the enemy's,

intending that these men should both cover the foraging parties from the camp on the far side and harass those of the Carthaginians.

# Book 3 Chapter 111: Hannibal Encourages his Troops

Hannibal, at this same time, seeing that circumstances were calling for a battle and an engagement with the enemy, but wary that his men might be demoralized by their recent setback, decided that the moment called for some encouragement and assembled the troops.

When they had gathered, he ordered them all to look around at the surrounding terrain and asked them what greater thing they could have prayed to the gods for, under the present circumstances, had they been given the choice, than to settle the whole issue in ground like this while holding so great a superiority in cavalry over the enemy.

When they all signaled their agreement at what he said, because its truth was so plain, he went on: "For this, then, first give thanks to the gods; for it is they who, in helping to secure our victory, have led the enemy into ground like this.

And second, give thanks to me, because I have forced the enemy to fight—for they can no longer escape it—and to fight openly on ground favorable to us.

But to exhort you now at any length to be confident and eager for the danger does not seem to me at all appropriate.

When you had no experience of fighting the Romans, that was necessary, and I did then address many arguments to you, with examples;

but now that you have, by common agreement, beaten the Romans three times in a row in battles of such magnitude, what argument could instill stronger confidence in you than the deeds themselves?

So then, through the earlier battles you have gained control of the countryside and its riches, in keeping with my promises, since I have proved truthful in everything I told you. But the present contest is for the cities and the riches within them.

If you prevail in it, you will at once be masters of all Italy; freed from your present hardships and gaining control of the whole prosperity of Rome, you will become, through this battle, the leaders and lords of everything.

Therefore there is no longer any need for words, but for deeds; for, if the gods are willing, I am convinced that I shall make good my promises to you any moment now."

Having said this and things much like it, while the men eagerly signaled their approval, he praised them, welcomed their enthusiasm, and dismissed the assembly. He at once made camp, setting his palisade along the same side of the river as the larger camp of the enemy.
