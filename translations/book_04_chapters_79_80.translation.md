# Book 4 Chapter 79: Surrender of Typanae and Phigalia

When these things had been accomplished, all the people throughout Triphylia were struck with alarm and began to deliberate about their own safety and that of their respective home cities.

Phillidas, for his part, abandoned Typanae—after first plundering some of the houses there—and withdrew to Lepreum.

This, indeed, was the kind of reward that the allies of the Aetolians regularly received at that time: not only to be openly deserted at the moments of greatest need, but, on top of that, to be plundered or betrayed and so to suffer at the hands of their own allies precisely what is the due lot of those defeated by an enemy.

The people of Typanae handed their city over to Philip, and the inhabitants of Hypana did much the same.

At the same time, the people of Phigalia—hearing of what had happened throughout Triphylia and being discontented with the alliance with the Aetolians—seized, in arms, the area around the polemarchium.

The Aetolian pirates who were lingering in this city for the sake of the plunder to be had from Messenia were at first able to lay hands on the Phigalians and bully them;

but when they saw the citizens gathering with one accord to defend themselves, they gave up the attempt, came to terms, took up their own belongings, and departed from the city.

The Phigalians then sent an embassy to Philip and put themselves and their city into his hands.

# Book 4 Chapter 80: The People of Lepreum Evict the Garrisons

While these affairs were still in progress, the people of Lepreum seized a certain quarter of their city and demanded that the Eleans, the Aetolians, and likewise the troops from the Lacedaemonians evacuate both the citadel and the city—for a relief force had come to them from the Lacedaemonians as well.

At first Phillidas and his men paid no heed but stayed put, expecting to overawe those in the city;

but when the king dispatched Taurion with soldiers to Phigalia and himself advanced toward Lepreum, drawing close to the city, Phillidas and his men, realizing the situation, were cowed, while the Lepreates were further emboldened in their resolve.

It was indeed a fine feat that the Lepreates accomplished: although there were within the walls a thousand Eleans, a thousand Aetolians together with the pirates, five hundred mercenaries, and two hundred Lacedaemonians, and although the citadel was held by these troops, they nonetheless made a stand for their own homeland and refused to give up their hopes.

Phillidas, seeing the Lepreates holding their ground like men and the Macedonians drawing near, withdrew from the city along with the Eleans and the troops from the Lacedaemonians.

So the Cretans sent by the Spartans returned home through Messenia, while Phillidas and his men made their retreat in the direction of Samicum. The mass of the Lepreates,

now in control of their homeland, sent out ambassadors to put their city into Philip's hands.

The king, on hearing what had happened, sent the rest of his force on to Lepreum, but took the peltasts and the light-armed troops and led them himself, eager to catch up with Phillidas and his men.

He overtook them and gained possession of all their baggage, but Phillidas and his men hurried on and managed to slip into Samicum.

Encamping before the place and bringing up the rest of his force from Lepreum, he made it appear to those inside that he intended to besiege the position.

The Aetolians, together with the Eleans, having nothing ready for withstanding a siege except their bare hands, were dismayed at their predicament and entered into talks with Philip about their safety.

Having obtained permission to march out under arms, they set off for Elis; and the king at once became master of Samicum.

After this, when the other towns also came over to him bearing the suppliant's olive branch, he took over Phrixa, Stylangium, Aepium, Bolax, Pyrgos, and Epitalium.

Having accomplished all this, he returned again to Lepreum, having brought all of Triphylia under his control in six days.

After exhorting the Lepreates as the occasion required and installing a garrison in the citadel, he set out with his army for Heraea, leaving Ladicus the Acarnanian as overseer of Triphylia.

On reaching the aforementioned city, he distributed all the spoils, then took up his baggage and, leaving Heraea, arrived at the height of winter at Megalopolis.
