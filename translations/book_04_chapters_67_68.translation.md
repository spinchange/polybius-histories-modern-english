# Book 4 Chapter 67: Philip Returns To the Peloponnese

So this Olympiad year was drawing to a close. Among the Aetolians, the time of the elections having now arrived, Dorimachus was chosen general. He at once took up his office, gathered the Aetolians under arms, and invaded the upland regions of Epirus, ravaging the country and pursuing its destruction with unusual ferocity.

For in most of what he did his aim was not so much his own profit as the injury of the Epirotes.

And when he reached the sanctuary at Dodona, he burned the colonnades, destroyed many of the votive offerings, and even razed the sacred building to the ground,

so that the Aetolians observed the bounds neither of peace nor of war, but in both circumstances alike acted on their designs in defiance of the common customs and laws of mankind.

Having accomplished these and similar deeds, Dorimachus then withdrew once more to his own country.

The winter was still advancing, and everyone had given up expecting Philip to appear because of the season. But the king took up three thousand bronze-shielded troops, two thousand peltasts, three hundred Cretans, and in addition to these about four hundred horsemen of the royal guard, and set out from Larisa.

He moved them across from Thessaly into Euboea, and from there to Cynos, and came through Boeotia and the Megarid to Corinth about the time of the winter solstice, having made his approach so swift and secret that not one of the Peloponnesians suspected what had happened.

He closed the gates of Corinth, posted guards to control the roads, and on the very next day summoned Aratus the elder to come to him from Sicyon. He also sent off dispatches to the general of the Achaeans and to the cities, in which he made clear when and where they must all assemble under arms.

Having arranged these matters, he broke camp and advanced, and pitched camp

At about this same time Euripidas, who had two companies of Eleans together with the pirates and mercenaries—so that the whole force amounted to about two thousand two hundred men, and along with these a hundred horsemen—set out from Psophis and made his march through Pheneus and Stymphalia. He knew nothing of Philip's movements, but intended to overrun the territory of the Sicyonians.

On the very night during which Philip happened to be encamping near the temple of the Dioscuri, Euripidas passed by the king's camp and was on the point of breaking into Sicyonian territory around dawn.

But some of Philip's Cretans, who had left their ranks and were ranging about in search of plunder, fell in with Euripidas and his men.

He questioned them, and so learned of the presence of the Macedonians. Without revealing to anyone any of what had happened, he took up his force, turned about, and withdrew again by the same road along which he had come,

wishing—and indeed hoping—to outstrip the Macedonians by getting through Stymphalia and reaching the difficult country that lay beyond it.
