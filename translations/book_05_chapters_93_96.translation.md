# Book 5 Chapter 93: Condition of Megalopolis

Having settled these matters, Aratus also resolved the disputes among the Megalopolitans with one another in accordance with the decree of the Achaeans.

For it happened that these people, having recently been ruined by Cleomenes and—as the saying goes—shaken from their very foundations as a state, were in need of many things and short of everything.

In their spirit they held firm, but in their resources, both public and private, they were unable to meet any demand.

For this reason everything was full of quarreling, rivalry, and mutual anger; for this is exactly what tends to happen, both in public affairs and in private lives, whenever resources fall short of each person's ambitions.

First, then, they disputed over the walling of the city. One party argued that it ought to be contracted and made only as large as they could realistically manage to wall and could defend when a crisis arose; for in the recent disaster, they said, it was precisely the city's size and its emptiness that had brought about their ruin.

In addition to this, they thought the landowners ought to contribute a third of their land toward settling the new inhabitants who were to be enrolled.

The other party would neither agree to make the city smaller nor consent to contribute a third of their holdings.

But above all they quarreled with one another over the laws drafted for them by Prytanis, whom Antigonus had given them as their lawgiver, and who was one of the distinguished men of the Peripatetic school and of that philosophical persuasion.

Such being the nature of the dispute, Aratus gave it all the attention he could and put an end to their contentiousness.

The terms on which they ended their differences with one another they inscribed on a stele and set up beside the altar of Hestia in the Homarium.

# Book 5 Chapter 94: Aetolian Raids

After settling these disputes, Aratus broke camp and came in person to the assembly of the Achaeans, while he handed the mercenaries over to Lycus of Pharae, since this man was at that time the deputy commander of the local confederate district.

But the Eleans, dissatisfied with Pyrrhias, once again brought in Euripidas as their general from the Aetolians.

He, watching for the time of the Achaean assembly, took sixty cavalry and two thousand infantry, set out, and passing through the territory of Pharae, overran the country as far as the district of Aegium.

After rounding up a considerable amount of plunder, he began his withdrawal in the direction of Leontium.

But Lycus and his men, on learning what had happened, came to the rescue in haste; engaging the enemy and joining battle on the spur of the moment, they cut down about four hundred of them and took about two hundred alive,

among whom were the notable men Physsias, Antanor, Clearchus, Androlochus, Euanoridas, Aristogeiton, Nicasippus, and Aspasius; and they captured all their arms and baggage.

At about the same time the Achaean admiral, having made an expedition to Molycria, returned with little short of a hundred captives.

Then, turning back again, he sailed to Chalceia and captured, from those who sailed out to oppose him, two warships together with their crews; and near Rhium he also took an Aetolian cutter along with its complement.

Since the spoils both by land and by sea came in around the same time, and a sufficient revenue and supply of provisions was gathered from them, the soldiers grew confident about receiving their pay, and the cities grew hopeful that they would not be burdened by levies.

# Book 5 Chapter 95: More Raids

At the same time as the events just described, Scerdilaidas, believing himself wronged by the king because some of the money agreed upon under the terms he had arranged with Philip was still owed to him, sent out fifteen galleys, undertaking the recovery of the money by means of a trick.

These sailed to Leucas, where everyone received them as friends on account of the joint operations they had previously carried out together.

Now, they had no time to do any other harm, nor were they able to; but against Agathinus and Cassander, the Corinthians who were sailing in Taurion's ships and had anchored alongside them as friends with four vessels, they treacherously launched an attack, and seizing the men together with their ships, they sent them off to Scerdilaidas.

After this they put out from Leucas and, sailing toward Malea, plundered and carried off the merchants they met.

As harvest time was now approaching,

and as Taurion and his men were neglecting the protection of the cities just mentioned, Aratus, with his picked troops, kept watch over the gathering of the grain around the territory of Argos,

while Euripidas, with his Aetolians, set out, intending to ravage the territory of the Tritaeans.

But Lycus and Demodocus, the Achaean cavalry commander, on learning of the Aetolians' expedition out of Elis, gathered the men of Dymae, Patrae, and Pharae, and together with these and the mercenaries, invaded Elean territory.

Arriving at the place called Phyxium, they sent forward their light-armed troops and cavalry to raid the countryside, but concealed their heavy infantry around the aforesaid place.

When the Eleans sallied out in full force against the raiders and pressed hard upon them as they withdrew, Lycus and his men rose up and fell upon those who had rushed forward.

The Eleans did not withstand the charge, but turned and fled at the mere sight of them; the Achaeans killed about two hundred of them, took eighty alive, and brought back in safety the plunder that had been driven off.

At the same time the Achaean admiral, making repeated landings on both Calydonia and the territory of Naupactus, ravaged the countryside and twice shattered the force sent to oppose him.

He also captured Cleonicus of Naupactus, who, because he was a proxenus of the Achaeans, was not sold off on the spot, and after some time was released without ransom.

# Book 5 Chapter 96: Agetas Leads the Aetolians Against Acarnania

About the same time Agetas, the general of the Aetolians, having called up the Aetolians in a general levy, plundered the territory of the Acarnanians and proceeded to ravage all of Epirus without hindrance.

Having accomplished this, he returned and dismissed the Aetolians to their cities.

The Acarnanians, making a counter-invasion into the territory of Stratus, were overcome by a panic and returned in disgrace, though without any loss, since the people of Stratus did not dare to pursue them, supposing that their withdrawal was being made to lure them into an ambush.

A case of counter-treachery also occurred at Phanoteus, in the following manner. Alexander, who had been put in charge of Phocis by Philip, organized an operation against the Aetolians through a certain Jason, who happened to have been placed by him in command of the city of Phanoteus.

This Jason sent word to Agetas, the general of the Aetolians, and agreed to hand over to them the citadel at Phanoteus, and concerning this he swore oaths and made a compact.

When the appointed day arrived, Agetas came by night to Phanoteus with his Aetolians, and after hiding the rest at a distance, he stayed back, while selecting a hundred of the fittest men he sent them to the citadel.

Jason, meanwhile, had Alexander ready with soldiers inside the city, and taking the young men in accordance with the oaths, he led them all up into the acropolis.

When Alexander and his men at once burst in upon them, the picked Aetolians were captured; and Agetas, when daylight came and he understood what had happened, led his force back again, having fallen victim to a stratagem not unlike the ones he himself had often employed.
