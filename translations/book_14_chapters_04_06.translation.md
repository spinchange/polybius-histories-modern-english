# Book 14 Chapter 4: The Romans Burn the Enemy Camp

When everything needed for the present operation was ready, he left in the camp men sufficient and suitable for its defense, took up his forces, and advanced just as the first watch was ending, for the enemy were about sixty stades away.

When he drew near the enemy, around the close of the third watch, he assigned half the soldiers and all the Numidians to Gaius Laelius and Massanissa, ordering them to attack Syphax’s palisade,

and exhorting them to prove themselves brave men and do nothing rashly, knowing clearly that, in proportion as darkness hinders and blocks the use of sight, night attacks require that one make up the loss by intelligence and courage.

He himself took the rest of the army and advanced against Hasdrubal. His calculation was not to begin the attack until Laelius and his force had first thrown fire among the enemy.

So, with these intentions, he advanced at a measured pace. Meanwhile the force under Laelius divided itself into two parts and attacked the enemy at the same time.

Since the arrangement of the huts was, as I said earlier, almost as though purposely designed for burning, the moment the leading men cast in the fire and it spread among the first tents, the disaster at once became uncontrollable because the huts stood so close together and because of the mass of combustible material beneath them.

Laelius stayed behind in reserve formation; but Massanissa, knowing the places by which those fleeing the fire were likely to retreat, stationed his soldiers at those points.

None of the Numidians had the least suspicion of what was happening—not even Syphax himself—but they took it to be an accidental fire in the palisade.

So some sprang from sleep without any suspicion, while others were still drinking and half drunk as they rushed from the huts.

Many were trampled down by their own people at the camp exits; many, hemmed in by the blaze, were burned alive; and those who did escape the flames all fell into the hands of the enemy and were destroyed, knowing neither what they were suffering nor what they were doing.

# Book 14 Chapter 5: Dreadful Scene In the Burning Camps

At this same moment the Carthaginians, seeing the mass of the fire and the greatness of the flame leaping up, supposed that the Numidians’ palisade had caught fire by accident. Some of them therefore ran straight out to help,

while all the rest poured unarmed out of their camp and gathered before their own encampment, astonished at what was happening.

Scipio, since events were advancing exactly as he could have wished, fell upon those who had come out, killing some and, by chasing others back, at the same time throwing fire into the huts.

Once this happened, the Phoenicians suffered under the fire and the whole surrounding confusion much the same fate as I have just described in the case of the Numidians.

Hasdrubal and those with him at once gave up trying to fight the fire, recognizing from what was happening that the disaster among the Numidians too had not arisen accidentally, as they had thought, but from the enemy’s attack and daring.

So they turned to saving themselves, though even in this there remained only a slight hope.

For the fire spread rapidly and took hold everywhere, while the passages were clogged with horses, pack animals, and men—some half dead and ruined by the fire, others frenzied with terror and driven out of their senses.

Thus these things became obstacles even to those resolved to fight bravely, and amid the panic and confusion escape was hopeless.

The case of Syphax and the other commanders was much the same. Yet both of the principal leaders managed to save themselves with a few horsemen.

But the remaining myriads of men, horses, and pack animals perished miserably and pitifully in the flames.

Some of the men, fleeing the force of the fire, met an ugly and shameful end at the hands of the enemy, being slaughtered naked, deprived not only of their weapons but even of their clothes.

In general the whole place was full of lamentation, disorderly shouting, fear, and a bewildering crash of noises, along with active fire and overwhelming flame.

Any one of these things by itself would have been enough to strike terror into human nature, much more when all of them came together in so astounding a fashion.

Therefore no one alive could, even by exaggeration, adequately match what happened, so completely did this disaster surpass all the events mentioned before in sheer horror.

For that reason, although Scipio accomplished many great and noble deeds, this seems to me the finest and boldest of all his achievements.

# Book 14 Chapter 6: Anda Surrenders to Rome

Yet when day came, and the enemy were either destroyed or in headlong flight, he exhorted the tribunes and at once pressed on in pursuit.

At first the Carthaginian commander stood his ground, even after receiving news of Scipio’s advance, because he trusted in the strength of the city.

But afterward, seeing that the inhabitants were in revolt, and terrified by Scipio’s approach, he fled with those who had escaped—no fewer than five hundred cavalry and about two thousand infantry.

The native inhabitants, now of one mind, put themselves in Roman hands.

Publius spared them, but he allowed the two neighboring cities to be plundered by the troops; and when he had accomplished this, he returned again to his original camp.

The Carthaginians, since their hopes had turned out contrary to the plans with which they had begun, took what had happened very hard.

For they had expected to shut up the Romans on the headland near Utica, where they had spent the winter, and to besiege them there, using their land forces on the one side and their naval forces on the other; and for this purpose they had prepared everything.

But now they found that not only had they unexpectedly and absurdly yielded the open country to the enemy, they were also brought to expect an imminent danger for themselves and their fatherland; therefore they were utterly stricken and panic-filled in spirit.

Still, since circumstances compelled them to make some provision and take counsel for the future, the council chamber was filled with bewilderment and with varied, troubled schemes.

Some said that they must send for Hannibal and recall him from Italy, since their one remaining hope lay in that general and the forces under him.

Others urged that envoys should be sent to Publius to discuss an armistice and speak about peace terms and a treaty, while others said they must keep heart, gather their forces, and send to Syphax.

For, they said, he had withdrawn nearby to Abba and was gathering together those who had escaped the danger. In the end this last opinion prevailed.
