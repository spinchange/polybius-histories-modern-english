# Book 3 Chapter 64: Scipio Crosses the Ticinus

Around the same days Publius, having now crossed the river Padus and having decided to advance still farther by crossing the Ticinus, ordered the men skilled in such work to build a bridge over the latter river, while he gathered the rest of his forces together and addressed them.

Most of what he said concerned the prestige of their homeland and the achievements of their ancestors, but the part bearing on the present moment ran as follows.

He told them that, even though they had as yet gained no actual experience of the enemy, the mere fact that they knew they were about to face Carthaginians in battle should make their hope of victory beyond all dispute.

And in general he said it would be an outrageous and absurd thing if the Carthaginians dared to look the Romans in the eye—men who had often been beaten by them, who had paid them heavy tribute over and over, and who for so many years now had been all but their slaves.

"And when, quite apart from all this, we have already had some experience of these very men now before us—namely that they do not dare to face us head-on—what conclusion should those who reason correctly draw about what is to come?

What is more, their cavalry, when it clashed with our horsemen at the river Rhone, came off badly: they lost many of their own men and fled in disgrace all the way back to their own camp.

And their general together with his whole army, the moment they learned that our soldiers were near, made a retreat that was little better than a flight, and out of fear, against their own intentions, resorted to the march over the Alps.

Hannibal is here now, he said, having destroyed the greater part of his army, while what remains is feeble and unfit for service because of the hardships they have suffered; likewise he has lost the greater part of his horses and rendered the rest useless owing to the length and difficulty of the journey.

By all this he tried to show that they had only to make their appearance before the enemy.

Above all he urged them to take heart by looking to his own presence among them: for he would never have left his fleet and his campaign in Iberia, the task he had been sent to carry out, and come here in such haste, had he not seen very clearly, on rational grounds, that this action was both necessary for his country and certain to bring victory.

Since all the men, both from their trust in the speaker and from the truth of what he said, were eager for the battle, he welcomed their enthusiasm and dismissed them, urging them in addition to be ready for whatever order should be passed along.

# Book 3 Chapter 65: Cavalry Engagement on the Ticinus

On the very next day both sides advanced along the river on the side toward the Alps, the Romans keeping the stream on their left and the Carthaginians on their right.

On the second day, learning through their foraging parties that they were close to one another, they encamped where they were and stayed there for the time being.

On the following day both sides took all their cavalry—and Publius also the javelin-throwers from among his infantry—and advanced across the plain, eager to scout out each other's forces.

As soon as they drew near and saw the dust rising up, they at once began to form up for battle.

Publius, placing his javelin-throwers and the Gallic cavalry that accompanied them in front, and stationing the rest in a line abreast, advanced at a walking pace.

Hannibal, drawing up his bridled cavalry and all the steadier part of his horse facing the front, went to meet the enemy, while he held the Numidian cavalry ready on each wing for an encircling movement.

Since both the commanders and the horsemen on each side were keen for the contest, the first clash turned out to be such that the javelin-throwers did not even manage to let fly their first missile, but reeled back and fled at once through the gaps to the rear of their own squadrons, panicked by the charge and terrified of being trampled by the cavalry bearing down on them.

Those who met one another face to face kept the contest evenly balanced for a long time;

for it was at once a cavalry battle and an infantry battle, because of the number of men who dismounted in the very midst of the fighting.

But when the Numidians had encircled them and fallen on them from behind, the foot javelin-throwers, who at first had escaped the cavalry clash, were now trampled down by the mass and the onslaught of the Numidians;

while those who from the start had been fighting the Carthaginians face to face—having lost many of their own men, but having destroyed still more of the Carthaginians—now, attacked from the rear by the Numidians, were put to flight: most of them scattered, but some rallied around their commander.

# Book 3 Chapter 66: Scipio Crosses the Po

Publius then broke camp and advanced across the plains toward the bridge over the Padus, in haste to get his legions across first.

For seeing that the ground was level, that the enemy was superior in cavalry, and that he himself was weighed down by his wound, he judged it necessary to bring his forces back to safety.

Hannibal for a while supposed that they would risk a battle with their infantry too; but when he saw that they had moved out of their camp, he followed as far as the first river and the bridge over it.

Finding that most of the planks had been torn up, but that the men guarding the bridge were still left behind near the river, he took these prisoner—about six hundred of them in number.

Then, hearing that the rest had already gotten a long way ahead, he turned about again in the opposite direction and made his march along the river, hurrying to reach a point on the Padus where it could easily be bridged.

On the second day he halted and bridged the crossing with river boats, and assigned to Hasdrubal the task of bringing the main body across, while he himself crossed at once and dealt with the envoys who had arrived from the neighboring districts.

For as soon as he had gained the advantage, all the neighboring Celts hastened, in keeping with their original intention, to become his friends, to supply him, and to join the Carthaginians on campaign.

After receiving those who had come in a friendly manner and bringing his forces over from the far side, he advanced along the river, taking the opposite direction to his earlier march: for he now made his way downstream, eager to come to grips with the enemy.

Publius, having crossed the Padus and encamped near the city of Placentia, which was a Roman colony, attended at once to himself and the other wounded, and at the same time, considering that he had brought his forces into a safe position, kept quiet.

Hannibal, arriving on the second day after his crossing near the enemy, on the third day drew up his army in full view of his opponents.

But when no one came out against him, he pitched camp, leaving an interval of about fifty stades between the two armies.
