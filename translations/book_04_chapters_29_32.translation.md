# Book 4 Chapter 29: The Illyrians and Acarnanians Join

While wintering in Macedonia, Philip carefully enrolled his forces for the campaign ahead, and at the same time secured his position against the barbarians who lived above Macedonia.

After this he met with Scerdilaidas and, boldly placing himself in his hands, discussed terms of friendship and alliance with him.

Partly by promising to help him set in order his affairs in Illyria, and partly by denouncing the Aetolians—who gave ample grounds for denunciation—he easily persuaded him to agree to what he proposed.

For private wrongdoing differs in no real way from public wrongdoing, but only in the number and scale of what results from it. Indeed, in private life the tribe of cheats and thieves comes to ruin chiefly in this way: by failing to deal justly with one another, and in short through their treacheries toward each other. And this is exactly what happened in the case of the Aetolians at that time.

For they had agreed to give Scerdilaidas a share of the plunder if he would join them in invading Achaea; and when he had been won over and had done so,

they sacked the city of the Cynaetheans, and after driving off many captives and herds of cattle, they made Scerdilaidas no sharer at all in what had been taken.

Because of this, resentment was already smouldering in him, so that with only a brief reminder from Philip he quickly gave his assent and agreed to join the common alliance, on these terms: that he should receive twenty talents a year, and should put to sea with thirty galleys and wage war against the Aetolians by sea.

# Book 4 Chapter 30: The Acarnanians Enter the War

So Philip was occupied with these matters. Meanwhile the envoys who had been sent out to the allies came first to Acarnania and met with the people there.

The Acarnanians sincerely confirmed the decree on their part and carried the war from their own territory against the Aetolians—even though they, if anyone, would have been justified in being excused for postponing it, hesitating, and in general fearing a war with their neighbours.

For they lay directly alongside the Aetolians, sharing a common border with their country; and still more because, on their own, they were easy to overpower; and, most of all, because only a short time before they had had a taste of the most dreadful sufferings on account of their hostility with the Aetolians.

But it seems to me that men of genuine character, both in public and in private affairs, never set anything above their duty; and this principle the Acarnanians, on most occasions, are found to have maintained as well as any of the Greeks, even though they set out from but slender resources.

With such people one should not hesitate to share in undertakings when circumstances demand it, but should rather be eager to do so, more than with any other of the Greeks; for both in private and in public life they have something steadfast and devoted to liberty about them.

The Epirotes, by contrast, after listening to the envoys, confirmed the decree in much the same way, and voted to wage war against the Aetolians—but only once King Philip too should have begun it.

To the envoys who came from the Aetolians, however, they replied that the Epirotes had resolved to maintain peace with them—conducting their affairs in an ignoble and double-dealing manner.

Envoys were also sent to King Ptolemy to urge him neither to send money to the Aetolians nor to furnish them with any other aid against Philip and the allies.

# Book 4 Chapter 31: Mistaken Policy of the Messenians

As for the Messenians—on whose account the war had begun in the first place—they answered those who had come to them that, since Phigalia lay on their borders and was subject to the Aetolians, they would not enter into the war until that city had been wrested away from the Aetolians.

This decision was forced through, though the common people were by no means pleased with it, by the ephors Oenis and Nicippus and certain others of the oligarchical party, who feared the boldness of the Aetolians—men who, in my judgement at least, were gravely mistaken and wide of the mark of what was needed.

For I maintain that, while war is indeed a fearful thing, it is not so fearful that one should endure anything whatever in order to avoid taking it up.

Otherwise, why do we all make so bold with our talk of equality before the law, freedom of speech, and the very name of liberty, if nothing is to count for more than peace?

We do not, after all, praise the Thebans for their conduct in the Persian Wars, because out of fear they withdrew from the dangers undertaken on behalf of Greece and chose the side of the Persians; nor do we praise Pindar, who joined them in counselling that they keep quiet, in these verses:

"Let a man set the common good of the citizens in fair weather, and seek out the bright light of high-hearted peace." For though at the moment he seemed to have spoken persuasively,

it was not long before his pronouncement was found to be the most shameful and most harmful of all;

for peace, when it goes with justice and honour, is the fairest and most profitable of possessions, but when it goes with baseness or disgraceful cowardice it is the most shameful and most ruinous of all things.

# Book 4 Chapter 32: The Messenians Too Much Inclined to Peace

The oligarchs who led the Messenians, aiming at their own immediate private advantage, were always more eager for peace than was right.

Because of this, although they had many opportunities and favourable moments, they let them slip, and at times escaped from fears and dangers as well; but along with this policy the reckoning against them was always mounting up, and in the end they made their country wrestle with the gravest disasters.

For my own part, I think the cause was this: that they bordered on two of the most powerful peoples in the Peloponnese—indeed almost in all of Greece—I mean the Arcadians and the Laconians,

of whom the one had been hostile and irreconcilable toward them ever since they had occupied the land, while the other was friendly and protective; yet they neither nobly took up their enmity against the Lacedaemonians nor their friendship with the Arcadians.

The result was that whenever these peoples were preoccupied in war against one another or against others, things went well for the Messenians; for they always enjoyed peace and fine weather, as it were, because their country lay off to one side.

But whenever the Lacedaemonians, having leisure and nothing to distract them, turned to doing them harm,

the Messenians were neither able by themselves to look the weight of Lacedaemon in the face, nor had they prepared in advance true friends who would stand by them in everything; and so they were forced either to be slaves to the Lacedaemonians, bearing their burdens, or, in fleeing that slavery, to be driven from their homes and made fugitives, abandoning their country together with their children and wives—

which is just what has now befallen them more than once, and not so very long ago.

Now it would be best if the present state of affairs among the Peloponnesians could grow together into something permanent, so that there should be no need of what I am about to say.

But should these arrangements ever undergo upheaval and change, I see only one hope for the Messenians and the Megalopolitans of being able to keep possession of their own land for the longer term: namely, that they come to a common mind in accordance with the judgement of Epaminondas, and choose to share with one another truly in every crisis and every undertaking.
