# Book 2 Chapter 8: Queen Teuta and Rome

This much I judged worth recording. Now the Illyrians had, even in earlier times, persistently preyed upon ships sailing from Italy.

During the period when they were operating around Phoenice, a good number of them broke away from the main fleet and fell upon many of the Italian merchants: some they merely robbed, others they slaughtered, and not a few of those they captured they carried off alive.

The Romans had until then turned a deaf ear to those who lodged complaints against the Illyrians; but now, when still more people came before the Senate on the matter, they appointed envoys to go to Illyria and look into the grievances described—Gaius and Lucius Coruncanius.

When the galleys returned to Teuta from Epirus, she was struck by the sheer quantity and the beauty of the spoils they brought back—for Phoenice was at that time by far the most prosperous of the cities of Epirus—and so she was doubly emboldened to press on with her depredations against the Greeks.

For the moment, however, she held back because of disturbances at home; but once she had quickly settled affairs with the Illyrians who had revolted, she laid siege to Issa, since it alone still refused to submit to her.

It was at this very time that the Roman envoys arrived. Granted an audience, they set out the wrongs that had been done to their citizens.

Throughout the entire discussion Teuta listened to them in an arrogant and thoroughly overbearing manner.

When they had finished speaking, she replied that, as a matter of public policy, she would try to see to it that no wrong was done to the Romans by the Illyrians; but that, as a private matter, it was not lawful for the kings of Illyria to bar the Illyrians from the profits to be had at sea.

The younger of the envoys, offended at this answer, spoke out with a frankness that was fitting in itself but in no way suited to the moment. For he said:

"Romans, Teuta, have a most excellent custom of using the state to punish private wrongs and to come to the aid of those who have been wronged.

We shall therefore endeavor, God willing, to compel you—and that soon and emphatically—to reform the royal practices of Illyria."

Receiving this frankness with a woman's temper and without reflection, she was so enraged at what had been said that, disregarding the rights established among all mankind, she sent men after the envoys as they sailed away with orders to kill the one who had spoken so frankly.

When news of this reached Rome, the Romans were incensed at the woman's lawless act and at once set about their preparations, enrolling legions and assembling a fleet.

# Book 2 Chapter 9: Teuta Sends Out Another Fleet

When the sailing season came round, Teuta fitted out more galleys than before and sent them off once again toward the regions of Greece.

Some of them made their crossing by the open sea straight for Corcyra, while a portion put in at the harbor of Epidamnus—ostensibly to take on water and provisions, but in reality to make an attempt on the city by treachery.

The Epidamnians received them without suspicion and carelessly, and the Illyrians went in wearing nothing but their loincloths, as though to fetch water, but with daggers hidden in the water-jars. In this way they cut down the men guarding the gate and quickly seized the gatehouse.

When, according to plan, reinforcements arrived in force from the ships, they let these men in and easily held most of the walls.

The people of the city, though caught unprepared by the surprise, came to the rescue with spirit and fought it out, so that in the end the Illyrians, after holding their ground for a long while, were driven out of the city.

So on this occasion the Epidamnians, through their negligence, came near to losing their homeland, but through their courage came off unharmed and the wiser for the future.

The Illyrian commanders then put to sea in haste, joined the squadron that had sailed ahead, and made land at Corcyra. There they made a terrifying landing and set about besieging the city.

At this turn of events the Corcyraeans, hard-pressed and despairing of their whole situation, sent envoys to the Achaeans and the Aetolians—and along with them the Apollonians and Epidamnians did the same—begging them to come to their aid quickly and not to stand by while they were driven from their homes by the Illyrians.

The Achaeans and Aetolians, having heard the envoys and granted their plea, jointly manned the ten decked ships of the Achaeans, and within a few days had them fitted out and sailed for Corcyra,

# Book 2 Chapter 10: Illyrian Victories

hoping to break the siege. But the Illyrians, having taken on seven decked ships supplied by the Acarnanians under the terms of their alliance, put out to meet them and engaged the Achaean vessels off the islands called Paxi.

Now the Acarnanians, and the Achaean ships posted opposite them, kept the fight even and came through the clashes unscathed, apart from wounds among the men themselves.

The Illyrians, however, lashed their own galleys together in groups of four and so closed with the enemy. They paid no regard to their own vessels but threw them in broadside and so worked to assist the enemy's ramming charges.

And when the enemy ships, having rammed and become locked in by the impact, were for the moment in difficulty—with the lashed-together galleys hanging on around their rams—the Illyrians then leapt onto the decks of the Achaean ships and overpowered them by sheer weight of numbers.

In this way they captured four four-banked ships, and sank one five-banked ship with all hands aboard—the ship on which Margos of Caryneia was sailing, a man who had served the common government of the Achaeans with complete integrity right up to his death.

Those who were fighting the Acarnanians, seeing the advantage the Illyrians had gained and trusting in their own swiftness, ran before the wind and made their withdrawal home in safety.

The Illyrian host, emboldened by their success, now pressed the siege with ease and full confidence.

The Corcyraeans, despairing of their whole situation after what had happened, held out under the siege only a little while longer and then came to terms with the Illyrians, accepting a garrison and, with the garrison, Demetrius of Pharos.

Once this had been arranged, the Illyrian commanders immediately put to sea; and, putting in at Epidamnus, set about besieging that city once again.

# Book 2 Chapter 11: Corcyra Submits To the Romans

At this same period, of the men holding the consulships, Gnaeus Fulvius set sail from Rome with two hundred ships, while Aulus Postumius set out with the land forces.

Gnaeus's first intention was to sail to Corcyra, supposing that he would still find the outcome of the siege undecided.

Although he came too late for that, he nevertheless sailed on to the island, wishing both to learn clearly what had happened concerning the city and to test the proposals being conveyed by Demetrius.

For Demetrius, being under suspicion and in fear of Teuta, was sending messages to the Romans, offering to hand over the city and to surrender the other affairs of which he was in charge.

The Corcyraeans, delighted at the arrival of the Romans, surrendered the Illyrian garrison with Demetrius's approval, and of their own accord, on being invited, gave themselves over wholeheartedly into the protection of Rome—judging this to be their one safeguard for the future against the lawlessness of the Illyrians.

The Romans, having received the Corcyraeans into their friendship, sailed for Apollonia, taking Demetrius as their guide for the remaining operations.

At the same time Postumius was ferrying his land forces across from Brundisium—some twenty thousand foot and about two thousand horse.

As soon as both forces had converged together upon Apollonia, and the Apollonians too had welcomed them and given themselves into Roman trust, they at once put to sea again on hearing that Epidamnus was under siege.

The Illyrians, learning of the Romans' approach, broke up the siege in complete disorder and fled.

The Romans, taking the Epidamnians too under their protection, advanced into the interior of Illyria, subduing the Ardiaei as they went.

When many envoys came to meet them—among them those from the Parthini, offering an unconditional surrender—they received these into their friendship; and likewise those who came from the Atintanes. They then advanced toward Issa, since that city too was being besieged by the Illyrians.

On reaching it and breaking the siege, they received the Issaeans as well into their protection.

In the course of their voyage along the coast they also stormed certain Illyrian cities; and at one of these, Nutria, they lost not only many soldiers but also some of the military tribunes and the quaestor.

They likewise captured twenty galleys that were carrying off the plunder from the countryside.

Of those besieging Issa, the men from Pharos were left unharmed for Demetrius's sake, while all the rest scattered and fled to Arbo.

Teuta herself, with a very few companions, escaped to Rhizon, a little town well fortified for strength, set back from the sea and lying right on the river Rhizon.

Having accomplished this, and having placed most of the Illyrians under Demetrius and invested him with great power, the Romans withdrew to Epidamnus with their fleet and their land army.

# Book 2 Chapter 12: Teuta Agrees to Pay Tribute to Rome

Gnaeus Fulvius, then, sailed back to Rome with the greater part of both the naval and the land forces.

Postumius stayed behind with forty ships, raised a legion from the surrounding cities, and wintered there, keeping watch over the Ardiaei and the other peoples who had given themselves into Roman trust.

About the spring season Teuta sent envoys to the Romans and concluded a treaty, in which she agreed to pay the tribute that had been imposed, to withdraw from all Illyria except for a few places, and—the crucial point, which most concerned the Greeks—not to sail beyond Lissus with more than two galleys, and those unarmed.

Once these terms had been settled, Postumius afterward dispatched envoys to the Aetolians and to the Achaean nation. On their arrival, they first explained the grounds for the war and for the crossing, then went through in order what had been accomplished and read out the treaty they had made with the Illyrians.

Having met with a fitting courtesy from each of the two nations, they then sailed back to Corcyra, having freed the Greeks from a considerable fear by means of the aforesaid treaty.

For it so happened that the Illyrians were at that time the common enemies not of some peoples only, but of all alike.

Such, then, was the first crossing of the Romans with an army into Illyria and these parts of Europe, along with their first involvement, by way of an embassy, in the affairs of Greece—and these were the causes that brought it about.

From this beginning the Romans straightaway sent yet other envoys to the Corinthians and to the Athenians; and it was then that the Corinthians first admitted the Romans to take part in the Isthmian Games.

Concerning these Celts, it seems to me useful to give an account in summary form, so as to keep intact the proper purpose of my introductory survey in accordance with my original design, and to go back in time to the beginning—to the point at which the people just mentioned first occupied the country.

For I consider the history of these matters not only worth knowing and remembering, but absolutely essential, in order to learn what men and what places Hannibal afterward relied upon when he set himself to break the power of Rome.

First I must speak about the country—what sort it is and how it lies in relation to the rest of Italy. For in this way it will be easier to grasp the distinctive features of the events as well, once the peculiarities of the localities and of the land have been sketched out.

Italy as a whole is triangular in shape. One of its sides, the one inclined toward the east, is bounded by the Ionian strait and, continuous with it, by the Adriatic gulf; the side facing south and west is bounded by the Sicilian and Tyrrhenian seas.

These two sides, meeting one another, form the apex of the triangle—the promontory of Italy that juts out toward the south, which is called Cocynthus and divides the Ionian strait from the Sicilian sea.

The remaining side, which stretches along the north and the interior, is bounded throughout by the foothills of the Alps. This range takes its beginning from Massalia and the region above the Sardinian sea, and runs on continuously as far as the head of the whole Adriatic, falling short only by a little where it stops before reaching it.
