# Book 3 Chapter 112: The Armies Face To Face

On the following day he ordered everyone to busy themselves with their equipment and with getting into condition. The day after that he drew up his troops along the river and made it clear that he was eager to fight the enemy.

Lucius Aemilius, however, was displeased with the ground, and since he could see that the Carthaginians would soon be forced to move camp in order to procure supplies, he kept quiet, securing his encampments with reserve guards.

Hannibal waited a considerable time, and when no one came out against him, he withdrew the rest of his army back into the entrenched camp, but loosed his Numidians against the men who were fetching water from the smaller camp.

When the Numidians charged right up to the rampart itself and prevented the watering, Gaius was provoked still further by this; the troops too were straining for battle and bore the postponements with impatience.

For waiting is the heaviest of all burdens to any man: but once a thing has been decided, then whatever one must suffer, even out of all the things reckoned terrible, must be endured.

When word reached Rome that the two armies were encamped facing each other and that clashes between the advance forces were taking place day after day, the city was on edge and full of fear,

for the mass of the people dreaded what was coming, because they had so often already been beaten before, and in their minds they foresaw and reckoned in advance what would happen if they were utterly defeated.

At that time all the oracles preserved among them were on everyone's lips; every temple and every house was full of portents and omens, so that vows and sacrifices and supplications and prayers to the gods filled the city.

For in times of crisis the Romans are formidable at propitiating both gods and men, and they consider nothing of the sort unseemly or beneath them in such circumstances.

# Book 3 Chapter 113: The Order of Battle

When Gaius took over the command on the next day in succession, just as the sun was beginning to rise, he set the army in motion from both camps at once.

Those from the larger camp he led across the river and at once drew up in line; joining to them those from the other camp, he arranged the whole force on the same straight line, making them all face south.

The Roman cavalry he stationed on the right wing, right beside the river; the infantry he extended continuously next to them along the same line, setting the maniples closer together than usual and making the depth of each company many times greater than its frontage.

The cavalry of the allies he posted on the left wing; and in front of the whole force he placed the light-armed troops, at a distance.

Together with the allies there were about eighty thousand infantry and a little more than six thousand cavalry.

At the same time Hannibal sent his Balearic slingers and javelin-men across the river and posted them in front of his force; the rest he led out of the camp and, bringing them over the stream at two places, drew them up against the enemy.

Right at the river, on his left, he placed the Iberian and Celtic cavalry facing the Roman cavalry; next to them, half of the Libyan infantry armed in the heavy fashion; next to these, the Iberians and Celts; beside them he placed the remaining portion of the Libyans; and on the right wing he posted the Numidian cavalry.

Once he had extended everything along a single line, he then took the central units of the Iberians and Celts and pushed them forward, arranging the other units next to them in proportionate gradation, so that the line formed a crescent-shaped bulge and grew thin in its own formation,

his intention being that the Libyans should hold the position of a reserve in the battle, while the Iberians and Celts bore the brunt of the first fighting.

# Book 3 Chapter 114: Arms of the Libyans, Iberians, and Celts

The Libyans' equipment was Roman, for Hannibal had armed them all with a selection from the spoils taken in the earlier battle.

As for the Iberians and Celts, their shields were much alike, but their swords were of opposite design.

For the Iberian sword could thrust just as effectively as it could strike downward, whereas the Gallic blade had only one use, the downward cut, and that one only with room to swing.

Since their companies were drawn up alternately—the Celts naked, the Iberians arrayed in their national fashion in short linen tunics bordered with purple—the sight that resulted was at once strange and terrifying.

The whole strength of the Carthaginian cavalry came to about ten thousand, and that of the infantry to not much more than forty thousand, including the Celts. Aemilius held the Roman right,

Gaius the left, and the center was held by Marcus and Gnaeus, who had been the commanders of the previous year.

On the Carthaginian side, Hasdrubal held the left and Hanno the right, while at the center was Hannibal himself, with his brother Mago beside him.

Since the Roman line faced south, as I said above, and the Carthaginian line faced north, the rising sun did no harm to either of them.

# Book 3 Chapter 115: The Battle of Cannae

When the first clash had come about between the forward troops, at the outset the fighting among the light-armed themselves was evenly matched.

But as soon as the Iberian and Celtic cavalry on the left closed with the Romans, these men made the battle a real and barbaric one:

for the combat did not follow the rules, with wheeling and counter-maneuver, but once they had collided they fought grappling man against man, dismounting from their horses.

When the Carthaginian horsemen had won the upper hand and killed most of their opponents in the struggle—for the Romans all fought with passion and courage—and were driving the rest along the river, slaughtering them and laying hands on them without mercy, then the infantry lines took over from the light-armed and engaged one another.

For a short time the ranks of the Iberians and Celts held and fought the Romans gallantly; but after that, crushed by the weight, they gave way and fell back, breaking up the crescent.

The Roman maniples, following them eagerly, easily cut through the enemy's line, since the Celts had been drawn up thinly while the Romans had massed together from the wings toward the center and the point of danger.

For the wings and the center did not come into action at the same time; rather the center engaged first, because the Celts, having been arranged in a crescent formation, projected far ahead of the wings, since the convex of the crescent faced the enemy.

But as the Romans pursued these troops and crowded in toward the center and the yielding part of the enemy, they pressed forward so far that on either side, along their exposed flanks, the Libyans in their heavy armor came up against them:

those on the right wing wheeled to the left and, charging from the spear side, took position alongside the enemy's flank,

while those on the left made their turn to the right and charged in from the shield side—the situation itself showing them what had to be done.

The result, just as Hannibal had foreseen, was that the Romans were cut off in the middle by the Libyans, caught there by their headlong pursuit of the Celts.
