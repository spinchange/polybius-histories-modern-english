# Book 1 Chapter 15: Misrepresentations of Philinus and Fabius

The truth of what I have said can be seen from the following examples. Philinus, at the very beginning of his narrative and of his second book, states that the Carthaginians and the Syracusans were jointly besieging Messene, having sat down before it for war.

He says that when the Romans arrived by sea and entered the city, they at once marched out against the Syracusans; that after taking heavy losses they fell back into Messene; and that when they next went out against the Carthaginians, they not only suffered heavy losses but also lost a considerable number of their soldiers taken alive.

Having said this, he then claims that after the engagement Hiero so far lost his head that he not only immediately burned his palisade and tents and fled by night to Syracuse, but also abandoned every one of the forts that had been positioned to hold down Messenian territory.

In the same way, he says, the Carthaginians too, right after the battle, abandoned their palisade, split up among the various towns, and no longer dared even to contest the open country; and that, in consequence, their commanders, seeing that their men had been thoroughly demoralized, resolved not to decide the issue by battle.

The Romans, he goes on, followed them up, and not only ravaged the territory of the Carthaginians and Syracusans, but even sat down before Syracuse itself and set about besieging it.

All of this, as it seems to me, is utterly absurd, and needs no refutation whatsoever.

For the very men whom he first set up as besieging Messene and winning the engagements, he goes on to present as fleeing, withdrawing from the open country, and in the end being besieged and broken in spirit.

And those whom he first put forward as defeated and besieged, he ends by showing as pursuing the enemy, immediately gaining control of the open country, and finally besieging Syracuse.

These statements cannot possibly be reconciled with one another—how could they?—but it must be that either his opening assumptions are false, or else his account of what followed.

In fact, the latter are true: the Carthaginians and Syracusans did withdraw from the open country, and the Romans did press on the heels of the war against Syracuse, and—as he himself says—against Echetla too, which lies in the middle of the territory of the Syracusans and Carthaginians.

It remains, then, that we must admit that the premises and opening assumptions are false, and that, although the Romans were in fact victorious from the outset in the engagements around Messene, they have been reported to us by this writer as defeated.

One could find Philinus to be like this throughout the whole of his work, and Fabius likewise, as will be demonstrated in connection with the events themselves.

Now that I have said what is fitting about this digression, I will return to the events and try, by always adding the next part of the account, to bring my readers in brief compass to a true understanding of the war I have been describing.

# Book 1 Chapter 16: King Hiero and Rome

When news of the successes of Appius and the legions reached Rome from Sicily, the Romans appointed Manius Otacilius and Manius Valerius as consuls and dispatched their entire force, together with both generals, to Sicily.

The Romans have, all told and apart from the allies, four Roman legions, which they enroll each year; each of these consists of four thousand infantry and three hundred cavalry.

When these forces arrived, most of the cities revolted both from the Carthaginians and from the Syracusans, and went over to the Romans.

Hiero, observing the confusion and dismay of the Sicilians, and at the same time the numbers and crushing weight of the Roman legions, reasoned from all this that the prospects of the Romans were brighter than those of the Carthaginians.

Inclining therefore in his calculations toward this side, he sent messages to the consuls, proposing terms of peace and friendship.

The Romans accepted, chiefly on account of supplies.

For since the Carthaginians commanded the sea, they were anxious that they might be cut off on every side from their necessities—warned by the fact that the troops who had crossed earlier had run into severe shortage of provisions.

And so, supposing that Hiero would be of great service to them in this respect, they gladly accepted his friendship.

They made a treaty on these terms: that the king should return his prisoners to the Romans without ransom, and pay them in addition a hundred talents of silver. From then on the Romans treated the Syracusans as friends and allies.

King Hiero, having placed himself under the protection of the Romans and continually supplying them with what their pressing needs required, reigned securely over the Syracusans for the rest of his days, courting honor and reputation among the Greeks.

For of all such men he is thought to have been the most distinguished, and to have enjoyed the fruits of his own sound judgment for the longest time, both in particular affairs and in general.

# Book 1 Chapter 17: The Siege of Agrigentum

When the treaty was brought back to Rome, and the people had accepted and ratified the settlement with Hiero, the Romans decided no longer to send out all their forces, but only two legions.

For they reckoned that the war had been made lighter for them, now that the king had come over to their side, and at the same time they supposed that in this way their forces would be more easily kept supplied with necessities.

The Carthaginians, seeing that Hiero had become their enemy and that the Romans were getting more thoroughly entangled in Sicilian affairs, concluded that they needed a heavier force, one with which they would be able to face the enemy and to hold together their position in Sicily.

So they recruited mercenaries from the country across the sea—many Ligurians and Celts, and still more Iberians than these—and sent them all to Sicily.

Seeing that the city of the Agrigentines was excellently suited for their preparations and was at the same time the most important city in their own province, they gathered into it their supplies and forces, deciding to use this city as their base of operations for the war.

As for the Romans, the generals who had made the treaty with Hiero had withdrawn, and their successors, appointed in their place—Lucius Postumius and Quintus Mamilius—arrived in Sicily with the legions.

Observing the Carthaginian plan and the preparations around Agrigentum, they resolved to take the matter in hand more boldly.

So they set aside the other operations of the war, and bringing their whole army to bear directly against Agrigentum, they pressed upon it; encamping eight stades from the city, they shut the Carthaginians up inside the walls.

Since it was the height of the grain harvest, and the siege was clearly going to be a long one, the soldiers set out to gather corn more eagerly than they should have.

The Carthaginians, seeing the enemy scattered over the countryside, came out and attacked the foragers. Having routed these easily, some of them rushed to plunder the palisade, others against the supporting pickets.

But the difference in their institutions saved the Roman cause then, as it had often done before. For among the Romans the penalty is death for the man who deserts his post and flees outright from the pickets.

And so on this occasion too, although the enemy were many times their number, they stood their ground gallantly; and though they lost many of their own men, they killed still more of the enemy.

Finally, surrounding the enemy just as they were on the very point of tearing apart the palisade, they destroyed some of them, and the rest, pressing upon them and cutting them down, they drove together in flight into the city.

# Book 1 Chapter 18: Relief Comes from Agrigentum

After this it came about that the Carthaginians grew more cautious about making attacks, and the Romans more careful in their foraging.

Since the Carthaginians would no longer come out against them except as far as skirmishing, the Roman generals divided their force into two parts: with one they remained near the temple of Asclepius in front of the city, and with the other they encamped in the quarters of the city that incline toward Heracleia.

The intervals between the two camps, on either side of the city, they fortified. They threw up the inner trench as a protection against those sallying out of the city, and the outer one they ran around as a guard against attacks from outside and against the men and supplies that customarily slip in and make their way into besieged cities.

The spaces between the trenches and the camps they secured with guard posts, fortifying the convenient positions at intervals.

As for provisions and the rest of their supplies, all the other allies gathered them and brought them to Herbesus, and the Romans themselves, hauling and carrying continually from this town—which was not far off—kept up an abundant supply of their necessities.

So for about five months they remained in the same positions, neither side able to gain any decisive advantage over the other beyond what occurred in the skirmishes themselves.

But as the Carthaginians were being squeezed by famine, owing to the multitude of men shut up in the city—for they were no fewer than fifty thousand—Hannibal, the commander appointed over the besieged forces, now hard pressed by the situation, kept sending continual messages to Carthage, making clear their predicament and calling for help.

The authorities in Carthage loaded their ships with the soldiers and elephants they had assembled and sent them off to Sicily, to Hanno, the other general.

He gathered his supplies and forces at Heracleia, and as a first step seized by a stratagem the city of the Herbesians, depriving the enemy's camps of their markets and their supply of necessities.

As a result, the Romans found themselves at once besieging and besieged in their circumstances. For they were brought by the lack of grain and the scarcity of necessities to such a pass that they more than once deliberated about raising the siege.

And in the end they would indeed have done so, had not Hiero, applying every effort and device, kept them supplied with moderate and necessary provisions.
