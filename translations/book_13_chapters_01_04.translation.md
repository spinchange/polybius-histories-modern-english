# Book 13 Chapter 1: The Aetolians

I. Greek Affairs

Because of the unbroken succession of wars and the extravagance of their way of life, the Aetolians had slipped into debt without realizing it themselves, much less anyone else.

So, being naturally inclined toward a change in their own constitution, they chose Dorimachus and Scopas to draft laws for them,

thinking that these men were, by temperament, eager for upheaval and were also deeply entangled in many ordinary financial obligations. After receiving this authority, they wrote the laws.

# Book 13 Chapter 1a: Chapter 1a

Alexander the Aetolian opposed the legislation being drafted by Dorimachus and Scopas, showing by many examples that wherever this growth had once taken root, it never stopped until it had brought great disasters on those who had made use of it.

Therefore he urged them to look not only at the present—whether they would get relief from their current obligations—
but also at the future.

For, he said, it was absurd that men should be ready in war to give up even their breath for the safety of their children, yet when they were deliberating, take no thought at all for the time that would come afterward.

# Book 13 Chapter 2: Scopas Goes to Egypt

Scopas, the Aetolian general, having failed to win the office for the sake of which he had dared to draft those laws, turned expectantly toward Alexandria, persuaded that the prospects there would restore what was lacking in his livelihood and satisfy the greedy craving in his soul. He did not understand that,

just as in the case of people suffering from dropsy, supplying moisture from outside never brings an end or satiety to their thirst unless someone first heals the diseased condition within the body itself, so too it is impossible to satisfy the desire for more unless one first corrects, by reason, the vice rooted in the soul. This became especially clear in the case of the very man now under discussion.

For when he arrived in Alexandria, besides the profits from the countryside over which he had authority because he was entrusted with major affairs, the king also assigned him a daily allowance of ten minas, and to those ranked just below him in command, one mina a day.

Yet even this was not enough for him, since he had long trained himself to press for more and more,

until, because of his greed, he became hated even by those who were giving him these things, and at last lost his life together with his gold.

# Book 13 Chapter 3: Philip's Treacherous Conduct, B. C. 204

This was the kind of villainy that followed—a thing no one could call kingly, though some nowadays choose to describe it as necessary to practical statesmanship because of the bad faith now so widely prevalent.

The men of old were very far from behaving in such a way. They were so alien to plotting against friends for the sake of increasing their own power that they did not even choose to conquer enemies by deceit,

because they believed that no success was either glorious or secure unless a man, fighting openly, had defeated his opponents by breaking their spirit.

Therefore they even came to agreements with one another not to use hidden missiles or long-range weapons against each other, holding that the only true decision in war was a battle fought hand to hand at close quarters.

For the same reason they used to announce wars and even battles beforehand, naming both the time when they intended to hazard all and the places where they were going to draw up their armies.

But now people say that any general who carries out military business openly is a poor one.

Among the Romans there still remains a faint trace of the ancient view in matters of war: they proclaim their wars in advance, make only sparing use of ambushes, and fight hand to hand at close quarters.

Let this be said against the excessive zeal now fashionable among leaders, in both political and military administration, for underhanded methods beyond what is necessary.

# Book 13 Chapter 4: Philip Intrigues Against Rhodes

Philip gave Heracleides, as though setting him a problem, instructions to consider how he might injure and destroy the Rhodian fleet,

and at the same time sent envoys to Crete to stir up and incite war against the Rhodians.

Heracleides, a man naturally fitted for wickedness, regarded the order as a godsend; and after devising whatever schemes he could in his own mind, he set out after a time and sailed to Rhodes.

This Heracleides was by birth a Tarentine, descended from mechanics and craftsmen, but he possessed exceptional gifts for shamelessness and unscrupulous dealing.

For first of all, in his early youth, he had openly prostituted his body; later he proved sharp-witted and possessed of a strong memory, overawing his inferiors with extreme boldness and audacity, while toward his superiors he was exceedingly fawning.

At first he was driven from his native city on suspicion of trying to betray Tarentum to the Romans. He had no political power, but as an architect, and through certain repairs to the walls, he had gained control of the keys of the gate leading inland.

After fleeing to the Romans, and then again carrying on correspondence and sending messages from there to Tarentum and Hannibal, he was detected; foreseeing what would follow, he fled once more to Philip.

There he gained such credit and influence that he became one of the chief causes of the ruin of so great a kingdom.
