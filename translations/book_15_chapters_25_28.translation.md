# Book 15 Chapter 25: Ptolemy Epiphanes Succeeds To the Crown

Sosibius, the false guardian of Ptolemy, had the reputation of being a shrewd and long-enduring instrument of evil at court.

He had first arranged the murder of Lysimachus, son of Arsinoe daughter of Lysimachus and Ptolemy; next that of Magas, son of Ptolemy and Berenice daughter of Magas; third, that of Berenice, mother of Ptolemy Philopator; fourth, that of Cleomenes the Spartan; and fifth, that of Arsinoe daughter of Berenice.

Three or four days later, after building a platform in the largest colonnaded court of the palace, Agathocles and Sosibius summoned the bodyguards and household attendants, together with the officers of infantry and cavalry.

When they had assembled, Agathocles and Sosibius mounted the platform, announced the deaths of the king and queen, and proclaimed the customary mourning to the crowd.

After this they placed the diadem on the child’s head and proclaimed him king, and then read out a forged will in which the dead king had supposedly left Agathocles and Sosibius as guardians of the boy.

They urged the officers to support the child and preserve his rule. Then they brought in two silver urns, pretending that one contained the king’s bones and the other those of Arsinoe.

In reality one did contain the king’s remains, but the other was filled with spices. After these arrangements they at once proceeded with the funeral. At that moment the truth about Arsinoe became plain to everyone.

For once her death was established, people began to inquire into the manner of it. Since no other explanation was available, and the true rumor had already spread though still disputed, what had really happened now fixed itself in every mind. So a great disturbance arose among the crowd.

No one paid any attention to the king, but with regard to Arsinoe some recalled her orphaned condition, others the outrage and humiliation she had suffered throughout her life, and when to these was added the misfortune of her death, the city was filled with groaning, tears, and unceasing lamentation.

Yet to those who judged rightly, these things were less signs of affection for Arsinoe than of hatred toward Agathocles and his circle.

When Agathocles had placed the urns in the royal burial chambers and ordered mourning clothes laid aside, his first move was to issue the troops two months’ pay, convinced that the hatred of the masses could be dulled by appealing to their advantage. He next administered the customary oath taken at the proclamation of kings.

He also sent away Philammon, who had overseen the murder of Arsinoe, making him governor of the Libyan districts around Cyrene, and he placed the child in the hands of Oenanthe and Agathocleia.

After this he sent Pelops son of Pelops to Asia, to Antiochus the king, to urge him to preserve the friendship and not violate the treaty made with the child’s father; and he sent Ptolemy son of Sosibius to Philip both to settle the matter of an intermarriage alliance and to ask for help if Antiochus should attempt any more open breach of faith.

He also appointed Ptolemy son of Agesarchus as envoy to the Romans, not because he meant him to press the embassy in earnest, but so that, once he reached Greece and met friends and relatives there, he would stay away.

For his aim was to remove all prominent men from his path.

He also sent Scopas the Aetolian to Greece to recruit mercenaries, providing a large sum of gold for the advance payments.

He had two purposes in this. One was to use the men thus recruited in the war against Antiochus. The other was to send the existing mercenaries out to country forts and settlements, and by means of the newcomers refill and remake the household regiments, the palace guards, and likewise the rest of the city watch.

He thought that troops recruited and paid by himself would feel no sympathy with what had happened before, because they knew nothing of it, and that, having all their hopes both of safety and advancement resting in him, they would be ready assistants and collaborators in whatever he ordered.

All this happened before the negotiations with Philip, as I have said; but since those matters had to be taken first in the order of the narrative, it was necessary to handle these events in this way, by first describing the audiences, the diplomatic dealings, and the dispatch of the ambassadors.

Agathocles, then, once he had removed the most distinguished men and to a great extent checked the anger of the crowd by distributing pay, promptly returned to his former habits. He filled the places of his friends

by drawing from the attendants and other servants the most worthless and shameless men.

He himself spent most of day and night in drunkenness and the excesses that follow it, sparing neither mature women nor brides nor unmarried girls, and he did all this with the most offensive display.

So there was widespread and manifold resentment; yet no relief or help appeared, and instead outrage, arrogance, and carelessness were continually added to the burden.

Thus the old hatred among the people flared up again, and everyone renewed in memory the misfortunes that had already befallen the kingdom through these very people.

But since they had no respectable figure to stand in front and on whom they might lean while pressing their anger against Agathocles and Agathocleia, they stayed quiet, watching for one remaining hope in Tlepolemus and fixing their attention on him.

As long as the king lived, Tlepolemus attended to his own affairs. But when the king died, he quickly won over the troops and once more became commander in the region of Pelusium.

At first he reported what was being done in the interest of the king, believing that there would be some governing council to hold the guardianship of the child and oversight of the whole state.

But when he saw that the men fit to serve as guardians had been removed and that Agathocles was boldly seizing control of everything, he quickly changed his purpose. Fearing the danger that threatened him because of the enmity between them, he gathered troops around himself and sought money, so that he might not lie easy to the hand of any enemy.

At the same time he did not despair of gaining the guardianship of the child and the chief direction of affairs for himself, thinking that by his own judgment he was far more worthy than Agathocles in every respect, and hearing besides that both the troops under his command and those in Alexandria placed their hopes in him as the man who would put down Agathocles’ insolence.

With dispositions such as these on his side, the quarrel soon grew, since both factions helped to inflame it.

For Tlepolemus, eager to win over the generals, the taxiarchs, and the officers under them, carefully arranged drinking parties; and at these gatherings, partly from the flattery of his companions and partly from his own impulse — since he was young and the conversation was carried on over wine — he cast jibes at the family of Agathocles, at first riddling and obscure, then ambiguous, and at last open and filled with the bitterest abuse.

He mocked the writer of lampoons, the sambuca-girl, the lady’s maid, and that little page who had done and suffered everything at drinking parties when as a boy he poured wine for the king.

And because those present were always laughing and adding something to the mockery, the matter quickly reached Agathocles and his circle.

Once the hostility had become open, Agathocles at once began circulating slanders against Tlepolemus, claiming that he was alienating himself from the king and inviting Antiochus to seize control of affairs.

He found many plausible arguments for this, twisting some from actual events and inventing and dressing up others from the beginning.

He did this because he wished to provoke the crowd against Tlepolemus. But the result was the opposite. For since the people had long fixed their hopes on Tlepolemus, they were delighted to see the quarrel growing hot.

# Book 15 Chapter 26: Another Murder Committed by Agathocles

Agathocles first assembled the Macedonians, and before them he entered with the king and Agathocleia.

At first he acted the part of a man unable to say what he wished because of the flood of tears that overcame him.

But after repeatedly wiping his face with his cloak and mastering the display, he lifted up the child and said, “Take this boy, whom his father, when dying, placed in this woman’s arms” — pointing to his sister — “and entrusted, Macedonians, to your keeping.

Her goodwill contributes only a slight advantage toward his safety; but his whole situation now lies with you and in your hands.

For Tlepolemus has long been clearly seen, by those who observe matters rightly, to aim at greater things than suit his station, and now he has fixed the very day and moment on which he means to take up the diadem.

Do not trust me about this,” he said, “but men who know the truth and are now present from the facts themselves.”

With that he brought in Critolaus, who declared that he had personally seen altars being prepared and sacrificial victims made ready among the populace for the proclamation of the diadem.

But when the Macedonians heard this, far from pitying him, they simply paid no attention to what he said. Muttering and whispering among themselves, they made such a confused uproar that Agathocles himself did not even understand how the assembly came to an end.

The same thing happened in his addresses to the other groups as well.

Meanwhile many people were sailing down from the upper camps, and some urged their kinsmen, others their friends, to help in the present crisis and not stand by while they were openly outraged by men so unworthy.

What especially stirred the masses to punish those in power was the knowledge that the coming struggle lay in their own hands, because they controlled all the supplies being brought into Alexandria.

Agathocles also killed Deinon son of Deinon, and this deed, as the proverb says, was the most just of all his injustices.

For when the letters concerning the murder of Arsinoe came into his hands, he had the chance to reveal the plot and save the kingdom, but instead he cooperated with Philammon and so became responsible for all the evils that followed. Later, after the murder had been carried out, he kept recalling it, pitying the victims and regretting that he had let such a moment pass; and when this became known to Agathocles, he at once met the punishment that suited him and lost his life.

# Book 15 Chapter 27: Growing Hatred of Agathocles

Something done by Agathocles and his own circle also helped to intensify the anger both of the people and of Tlepolemus.

They seized Danae, the mother-in-law of Tlepolemus, from the temple of Demeter, dragged her unveiled through the middle of the city, and threw her into prison, wishing to make their quarrel with Tlepolemus unmistakably public.

The populace, outraged by this, no longer kept their talk private or confined to secret circles. Some by night wrote on the walls in every quarter, while others by day gathered in groups and openly expressed their hatred toward those in power.

Agathocles and his circle, seeing what was happening and holding sinister expectations about their own future, sometimes planned flight; but because nothing had been prepared for that course, thanks to their own incompetence, they abandoned the idea.

At other times they drew up lists of conspirators and partners for a daring stroke, intending at once to cut down some enemies, arrest others, and after that seize tyrannical power.

While they were considering these plans, a report reached them against a certain Moeragenes, one of the bodyguards, that he was revealing everything to Tlepolemus and cooperating with him because of his connection with Adaeus, then commander at Bubastus.

Agathocles immediately ordered Nicostratus, the secretary in charge of correspondence, to arrest Moeragenes and examine him thoroughly, putting every form of torture before him.

So Moeragenes was at once arrested by Nicostratus and taken to a secluded part of the palace, where he was first questioned directly about the charges.

When he admitted nothing, he was stripped, and some were making ready the instruments of torture while others, with whips in hand, were taking off their cloaks.

At just that moment one of the attendants ran up to Nicostratus, whispered something in his ear, and hurried away again. Nicostratus followed close behind him,

saying nothing but repeatedly slapping his thigh as he went.

# Book 15 Chapter 28: Moeragenes Escapes

What happened to Moeragenes then was astonishing and beyond belief.

The floggers were standing there all but ready to bring down their whips, while others at his feet were preparing the instruments of compulsion.

But once Nicostratus had gone away, they all stood gaping and staring at one another, constantly expecting the man to come back.

As time passed, those standing by gradually slipped away one by one, until Moeragenes was left alone. Then, passing through the palace, he unexpectedly escaped half naked into a tent of the Macedonians near the court.

Finding them by chance at breakfast and gathered together, he told them what had happened to him and the strange way he had been saved.

At first they doubted him, but when they saw him naked they were forced to believe.

Taking advantage of this reversal, Moeragenes begged the Macedonians with tears to secure not only his own safety, but also that of the king and, above all, their own.

For, he said, destruction was plain to all unless they seized the moment when the hatred of the crowd was at its height and everyone stood ready to punish Agathocles.

And that moment, he said, was now in full strength.
