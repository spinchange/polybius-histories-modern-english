# Book 5 Chapter 1: The Situation in the Summer of B. C. 218

By this time the year of Aratus the Younger’s strategia had run its course, about the season of the rising of the Pleiades; that was how the Achaeans then reckoned the year.

So he laid down office, and Eperatus took over the leadership of the Achaeans, while Dorimachus remained Strategus of the Aetolians.

At the same period, as summer was beginning, Hannibal had openly taken up war against the Romans, setting out from New Carthage and crossing the Iberus to begin his advance and march into Italy.

The Romans, for their part, sent Tiberius Sempronius to Libya with an army, and Publius Cornelius to Iberia.

Antiochus and Ptolemy, abandoning embassies and verbal argument over their claim to Coele-Syria, were now beginning actual war against one another.

Philip, meanwhile, being short of grain and money for his forces, summoned the Achaeans to a general assembly through their magistrates.

When the people had assembled in Aegium according to federal law, Philip saw that the elder and younger Aratus were unwilling to support him because of the election intrigues carried on against them by Apelles; and he saw too that Eperatus was naturally ineffective and despised by everyone.

Reflecting on what had been said before, and on the ignorance and folly of Apelles and Leontius, he decided once more to stand by Aratus.

So he persuaded the magistrates to move the assembly to Sicyon. There he took both Aratus the elder and Aratus the younger into conference, laid all the blame for what had happened on Apelles, and urged them to remain faithful to their original policy.

They readily agreed. Philip then came before the Achaean assembly, and with the help of those two men he carried through everything he had set his mind on.

The Achaeans voted to give him fifty talents at once for his first instalment, to pay his army for three months, and to add ten thousand medimni of grain. Thereafter, so long as he remained in the Peloponnese and fought alongside them, they were to pay him seventeen talents each month.

# Book 5 Chapter 2: Philip Decides to Fight at Sea

After this decree had been passed, the Achaeans dispersed to their cities.

Philip then consulted with his friends, and when his forces had gathered back from winter quarters, he decided to conduct the war by sea.

He was convinced that this would let him appear quickly and unexpectedly at any point, while the enemy would be least able to bring help to one another because they were scattered through different regions and each would fear for its own safety, since a hostile approach by sea is so sudden and swift.

For he was still at war with the Aetolians, the Spartans, and the Eleans.

Having reached this decision, he gathered the ships of the Achaeans and his own ships at Lechaeum, and kept drilling his phalangites day after day, training them to the oar as well, while the Macedonians readily obeyed his orders.

For in land battles they are among the bravest of soldiers, and when the need arises they are among the quickest to take up service at sea; moreover, they are exceptionally willing workers when it comes to digging trenches, building palisades, and all such hard labor.

They are much like the Aeacidae whom Hesiod represents as

“rejoicing in war as in a feast.”

So the king and the Macedonian army remained at Corinth, occupied with naval drill and preparations.

Apelles, however, unable either to hold Philip under his control or to bear his loss of influence, formed a plot with Leontius and Megaleas: these two were to stay with the king and deliberately hamper his service, while he himself would go off to Chalcis and see that no supplies were sent to Philip from that quarter for the furtherance of his plans.

Having made this arrangement and behaved treacherously toward the others, he went to Chalcis under various plausible pretexts to the king.

There he stayed and so faithfully kept to the terms of the plot, with everyone obeying him because of the trust he had already won, that in the end the king was driven by lack of funds to pawn some of the silver used for his own table in order to keep things going.

When the ships had been collected and the Macedonians were already trained to the oar, the king gave out grain rations and pay, put to sea, and on the second day reached Patrae with six thousand Macedonians and twelve hundred mercenaries.

# Book 5 Chapter 3: Philip In Cephallenia

About the same time, Dorimachus, the Strategus of the Aetolians, sent Agelaus and Scopas into Elis with five hundred Neo-Cretans.

The Eleans, fearing that Philip might attempt to besiege Cyllene, began gathering mercenaries, arming their own citizens, and carefully strengthening the defenses of Cyllene.

When Philip saw what they were doing, he left at Dyme a force made up of the Achaean mercenaries, some of the Cretans serving with him, some of the Gallic cavalry, and two thousand picked Achaean foot soldiers, to serve both as a reserve and as an advance guard against any danger from Elis.

He himself had earlier written to the Messenians, the Epirotes, the Acarnanians, and Scerdilaidas, asking each of them to man the ships available to them and meet him at Cephallenia. Then he sailed from Patrae at the appointed time and made for Cephallenia, landing off Pronni.

But when he saw that the little stronghold there was hard to besiege and that the ground was narrow and confined, he sailed past with his fleet and anchored before the city of the Palaeans.

Seeing that this district was full of grain and could support an army, he put his troops ashore and encamped near the city, while he brought his ships close together and enclosed them with a trench and palisade, sending the Macedonians out to forage.

He himself went around the city, inspecting where works and siege engines might be brought up to the wall, since he wanted both to receive his allies and to take the city.

He wished to secure it first because it would deprive the Aetolians of their most important support: with Cephallenian ships they made their crossings into the Peloponnese and ravaged the coasts of Epirus and Acarnania.

Second, he wanted to obtain for himself, and for his allies, a useful base of operations against the enemy’s territory.

For Cephallenia lies opposite the Corinthian Gulf, extending toward the Sicilian Sea; and it commands the northwestern parts of the Peloponnese, especially Elis, as well as the south-western coasts of Epirus, Aetolia, and Acarnania.

# Book 5 Chapter 4: Philip Besieges Palus

For that reason Philip was eager to seize the island, since it was so well placed both for gathering allies and for operating against the enemy, and equally well situated for protecting friendly territory.

He therefore pressed on, determined to bring the island under his control.

He examined the city carefully and saw that all its other sides were enclosed either by the sea or by cliffs, and that only a short stretch of level ground remained, facing Zacynthus. He decided to bring up his siege works there and conduct the whole siege from that point.

The king was occupied with these matters, and with them alone.

At about this time fifteen boats arrived from Scerdilaidas, since most of his vessels had been prevented from sailing because of the plots and disturbances among the rulers of the cities in Illyria.

The appointed allies also arrived from the Epirotes, the Acarnanians, and even the Messenians.

For once the city of Phigaleia had been taken, the Messenians were by then openly taking part in the war.

When everything needed for the siege had been prepared, Philip placed his missiles and stone-throwers in suitable positions to keep the defenders down, encouraged the Macedonians, brought the siege engines up to the walls, and then used them to begin mining.

The Macedonians worked with such zeal that in a short time the wall was left hanging for a length of two plethra.

Then the king came close to the wall and urged those inside the city to make peace with him.

When they paid no attention, he set fire to the supports and brought down in a single collapse the whole section of wall that had been propped up.

As soon as this happened, he sent in first the peltasts under Leontius, drawing them up in column and ordering them to force their way through the breach.

But Leontius, keeping faith with the arrangement he had made with Apelles, three times in succession held the young troops back after they had crossed the breach and prevented the capture of the city from being completed.

He had already corrupted the most prominent officers of the individual companies; and he himself, deliberately acting the coward and failing at every point, finally withdrew from the city after suffering many wounds, although he could easily have mastered the enemy.

When the king saw the officers behaving cowardly and many Macedonians wounded, he gave up the siege and consulted with his friends about what should be done next.
