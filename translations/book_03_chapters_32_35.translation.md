# Book 3 Chapter 32: This History is Not Difficult to Understand

We must regard those who suppose that my work is hard to acquire and hard to read through, on account of the number and bulk of its books, as simply mistaken.

For how much easier is it both to obtain and to read through forty books—woven together, as it were, on a single thread—and to follow clearly the events in Italy, Sicily, and Libya from the time of Pyrrhus down to the capture of Carthage,

and those in the rest of the world, in unbroken sequence from the flight of Cleomenes the Spartan down to the engagement between the Achaeans and the Romans at the Isthmus—how much easier than to read or to acquire the separate compositions of those who write only of particular events?

For, quite apart from the fact that such works are many times more numerous than my own treatise, it is impossible for their readers to grasp anything from them with any certainty: first, because most of these writers do not give the same account of the same events,

and next, because they leave out the events that run parallel to one another in time—events which, when set side by side and viewed and compared together, lead each to a quite different verdict than they receive under piecemeal treatment—while of the most decisive matters these writers cannot even take hold at all.

For we maintain that by far the most essential parts of history are the consequences that follow upon actions, the circumstances that accompany them, and above all the matter of their causes.

Thus we observe that the war with Antiochus took its origin from the war with Philip, the war with Philip from the war with Hannibal, and the war with Hannibal from the war over Sicily; and that the many and varied developments that lay between these wars all converged upon one and the same end.

Now all of this can be recognized and learned from those who write universal history, but it cannot be learned from those who write of the wars themselves in isolation—the Persian War, say, or the war with Philip—

unless a man, by reading the accounts of the pitched battles that those writers give, imagines that he has thereby gained a clear understanding of the whole conduct and design of the war.

But that is not so at all. Rather, by as much as understanding surpasses mere hearing, by so much, I hold, does my history surpass the compositions that treat only of particular events.

# Book 3 Chapter 33: Hannibal's Preparations

As for the Roman envoys—for it was at this point that we broke off into our digression—when they had heard out the case of the Carthaginians, they said nothing further,

except that the eldest of them, pointing to the fold of his toga before the council, declared that there in it he carried both war and peace for them, and that he would shake out and leave behind whichever of the two they should bid him.

The king of the Carthaginians told him to shake out whichever of the two seemed good to the Romans.

And when the Roman said that he would shake out war, many of those in the council cried out together that they accepted it. On these terms the envoys and the council parted.

Hannibal, meanwhile, while wintering at New Carthage, first dismissed the Iberians to their own cities, wishing them to be ready and eager for what was to come.

Secondly, he gave instructions to his brother Hasdrubal as to how he should manage the government and dominion over Iberia, and conduct the preparations against the Romans, in case he himself should be away somewhere.

Thirdly, he took thought beforehand for the security of affairs in Libya.

Reasoning with great experience and prudence, he transferred soldiers from Libya into Iberia and from Iberia into Libya, binding fast the loyalty of each group toward the other through this arrangement.

Those who crossed over into Libya were the Thersitae, the Mastiani,

and besides these the Oretes (an Iberian people) and the Olcades—altogether from these nations twelve hundred cavalry and thirteen thousand eight hundred and fifty infantry, and in addition eight hundred and seventy Balearic men,

whom they properly call slingers, and from this very function the people themselves and their island take their shared name.

Of the troops just mentioned, he stationed the greater part in Metagonia in Libya, and some in Carthage itself.

From the cities of the people called the Metagonians he sent another four thousand infantry to Carthage, who served at once as hostages and as a body of reinforcement.

In Iberia he left with his brother Hasdrubal fifty quinqueremes, two quadriremes, and five triremes—of these, thirty-two of the quinqueremes were furnished with crews, and all five of the triremes.

There were also cavalry: four hundred and fifty Libyophoenicians and Libyans, three hundred Lergetes, and eighteen hundred Numidians of the Masylii, Masaesylii, Maccoei, and Maurusii who dwell along the Ocean;

and infantry: eleven thousand eight hundred and fifty Libyans, three hundred Ligurians, five hundred Balearic Islanders, and twenty-one elephants.

No one should be surprised at the precision of this enumeration—if I have used a precision concerning Hannibal's operations in Iberia such as a man would scarcely employ even of matters he had managed himself in detail—nor should anyone condemn me beforehand for behaving like those historians who lie plausibly.

For I myself found this record set down on a bronze tablet at Lacinium, drawn up by Hannibal during the period when he was operating in the regions of Italy, and I judged it to be entirely trustworthy, at least for such matters; and therefore I chose to follow this record.

# Book 3 Chapter 34: Hannibal Contacts the Celts

Hannibal, having taken every precaution for the security of affairs both in Libya and in Iberia, now waited expectantly for the messengers being sent to him from the Celts.

For he had clearly informed himself of the excellence of the land at the foot of the Alps and around the river Padus, of the multitude of those who inhabited it, and further of the men's daring in war,

and—most important of all—of the hostility they bore toward the Romans from the earlier war, which I described in the book before this one, so that those who read might be able to follow what is now about to be told.

He therefore clung to this hope and promised everything, sending careful messages to the chieftains of the Celts, both those on the near side of the Alps and those dwelling in the Alps themselves,

since he believed that he could establish the war against the Romans within Italy only if he could pass through the difficult country that lay before him, reach the regions just mentioned, and make use of the Celts as helpers and allies for the undertaking he had in view.

When the messengers arrived and reported the willingness and expectation of the Celts, and declared that the crossing of the Alpine mountains, though very toilsome and difficult, was nevertheless not impossible, he gathered his forces from their winter quarters at the approach of spring.

Since the news from Carthage had also just reached him, his spirit was raised, and, trusting in the goodwill of his fellow citizens, he now openly summoned his forces to the war against the Romans.

He set before them how the Romans had ventured to demand his own surrender and that of all the commanders of the army, and he pointed out the excellence of the country to which they were going, and the goodwill and alliance of the Celts.

When the troops eagerly rallied to his side, he praised them, named a fixed day on which he would make the departure, and for the moment dismissed the assembly.

# Book 3 Chapter 35: Hannibal Crosses the Pyrenees

Having carried out the measures described during the winter quarters, and having provided sufficient security for affairs both in Libya and in Iberia, when the appointed day came he set out, with about ninety thousand infantry and around twelve thousand cavalry.

Crossing the river Iber, he subdued the nation of the Ilurgetes and the Bargusii, and also the Aerenosii and the Andosini, as far as the place called the Pyrenees.

When he had brought all of them under his control and taken some cities by storm—quickly and beyond expectation, but only after many great struggles and heavy loss of men—

he left Hanno as governor over all the country on the near side of the river, and as master over the Bargusii in particular; for he distrusted these people most of all, on account of their goodwill toward the Romans.

He also assigned to Hanno a portion of the forces he had: ten thousand infantry and a thousand cavalry, and he left with him the baggage of those who were setting out with himself.

He sent home an equal number to the troops just mentioned, wishing both to leave these men well disposed toward him and to hold out to the rest a hope of returning home—both to the Iberians serving in his own army and, no less, to those remaining at home—so that all might set out eagerly should there ever be need of reinforcement from them.

Taking up the rest of his army, lightly equipped—fifty thousand infantry and close to nine thousand cavalry—he led them through the mountains called the Pyrenees toward the crossing of the river called the Rhone,

having a force not so much large as serviceable and exceptionally well trained from the continuous fighting in Iberia.
