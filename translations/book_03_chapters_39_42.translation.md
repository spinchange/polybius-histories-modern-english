At this time the Carthaginians were masters of all those parts of Libya that face the inner sea, from the Altars of Philaenus, which lie along the Great Syrtis, as far as the Pillars of Heracles.

This stretch of coastline is more than sixteen thousand stades in length.

Having crossed the strait at the Pillars of Heracles, they had likewise gained control of all Iberia as far as the headland on our sea where the Pyrenees range comes to an end—the mountains that form the boundary between the Iberians and the Celts.

This place is about eight thousand stades distant from the mouth at the Pillars of Heracles.

For the distance from the Pillars to New Town happens to be three thousand stades—this was the base from which Hannibal launched his expedition into Italy (some people call New Town "New Carthage")—and from there to the river Iber it is two thousand six hundred stades;

from that river again to Emporium is one thousand six hundred, and from the city of Emporium to *** is about six hundred,

and from there on to the crossing of the Rhone is about one thousand six hundred. (These distances have now been carefully measured out and marked by the Romans in segments of eight stades.)

From the crossing of the Rhone, for those marching alongside the river itself toward its sources up to the start of the ascent into the Alps that leads into Italy, it is one thousand four hundred stades.

What remains are the passes over the Alps themselves, about one thousand two hundred stades; once he had crossed these, he would arrive in the plains around the Padus in Italy.

So that the whole distance he had to traverse from New Town was about nine thousand stades.

Of this territory he had by now covered nearly half in terms of sheer distance, but in terms of difficulty the greater part of the march still lay ahead of him.

# Book 3 Chapter 40: Gauls Attack the Military Colonies

Hannibal, then, set about forcing the passes over the Pyrenees, full of fear of the Celts because of the strength of the terrain.

The Romans, meanwhile, at this same period had heard from the envoys they had sent to Carthage both the decisions reached and the words that had been spoken; and when news fell upon them, sooner than they expected, that Hannibal had crossed the river Iber with his army, they resolved to send Publius Cornelius with legions into Iberia, and Tiberius Sempronius into Libya.

While these commanders were busy with the enrolment of their legions and the rest of their preparations, the people pressed on to bring to completion the work of the colonies which they had earlier resolved to send out into Gaul.

They fortified the towns vigorously, and ordered the settlers to be on the spot within thirty days, their number amounting to six thousand for each of the two cities;

one of these they founded on the near side of the river Padus, naming it Placentia, and the other on the far side, calling it Cremona.

These colonies had just been settled when the Gauls called the Boii—who had long been lying in wait, as it were, to throw off their friendship with the Romans, but had until then lacked an opportunity—

now grew excited and, trusting in the presence of the Carthaginians on the strength of the reports passing among them, revolted from the Romans, abandoning the hostages they had given when they came out of the earlier war, which I described in the previous book.

They appealed to the Insubres and, joining forces in keeping with their long-standing anger, overran the land that had been parcelled out by the Romans; pursuing the fleeing colonists, they shut them up in Mutina, a Roman colony, and laid siege to it.

Among those they trapped inside were three distinguished men who had been sent out to divide up the land: one of these was Gaius Lutatius, who had held the consulship, and the other two had held the praetorship (the office of the six axes).

When these men thought it necessary to come to a parley with them, the Boii agreed. But when the men came out, the Boii violated the truce and seized them, hoping by their means to recover their own hostages.

Lucius Manlius, who was a praetor stationed in the area with an army, heard what had happened and hurried to the rescue.

The Boii, learning of his approach, prepared ambushes in some woods, and as soon as he had entered the wooded ground they fell upon him from all sides at once and killed many of the Romans.

The survivors at first rushed to flee, but once they reached open ground they pulled themselves together to such a degree that they managed, with difficulty, to make an orderly withdrawal. The Boii, however, followed close behind and shut these men too up in the village called Tannes.

When news reached Rome that the fourth legion was surrounded by the Boii and being besieged with full force, they hastily dispatched the legions that had been assigned to Publius to the relief of these men, putting a praetor in command of them, and they instructed Publius to gather and enrol other legions from among the allies.

# Book 3 Chapter 41: The Consuls Set Out to Iberia and Libya

Such, then, was the state of Celtic affairs from the beginning down to the arrival of Hannibal, and they had reached the outcome that I have described both earlier and now.

The Roman commanders, having made ready everything for their own undertakings, set sail at the start of the season for the operations before them—Publius to Iberia with sixty ships, and Tiberius Sempronius to Libya with a hundred and sixty quinqueremes.

With these he set out to wage war so formidably and made such preparations at Lilybaeum—mustering everyone from every quarter—as though he meant to lay siege to Carthage itself the moment he landed.

Publius, sailing along the Ligurian coast, came on the fifth day from Pisae to the region around Massalia,

and, anchoring off the first mouth of the Rhone, the one called the Massaliote mouth,

he began disembarking his forces. He had heard that Hannibal was already crossing the Pyrenees, but he was convinced that he was still far off, both because of the difficulty of the terrain and because of the multitude of Celts lying between them.

Hannibal, however, contrary to expectation, partly winning over the Celts with money and partly forcing his way through them, arrived with his forces at the crossing of the Rhone, keeping the Sardinian Sea on his right.

When it was made clear to Publius that the enemy were at hand, he partly disbelieved it because of the speed of their arrival and partly wished to learn the precise truth; so while he himself let his troops recover from the voyage and deliberated with the tribunes about which ground to use and how to engage the enemy,

he sent out three hundred of his most courageous cavalry, attaching to them as both guides and supporting fighters some Celts who happened to be serving as mercenaries with the Massaliotes.

# Book 3 Chapter 42: The Passage of the Rhone

Hannibal, on reaching the region of the river, at once set about making the crossing where the stream runs in a single channel, his camp being about four days' march from the sea.

Making friends in every way with the people living along the river, he bought up from them all their dugout boats and small craft, which were plentiful in number because many of those dwelling beside the Rhone engage in seaborne trade.

He also obtained from them suitable timber for the construction of dugouts, so that within two days a countless number of transport boats was produced, each man eager not to depend on his neighbour but to hold in his own hands his hopes of getting across.

At this time a host of barbarians had gathered on the far bank in order to prevent the Carthaginians from crossing.

Looking at them, Hannibal reasoned from the present circumstances that it was impossible either to force a crossing with so many enemies posted opposite, or to wait where he was, for fear of being attacked by the enemy from every side;

so when the third night came on he sent off a part of his force, providing them with local guides and placing over them all Hanno, the son of Bomilcar the king.

These men marched up against the current along the river for two hundred stades, and when they reached a certain place where the river happened to divide around an island-like piece of ground, they halted there.

From the woodland close at hand, by fastening some pieces of timber together and lashing others, they constructed in a short time many rafts, sufficient for their immediate need; on these they crossed over safely, with no one to stop them.

Seizing a strong position, they stayed there that day to rest from their recent hardships, while at the same time making preparations for the task to come, according to their orders.

Hannibal, for his part, was doing much the same with the forces left behind with him.

The greatest difficulty he faced was getting the elephants across; these numbered thirty-seven.
