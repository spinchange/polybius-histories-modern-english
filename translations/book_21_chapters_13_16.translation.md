# Book 21 Chapter 13: Antiochus Sends an Envoy To Discuss Peace

After his defeat in the sea battle Antiochus stayed at Sardis, wasting time and delaying in the larger conduct of the war.

As soon as he heard that the enemy had crossed over, his spirit was crushed and he lost hope; so he decided to send to Lucius and Publius to negotiate a settlement.

He chose Heracleides of Byzantium and sent him out with instructions to cede the cities of Lampsacus and Smyrna, and also Alexandria, from which the war had originally taken its beginning.

Likewise, if the Romans wished to take away any other cities in Aeolis and Ionia that had chosen their side in the present war, he was prepared for that too.

In addition, he would pay half the expense they had incurred in the war against him.

These were the instructions Heracleides had for the public audience; but for Publius in private he had other proposals, which I shall explain in due course.

When this envoy reached the Hellespont and found the Romans still waiting in the camp where they had first encamped after crossing,

he was at first delighted, thinking that their remaining where they were and not yet moving on would help his negotiations.

But when he learned that Publius was still on the other side, he was troubled, because the greatest weight in the matter rested on Publius's judgment.

The reason why the army remained in its first camp and why Publius was apart from the forces was that he was one of the Salii.

As I said in my work on the constitution, this is one of the three priestly bodies by which Rome performs its most notable sacrifices to the gods.

During the season of that sacrifice, the law requires that these Salii not change their location for thirty days, wherever they may happen to be caught.

This happened to Publius then. Just as the army was about to cross over, that sacred period overtook him, so that he could not change place.

As a result, Scipio was separated from the army and remained in Europe, while the forces that had crossed stayed where they were and could do nothing further, awaiting the arrival of the man just mentioned.

# Book 21 Chapter 14: Antiochus Tries To Negotiate

After some days, when Publius had arrived, Heracleides was summoned before the council and spoke on the instructions he carried.

He said that Antiochus would withdraw from Lampsacus and Smyrna and also from the city of Alexandria, as well as from any cities in Aeolis and Ionia that had chosen the Roman side.

In addition, he would undertake to pay half the expense they had incurred in the present war.

He also said much else on the same subject, urging the Romans not to test fortune too far, since they were men, and not to make the scope of their power boundless, but to set limits to it—if possible, the limits of Europe.

Even that, he said, would be a great and astonishing dominion, since none of their predecessors had attained it.

But if they were determined to lay hold of some part of Asia as well, let them define what parts those were, and the king would go to the utmost of his power to satisfy them.

After this had been said, the council decided that the general should reply that Antiochus ought to pay not half the cost of the war but all of it, since the war had arisen originally not because of Rome but because of him.

As for the cities, he must not free only those in Aeolis and Ionia, but give up all his dominion on this side of Taurus.

When the envoy heard this answer from the council, and saw that the demands went far beyond his instructions, he made no further attempt in the public meeting but instead devoted himself to courting Publius privately.

# Book 21 Chapter 15: Scipio Scorns Antiochus's Secret Proposal

When he found a suitable opportunity, he spoke of the proposals entrusted to him.

First, the king would restore Publius's son without ransom.

For at the beginning of the war Scipio's son had fallen into the hands of Antiochus's people.

Second, the king was ready even now to give whatever sum of money Publius might name, and after that to make the resources of his kingdom common with him, if only he would support the peace terms proposed by the king.

Publius said that he accepted the offer concerning his son and would be deeply grateful if Antiochus fulfilled that promise.

But as for the rest, he said the king entirely failed to understand his own interest, not only in this private approach to him, but in the public negotiations as well.

If Antiochus had made these offers while still master of Lysimacheia and of the entrance into the Chersonese, he would quickly have obtained his aim.

Likewise, even after giving up those places, if he had come to the Hellespont with his army, clearly prepared to prevent our crossing, and had then negotiated on these same points, he might still have achieved what he wanted.

But now, after allowing our forces to set foot in Asia and accepting not only the bridle but even the rider, he comes to propose equal terms of peace. Naturally he must fail and be disappointed in his hopes.

Therefore Publius advised him to take better counsel about the present and to look truly at the situation.

In return for the king's promise about his son, he said he would give advice worth the favor offered: the king should yield on every point, but under no circumstances fight the Romans.

After hearing this, Heracleides returned and reported the details to the king.

Antiochus, believing that no harsher terms could be laid on him if he were defeated in battle than those already being demanded, gave up the business of negotiation and made every kind of preparation for a decisive struggle.

# Book 21 Chapter 16: Zeuxis and Antipater Sent to Negotiate Peace

After the victory the Romans took possession of Sardis and its citadel, and then Musaeus came from Antiochus under flag of truce.

Publius and his associates received him graciously, and he said that Antiochus wished to send envoys to discuss the whole settlement.

Accordingly he asked that safe conduct be given to those who came. When this was granted, he returned.

After some days envoys came from King Antiochus: Zeuxis, formerly satrap of Lydia, and Antipater his nephew.

Their first concern was to meet King Eumenes, because they feared that, owing to the earlier friction, he might be especially eager to harm them.

But contrary to expectation they found him moderate and mild, and so at once moved on to the general audience.

Summoned into the council, they said much else besides, urging the Romans to use their success with gentleness and magnanimity.

They declared that this was not so much to Antiochus's advantage as to that of the Romans themselves, since fortune had now handed them mastery and dominion over the inhabited world.

In the end they simply asked what Antiochus must do to obtain peace and friendship with Rome.

The men in the council had already sat together and deliberated about these matters, and now instructed Publius to set out the decision.
