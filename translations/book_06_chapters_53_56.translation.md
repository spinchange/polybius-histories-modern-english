# Book 6 Chapter 53: Roman Funeral Rites

Whenever one of their distinguished men dies, during the funeral procession the body, together with all its honors, is carried into the forum to the place called the Rostra, sometimes set upright in full view, though more rarely laid out reclining.

With the whole people standing around, his son—if he has left one of full age and he happens to be present—or otherwise some other relative, mounts the Rostra and speaks about the dead man's virtues and the achievements he accomplished in life.

By this means the deeds are brought back before the eyes of the crowd, and not only those who shared in them but even those who had no part in them are moved to sympathy, so that the loss seems not a private sorrow for the mourners but a public one for the whole people.

After the burial and the customary rites, they place the likeness of the dead man in the most conspicuous part of the house, enclosed in a small wooden shrine.

This likeness is a mask made in an extraordinarily faithful resemblance, both in features and in coloring.

They display these images at public sacrifices with great care, and whenever another eminent member of the family dies, they carry them in the funeral procession, placing them on men who seem to match the originals as closely as possible in height and general build.

These men put on the garments appropriate to the offices the dead once held: if the man was a consul or praetor, garments with purple border; if a censor, purple robes; if he had celebrated a triumph or done some equivalent deed, robes embroidered with gold.

They ride in chariots, and before them are carried the rods, axes, and all the other insignia proper to the magistracies, in proportion to the rank each had reached in the service of the state during his lifetime.

When they reach the Rostra, they all sit in order on ivory chairs. A young man who loves glory and noble deeds could hardly behold a finer sight.

For who would not be stirred by seeing all at once the images of men famous for their virtue, as if they were living and breathing? What spectacle could be more splendid?

# Book 6 Chapter 54: The Glorious Memory of Brave Men Kept Alive

And the speaker, after finishing his account of the man who is about to be buried, goes on to the others whose representatives are present, beginning with the most ancient and recounting the successes and deeds of each.

Thus the renown of good men is continually made new, the glory of those who achieved some noble deed is made immortal, and the fame of those who benefited their country becomes known to the multitude and handed down to later generations.

Most of all, the young are inspired to endure every hardship for the common good in hopes of winning the glory that attends brave men.

And what I say is confirmed by the facts. Many Romans have voluntarily fought single combats on which the issue of the whole battle depended, and not a few have chosen certain death, some in war for the safety of others, some in peace for the security of the commonwealth.

There have even been magistrates who put their own sons to death, against every custom and every law, valuing the interest of their country above the natural bond with their nearest kin.

Many such stories are told of many Roman men, but one example named specifically will be enough for the present, both as proof and as illustration.

# Book 6 Chapter 55: Patriotism and Purity At Rome

They tell the story of Horatius, surnamed Cocles. While he was fighting against two of the enemy at the far end of the bridge over the Tiber, the bridge that stands before the city, he saw a large body coming up to support them and feared that they would force their way across into the city.

So he turned and shouted to the men behind him to retreat as quickly as possible and break down the bridge. They obeyed, and while they were cutting it apart he held his ground, receiving many wounds and checking the enemy's advance—not so much because they were daunted by his strength as because they were astonished by his steadfastness and daring.

Once the bridge had been broken, the enemy's attack was stopped; and Cocles, throwing himself into the river in full armor, deliberately gave up his life, valuing the safety of his country and the glory he would win thereafter more than his present life and all the years still left to him.

Such, it seems, is the zeal and ambition for noble deeds that Roman customs implant in the young.

# Book 6 Chapter 56: Religion Keeps the Roman Commonwealth Together

In matters of money as well, Roman customs and principles are better than those of the Carthaginians.

Among the Carthaginians nothing connected with profit is considered shameful; among the Romans nothing is more disgraceful than taking bribes and making gain from what one ought not.

For the more one kind of people honors profit from the most successful source, the more the other treats unlawful gain as a reproach.

The proof is this: among the Carthaginians men openly give gifts to obtain office, whereas among the Romans the penalty for such conduct is death.

Since the prizes offered for virtue are thus opposite among the two peoples, it is natural that their ways of pursuing them should also be different.

But the greatest superiority of the Roman constitution, in my opinion, lies in their attitude toward the gods.

Indeed, what among other peoples is criticized as superstition is, I think, the very thing that holds the Roman state together.

This element has been carried so far among them and introduced so thoroughly into both private life and public business that nothing could surpass it, and many might find that astonishing.

For my part, I think they have done this for the sake of the multitude.

If it were possible to found a state composed of wise men, perhaps such a device would not be necessary.

But since every crowd is fickle, full of lawless desires, irrational anger, and violent passion, the only thing left is to keep the masses in check by unseen fears and such theatrical impressions.

So it seems to me that the ancients were not introducing notions about the gods and beliefs about punishments in the underworld among the people at random or without purpose; rather, those of our own day act rashly and senselessly in trying to cast them out.

This is why, among other things, Greek officials entrusted with only a single talent, though they have ten clerks, as many seals, and twice as many witnesses, still cannot be trusted to keep faith.

At Rome, by contrast, men serving in offices or embassies handle very large sums of money, yet through the force of their oath alone they remain faithful to their duty.

And whereas among other peoples it is rare to find a man who keeps his hands off public money and stays clean in such matters, among the Romans it is rare to catch anyone in the act of such wrongdoing.
