# Book 4 Chapter 37: Universal War

At this time it happened that Aratus's term of office was already coming to an end, and that his son, Aratus, who had been elected by the Achaeans, was taking over the generalship.

Scopas was general of the Aetolians, and just then his term of office stood at about its midpoint. For the Aetolians held their elections immediately after the autumn equinox, whereas the Achaeans held theirs about the time of the rising of the Pleiades.

Now, as summer was already setting in and the younger Aratus had taken over the generalship, all these affairs at once took up their undertakings and their beginnings.

For at this same period Hannibal set about besieging Saguntum, while the Romans were dispatching Lucius Aemilius with a force into Illyria against Demetrius of Pharos—matters of which we have given an account in the previous book.

Antiochus, after Theodotus had handed over Ptolemais and Tyre to him, was setting out to make an attempt on the regions of Coele-Syria, while Ptolemy was occupied with preparations for the war against Antiochus.

Lycurgus, wishing to begin in the same manner as Cleomenes, had encamped before the Athenaeum of the Megalopolitans and was besieging it. The Achaeans were gathering mercenary cavalry and infantry for the war that surrounded them.

Philip, meanwhile, set out from Macedonia with his army, having ten thousand Macedonian phalangites, five thousand peltasts, and along with these eight hundred cavalry.

For toward the necessary needs of life, the regions of the Pontus furnish us with livestock and with the great number of persons brought into slavery, supplying them, by common admission, in the most abundant and most serviceable quantity; while toward our comforts they provide us unstintingly with honey, wax, and salt-fish.

They also receive what is surplus in our own regions—olive oil and every kind of wine—and they exchange these for grain, sometimes giving it when it is opportune, sometimes taking it.

From all these things the Greeks would necessarily have been either wholly cut off, or else have found the exchange of them entirely unprofitable, had the Byzantines chosen to be obstructive and to make common cause now with the Gauls, now still more with the Thracians—or had they not inhabited the place at all.

Now perhaps the greatest advantages for their own livelihood accrue to the Byzantines themselves, because of the special features of their location.

# Book 4 Chapter 39: The Black Sea

The sea called the Pontus has a circumference of close to twenty-two thousand stades, and two mouths lying diametrically opposite to each other, the one opening from the Propontis, the other from the Maeotic Lake, which itself by itself has a circumference of eight thousand stades.

Into the aforementioned basins many great rivers discharge from Asia, and still greater and more numerous ones from Europe; and so it comes about that the Maeotis, being filled up by these, flows into the Pontus through its mouth, and the Pontus into the Propontis.

The mouth of the Maeotis is called the Cimmerian Bosporus, which has a breadth of about thirty stades and a length of sixty, and is shallow throughout.

The mouth of the Pontus is likewise named the Thracian Bosporus, and its length is one hundred and twenty stades, while its breadth is not everywhere the same.

The mouth begins, on the Propontis side, at the channel between Calchedon and Byzantium,

which is fourteen stades across; and on the Pontus side, at the place called Hieron, where they say that Jason, on his return voyage from Colchis, first sacrificed to the twelve gods. This place lies on the Asiatic side, and is twelve stades distant from Europe, measured to the Sarapieum of Thrace, which lies directly opposite.

There are two causes of the continual outflow of both the Maeotis and the Pontus. The first is immediate and plain to everyone: as many streams fall into vessels of fixed circumference, the liquid keeps growing greater and greater;

and if there were no outlet, it would necessarily keep rising and take up an ever greater and larger area of the basin, whereas, since outlets do exist, the water that is added and grows excessive must overflow and run off and be carried away continuously through the existing mouths.

The second cause is this: since the rivers carry much sediment of every kind into the aforementioned basins during heavy rains, the water, pressed out by the accumulating silt deposits, keeps rising and is carried away in the same manner through the existing outlets.

And since the silting and the inflow from the rivers are unceasing and continuous, the discharge through the mouths must necessarily be unceasing and continuous too.

These, then, are the true causes of the outflow of the Pontus, deriving their credibility not from merchants' tales, but from observation according to nature—

And since we have settled on this topic, we must leave nothing idle or resting on mere assertion—as most writers are accustomed to do—but must employ a more demonstrative manner of exposition, so that we leave none of the points under inquiry unresolved for our attentive readers.

For this is the peculiar mark of the present age, in which, all seas and lands having become navigable and passable, it would no longer be fitting to use poets and myth-writers as witnesses concerning things unknown—

which is what our predecessors did about most matters, offering, as Heraclitus says, untrustworthy guarantors for disputed points. We must rather try, through history itself, to present sufficient credibility to our hearers.

We assert, then, that the Pontus has been silting up both in the past and now, and that in time both the Maeotis and the Pontus will be entirely filled up—provided the same arrangement of the regions remains, and the causes of the silting continue to operate without interruption.

For whenever time is infinite, and the basins are in every way wholly finite, it is plain that, even if only a trifling amount is brought in, they will be filled in time.

For by nature, a finite thing that is continuously being formed or destroyed in infinite time—even if it happens by the smallest degree (for let this now be supposed)—must inevitably reach completion according to the proposition.

And whenever it is not a trifling amount, but actually a very great deal of silt that is brought in, it is clear that what we are now describing will come to pass not at some distant time, but quickly.

And this is indeed seen to be happening. The Maeotis, then, has already become silted up: for the greater part of it lies at a depth of seven or five fathoms, and on this account it can no longer be sailed by large ships without a pilot.

And although from the beginning it was a sea flowing together with the Pontus, as the ancients agree, it is now a freshwater lake, the sea-water having been pressed out by the silt deposits, and the inflow of the rivers having prevailed.
