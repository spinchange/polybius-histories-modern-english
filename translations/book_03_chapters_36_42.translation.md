# Book 3 Chapter 36: Geography of Hannibal's March

So that my account does not become entirely unclear through ignorance of the places involved, I ought to state from where Hannibal set out, through which and how many regions he passed, and into what part of Italy he descended.

But I should not merely give the bare names of the places, rivers, and cities, as some historians do, who assume that in every case this by itself is enough for knowledge and clarity.

In my view, where the places are already familiar, the listing of names contributes something—indeed something considerable—toward jogging the memory. But where they are unknown, the recitation of names has exactly the same effect as words that are meaningless and merely so much noise.

For when the mind has nothing to fix on and is unable to attach what is said to anything it already knows, the narrative remains ungraspable and falls on deaf ears.

We must therefore set out a method by which, when speaking of unknown places, it will be possible to bring the listeners, to some degree, to true and recognizable conceptions.

The first and most important kind of knowledge, then,

and moreover the one common to all human beings, is the division and arrangement of the heaven that surrounds us—the scheme by which all of us, anyone with even a little sense, recognize east, west, south, and north.

The second is the practice by which we assign the places on the earth to each of these directions and, always carrying in our mind what is being described and referring it to one of the points just mentioned, arrive at familiar and habitual notions about regions that are unknown and

# Book 3 Chapter 37: Three Geographic Divisions of the World

unseen. Now that these points have been laid down concerning the earth as a whole, the next step would be, dividing up our own inhabited world on the same principle, to bring it within our listeners' grasp.

This world is divided into three parts, with three names: one part of it they call Asia, another Libya, and the third Europe.

The boundaries marking off these divisions are the river Don, the Nile, and the strait at the Pillars of Heracles.

Asia, then, lies between the Nile and the Don, and falls within that span of the surrounding heaven that stretches between the summer sunrise (the northeast) and the south.

Libya lies between the Nile and the Pillars of Heracles, and falls beneath the southern portion of the heaven, extending continuously toward the winter sunsets (the southwest) as far as the line of the equinoctial sunset, which falls at the Pillars of Heracles.

These two regions, then, viewed in broad terms, occupy the territory to the south of our sea, running from east to west.

Europe lies opposite both of them, toward the north, facing them, and extends continuously from east to west.

Its largest and deepest part lies directly beneath the north, between the river Don and the Narbo—a river not far to the west of Marseilles and of the mouths through which the Rhone discharges into the Sardinian Sea.

From the Narbo and the country around it, the Celts occupy the land as far as the mountains called the Pyrenees, which run without a break from our sea to the outer sea.

The remaining part of Europe, from the aforesaid mountains and reaching toward the west and the Pillars of Heracles, is bounded by both our sea and the outer sea. The portion stretching along our sea as far as the Pillars of Heracles is called Iberia,

while the portion lying along the outer sea—the one called the Great Sea—has no common name, because it has only recently been explored. It is inhabited entirely by barbarian peoples, very numerous, about whom I will give

a detailed account later. Just as no one, down to our own day, can say for certain about Asia and Libya, where they approach each other in the region of Ethiopia, whether the land to the south is continuous mainland or is surrounded by sea,

in the same way the region between the Don and the Narbo, stretching up toward the north, remains unknown to us up to now—unless at some later point, by diligent inquiry, we should investigate it.

As for those who say or write anything about these regions beyond this, we must regard them as ignorant and as spinning fables.

Let this much, then, be said by me, so that my narrative may not be completely impossible to follow for those unfamiliar with the places, but so that, at least in terms of the broad distinctions, they may be able to fit and refer what is said to something in their minds, judging by reference to the points of the heaven.

For just as in seeing we are accustomed always to turn our faces toward whatever is being pointed out to us, so too in thought we must direct and incline ourselves toward the places that

# Book 3 Chapter 39: The Length of Hannibal's March

are being indicated through the account. But setting these matters aside, let us turn to the continuation of the narrative before us.

At this time the Carthaginians controlled all those parts of Libya that face the inner sea, from the Altars of Philaenus, which lie on the Great Syrtis, as far as the Pillars of Heracles.

The length of this coastline is more than sixteen thousand stades.

Having crossed the strait at the Pillars of Heracles, they had likewise gained control of the whole of Iberia as far as the headland that forms the limit, at our sea, of the Pyrenees mountains, which separate the Iberians and the Celts.

This point is about eight thousand stades from the strait at the Pillars of Heracles.

The breakdown is as follows: from the Pillars to New Town it is three thousand stades—the place from which Hannibal launched his march into Italy. (Some call New Town New Carthage.) From there to the river Ebro is two thousand six hundred stades;

from that river again to Emporium, one thousand six hundred; and from the city of Emporium to *** about six hundred;

and further, from there to the crossing of the Rhone about one thousand six hundred. (These distances have now been carefully paced out and marked off in eight-stade intervals by the Romans.)

From the crossing of the Rhone, marching alongside the river itself toward its sources, as far as the ascent into the Alps that leads to Italy, is one thousand four hundred stades.

What remains is the passes over the Alps, about one thousand two hundred stades; having crossed these, he was to arrive at the plains around the Po in Italy.

So that the total distance from New Town that he had to traverse was about nine thousand stades.

Of this terrain he had by now covered nearly half in terms of distance, but in terms of difficulty the greater part of his journey still lay ahead of him.

# Book 3 Chapter 40: Gauls Attack the Military Colonies

So Hannibal was setting about forcing the passes through the Pyrenees, in great fear of the Celts because of the strength of the positions.

The Romans, meanwhile, at about this same time, had heard from the envoys they had sent to Carthage both the decisions taken and the speeches that had been made; and when news reached them—sooner than they expected—that Hannibal had crossed the river Ebro with his army, they resolved to send out, with their legions, Publius Cornelius to Iberia and Tiberius Sempronius to Libya.

While these men were occupied with the enrollment of their legions and the rest of their preparations, the Romans pressed to bring to completion the matter of the colonies that they had earlier decided to send out into Gaul.

They energetically fortified the towns and ordered the settlers—about six thousand for each town—to take up residence on the spot within thirty days.

One of these they founded on the near side of the river Po, naming it Placentia; the other on the far side, calling it Cremona.

No sooner had these colonies been settled than the Gauls called the Boii—who had long been, as it were, lying in wait to break their friendship with the Romans but had until then lacked an opportunity—

now grew excited and, trusting in the arrival of the Carthaginians on the strength of the messages passing back and forth, revolted from the Romans, abandoning the hostages they had given on coming out of the earlier war, which I described in my previous book.

After calling on the Insubres and uniting with them in their long-standing anger, they overran the land that had been allotted by the Romans, and pursuing the fleeing colonists to Mutina—a Roman colony—they laid siege to it.

Among those they trapped inside were three distinguished men who had been sent out to apportion the land: one of them was Gaius Lutatius, a former consul, the other two former praetors.

When these men thought it necessary to come to a parley with them, the Boii agreed. But when the men came out, the Boii broke faith and seized them, hoping by this means to recover their own hostages.

Lucius Manlius, a praetor stationed in the district with a force, heard what had happened and hurried to the rescue.

The Boii, learning of his approach, set ambushes in some thickets; and as soon as he entered the wooded ground, they fell on him from every side at once and killed many of the Romans.

The survivors at first rushed into flight; but once they reached open ground, they held together to some extent, so that they barely managed to make an orderly withdrawal. The Boii, however, kept up the pursuit and shut these men too inside the village called Tannes.

When the news reached Rome that the fourth legion was surrounded by the Boii and being besieged with full force, they hurriedly dispatched the legions that had been assigned to Publius to their relief, putting a praetor in command, and ordered Publius to gather and enroll other troops for himself from the allies.

# Book 3 Chapter 41: The Consuls Set Out to Iberia and Libya

The affairs of the Celts, then, from the start down to the arrival of Hannibal, stood thus and had taken the course I have described both earlier and just now.

The Roman commanders, having made their preparations for their respective undertakings, set sail at the start of the campaigning season for the operations before them—Publius to Iberia with sixty ships, and Tiberius Sempronius to Libya with a hundred and sixty quinqueremes.

With these Tiberius set out to wage war so menacingly, and made such preparations at Lilybaeum, gathering everyone from everywhere, that he meant to besiege Carthage itself immediately upon landing.

Publius, meanwhile, coasting along Liguria, came on the fifth day from Pisa to the region of Marseilles,

and after anchoring at the first mouth of the Rhone, the one called the Mouth of Marseilles,

he disembarked his forces. He had heard that Hannibal was already crossing the Pyrenees mountains, but was convinced that he was still far off, both because of the difficult terrain and because of the great number of Celtic peoples lying in between.

Hannibal, however, unexpectedly—having won over some of the Celts with money and overpowered others—arrived with his forces at the crossing of the Rhone, keeping the Sardinian Sea on his right.

When Publius was informed that the enemy were at hand, partly disbelieving it because of the speed of their arrival, and partly wishing to learn the exact truth, he himself rested his forces after the voyage and deliberated with his tribunes about which positions to use and how to engage the enemy;

and he sent out three hundred of his most courageous cavalry, attaching to them as guides and fellow combatants some Celts who happened to be serving as mercenaries among the Massaliots.

# Book 3 Chapter 42: The Passage of the Rhone

Hannibal, on reaching the region of the river, immediately set about making the crossing where the stream runs in a single channel, his camp being about four days' march from the sea.

Making the people living along the river friendly to him by every means, he bought up from them all their dugout canoes and small boats, which were numerous enough, since many of those living along the Rhone engage in seaborne trade.

He also obtained from them the timber suitable for building dugouts; and from this, in two days, an innumerable number of ferry-craft was produced, since every man was eager not to depend on his neighbor but to have the means of crossing in his own hands.

At this time a multitude of barbarians had gathered on the far bank in order to prevent the Carthaginians from crossing.

Looking at them and reasoning from the present circumstances that it was impossible either to force a crossing with so many enemies posted opposite, or to stay where he was without being attacked on all sides,

Hannibal, when the third night came, sent off a part of his force, providing them with local guides and putting Hanno, son of King Bomilcar, in overall command.

These men marched up along the river against the current for two hundred stades, and arriving at a spot where the river happened to divide around an island-like piece of ground, there they halted.

From the woods close at hand, partly nailing the timber together and partly lashing it, they put together in a short time a large number of rafts, sufficient for the immediate need; on these they crossed over safely, with no one to stop them.

Having seized a strong position, they stayed there that day, resting themselves after their recent hardship while also preparing, according to the plan, for the task to come.

Hannibal, for his part, did much the same with the forces he had kept with himself.

His greatest difficulty was getting the elephants across; these numbered thirty-seven.
