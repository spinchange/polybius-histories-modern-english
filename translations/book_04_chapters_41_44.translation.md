# Book 4 Chapter 41: Flow of the Danube into the Black Sea

What is happening here is plain. Since the Danube flows out of Europe into the Pontus through several mouths, the result is that, opposite it, a bank has built up for nearly a thousand stades, lying about a day's sail out from the present coastline and formed from the silt carried down through those mouths.

Onto this bank sailors crossing the Pontus, while still running through what they take to be open water, run aground at night before they realize it. Seamen call these shoals "the Breasts."

As for why the deposit does not accumulate right against the shore but is instead driven far out, this is the cause we must reckon on.

So far as the rivers' currents, by the sheer force of their flow, overpower the sea and thrust it back, just so far must the silt—and everything else carried along by the streams—be pushed forward, finding no rest or settling place at all.

But once the currents are spent by the depth and mass of the sea, then it is only natural that the silt, sinking of its own nature, should at last come to rest and settle.

This is why, with violent and large rivers, the banks form far out while the water close inshore is deep, whereas with smaller, gently flowing rivers the sandbars build up right at their mouths.

The point becomes most obvious during heavy rains: for then even insignificant streams, once they get the better of the waves at their mouths, push the silt out to sea so far that the distance, in each case, is proportional to the force of the streams pouring in.

As for the size of the bank just described, and in general the mass of stones, timber, and earth swept down by the rivers, there is no reason whatever to be skeptical—

indeed it would be foolish, when we see with our own eyes how an ordinary mountain torrent will often, in a short time, gouge out and cut through sheer heights, carry off timber, soil, and stone of every kind, and pile up such enormous mounds of debris that at times it transforms a place so utterly that within a short span it is no longer even recognizable.

And here is an indication of what is to come: by just as much as the Maeotis is now fresher than the Pontic sea, by so much is the Pontic sea plainly observed to differ from our own.

From this it is clear that, once the time it has taken for the Maeotis to fill in stands to a further span of time in the same ratio as the size of the Pontic basin stands to the Maeotic, then the Pontus too will become shallow, fresh, and marshy, much like the Maeotic lake.

In fact we should expect this to happen even sooner, inasmuch as the rivers emptying into it are larger and more numerous.

Let this much, then, be my reply to those disposed to disbelieve that the Pontus is even now being silted up, that it will one day be filled in, and that so vast a sea will turn into a lake and a shoal.

And let it be said still more for the sake of guarding against the falsehoods and marvels of seafarers, so that our inexperience may not force us to gape childishly at everything we are told, but that, possessing some traces of the truth, we may to some extent judge from them whether what this or that person reports is true or the reverse.

# Book 4 Chapter 43: The Site of Byzantium

The strait that joins the Pontus and the Propontis being a hundred and twenty stades in length, as I said just now—with the Hieron marking its limit toward the Pontus and the narrows at Byzantium marking its limit toward the Propontis—

midway between these stands the Hermaeum, on the European side, set on a headland that juts out into the strait. It lies about five stades from Asia and stands at the very narrowest point of the whole strait; it was here, they say, that Darius bridged the channel at the time he made his crossing against the Scythians.

Along the rest of its course from the Pontus the flow of the current is much the same, owing to the similarity of the land bordering the strait on either side;

but when the current, driving down from the Pontus and squeezed together, strikes with force against the European Hermaeum, where we said the strait is narrowest, it is then deflected as though by a blow and crashes against the shores of Asia opposite.

From there, as if rebounding, it makes its return toward the European headlands called the Hearths.

Setting off again from there, it strikes the place called the Cow, a spot on the Asian side where, the myths tell, Io first set foot after crossing over.

Finally the current, setting off from the Cow, runs straight to Byzantium itself; splitting around the city, a small part of it is channeled off into the gulf called the Horn, while the greater part swerves away once more.

It no longer has the force, however, to drive across to the land opposite, where Chalcedon lies;

for, having rebounded back and forth several times, and the channel now being broad, the current at this point loses its momentum and no longer makes short reflections at a sharp angle toward the far shore, but rather at an obtuse one;

and so, leaving the city of the Chalcedonians aside, it runs

down the middle of the channel. And what makes the city of the Byzantines so well situated, but that of the Chalcedonians the reverse, is precisely what I have just described—even though, to the eye, the position of both seems equally favorable.

Yet, for all that, it is not easy to sail into the one even if you wish to, while to the other the current carries you of necessity, whether you wish it or not, as we have just explained.

Here is the proof: those who wish to cross from Chalcedon to Byzantium cannot sail straight across, because of the current in between, but instead coast up to the Cow and to the place called Chrysopolis—

the place the Athenians once seized and, on Alcibiades' advice, first used to levy tolls on ships sailing into the Pontus—and from there they let themselves run down with the current, which carries them inevitably to Byzantium.

The case is much the same with the voyage on the other side of Byzantium:

for whether a man runs from the Hellespont before south winds, or toward the Hellespont from the Pontus before the Etesian winds, the voyage along the European shore from Byzantium is straight and easy to manage as far as the narrows of the Propontis at Abydos and Sestos, and likewise back again from there to Byzantium;

but from Chalcedon along the Asian side the case is the opposite, since the coastal route is broken up by bays and the territory of the Cyzicenes projects far out.

For a man traveling from the Hellespont to Chalcedon to keep to the European route, and then, as he draws near the region of Byzantium, to turn and run across to Chalcedon, is difficult, on account of the current and the reasons already given.

Likewise, for one sailing out from Chalcedon to run straight across to Thrace is altogether impossible, both because of the current in between and because each of the winds blows contrary to both attempts;

for it is the south wind that brings you into the Pontus and the north wind that takes you out, and these are necessarily the winds one must rely on for each of the two voyages.
