# Book 12 Chapter 17: A Criticism on Ephorus and Callisthenes

So that I may not seem to be running down such distinguished writers without warrant, I will recall a single battle — one that happens to be among the most celebrated ever fought, not very remote from us in time, and, most importantly, one at which Callisthenes himself was present.

I mean the engagement in Cilicia between Alexander and Darius. Callisthenes says that Alexander had by then already made his way through the narrow passes and through what are called the Cilician Gates, while Darius, taking the route through the so-called Amanid Gates, came down with his army into Cilicia.

On learning from the local people that Alexander was advancing in the direction of Syria, Darius followed after him, and on coming up close to the passes he encamped beside the river Pinarus.

The width of the ground, he says, was no more than fourteen stades from the sea up to the foot of the mountains.

Across this stretch the aforementioned river ran at an angle: right where it issued from the mountains its banks were broken with gullies, and in its course through the level country down to the sea it had steep and difficult bluffs.

Having laid down this description, he says that when Alexander's men, having turned about, began drawing near and advancing back against them, Darius and his officers decided to draw up the whole phalanx within the encampment itself, just as it had been from the start, and to use the river as a defensive barrier, since it flowed right past the camp.

After this, he says, they stationed the cavalry along the sea, the mercenaries next to them right along the river, and the peltasts beyond these, linking up with the mountains.

# Book 12 Chapter 18: The Battle of Issus

But how he could have drawn these troops up in front of the phalanx, when the river was flowing right beside the encampment, is hard to understand — especially given that they were present in such great numbers.

For there were thirty thousand cavalry, as Callisthenes himself states, and thirty thousand mercenaries; and it is easy to work out how much ground these would have needed.

At the very most, cavalry is drawn up eight deep for any real practical purpose, and between each squadron there has to be an interval equal to its frontage, so that they can be useful in wheeling and in countermarching.

On this reckoning a stade holds eight hundred horsemen, ten stades hold eight thousand, and four stades hold three thousand two hundred; so that the fourteen-stade space would be filled by eleven thousand two hundred.

But if he were to deploy the whole thirty thousand, the cavalry alone would fall only a little short of forming three phalanxes one behind another.

Into what space, then, was the mass of mercenaries drawn up? Unless, by Zeus, behind the cavalry — but he does not say that; rather, he says that these mercenaries clashed with the Macedonians during the advance.

From this one is forced to take it that the cavalry's line occupied half the ground, the half by the sea, and the mercenaries the other half, the half toward the mountains.

And from these figures it is easy to reckon how deep the cavalry stood, and how great a distance the river must have been from the encampment.

After this, he says that as the enemy drew near, Darius himself, who was positioned at the center of the line, called the mercenaries over to himself from the wing.

But how this can be meant is a puzzle: the junction of the mercenaries and the cavalry must have been at the center of the ground, so that, since Darius was right there among the mercenaries, where and to what point and how could he have been calling the mercenaries over?

Finally, he says that the cavalry from the right wing led an advance and charged into Alexander's force, and that these received them gallantly, charged back, and made a fierce fight of it.

But that there was a river in between — and a river of just the kind he had himself described a moment before — he forgot.

# Book 12 Chapter 19: Callisthenes Vague on Alexander's Movements

His account of Alexander's movements is much the same. He says that Alexander made his crossing into Asia with forty thousand infantry and four thousand five hundred cavalry,

and that as he was about to invade Cilicia a further force came from Macedonia: five thousand infantry and eight hundred cavalry.

Even if one were to subtract from these three thousand infantry and three hundred cavalry — making the most generous allowance for losses incurred along the way in the various operations — there would still be left forty-two thousand infantry and five thousand cavalry.

On these assumptions, he says that Alexander learned of Darius's arrival in Cilicia when he was a hundred stades away from him, having already passed through the narrows;

and that he therefore turned about and made his way back through the narrows, leading first the phalanx, then the cavalry, and last of all the baggage train. As soon as the head of the column emerged into open ground,

he gave the order for everyone to extend the line, bringing up the phalanx alongside, and made its depth thirty-two; then, after that, again into sixteen; and finally, as he neared the enemy, into eight.

But these are even greater absurdities than the ones already mentioned. A stade, on the marching intervals, holds — when the men are sixteen deep — sixteen hundred men, each man occupying six feet.

It is plain, then, that ten stades will hold sixteen thousand, and twenty stades twice that number.

From this it is readily seen that at the moment Alexander made his force sixteen deep, the width of ground had to be twenty stades, and there would still be left over all the cavalry and ten thousand of the infantry.

# Book 12 Chapter 20: Inaccuracies about Tactics

After this he says that Alexander led his force forward in line abreast, when he was about forty stades from the enemy.

It is hard to imagine an absurdity greater than this. For where could anyone find ground of such a kind — and especially in Cilicia — as to lead a sarissa-bearing phalanx in line abreast over a front of twenty stades and a depth of forty?

So many are the obstacles to such a formation and operation that one could not easily even count them. But one of the things mentioned by Callisthenes himself is enough to settle the matter:

he says that the winter torrents pouring down from the mountains make so many washed-out gullies across the plain that, as he reports, most of the Persians too perished in such hollows during their flight.

But, by Zeus — it may be said — Alexander wanted to be ready for the enemy's appearance.

Yet what is less ready than a phalanx broken up and strung out along its front? For how much easier it is to deploy from a suitable marching order than to bring a force that is broken up and pulled apart along its front onto a single straight line and form it up for battle in wooded and broken country!

It was therefore far better — not just by a little — to advance in a double or quadruple phalanx of suitable form, for which it is not impossible to find marching ground, and which it is easy to deploy quickly, since one can learn of the enemy's presence well in advance through the advance scouts.

But this man, apart from everything else, did not even post the cavalry in front while leading his force in line abreast over level ground; instead he set them on an equal footing with the infantry.
