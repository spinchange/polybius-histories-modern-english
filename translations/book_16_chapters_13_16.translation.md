# Book 16 Chapter 13: Affairs in Greece

II. Affairs in Greece

I have already explained what policy Nabis, tyrant of the Lacedaemonians, adopted from the beginning, and how, after driving out the citizens, he freed the slaves and married them to their masters' wives and daughters.

I have also shown how he built up his power by making Sparta a kind of inviolable refuge for those who fled their own countries because of impiety or wickedness, and so gathered there a crowd of lawless men.

I will now explain how, at this same time, although he was the ally of the Aetolians, Eleans, and Messenians, and was bound by oath and treaty to help them if anyone attacked them, he disregarded all these pledges and attempted a treacherous attack on the city of the Messenians.

# Book 16 Chapter 14: Digression on the Merits of the Historians Zeno and Antisthenes of Rhodes

Polybius says that since some historians of particular episodes have written about this same period, in which the events at Messene and the sea-battles already mentioned took place, he wishes to discuss them briefly.

He will not speak of them all, but only of those whom he thinks worthy of remembrance and distinction, namely Zeno and Antisthenes of Rhodes.

I judge them worthy of notice for several reasons. They were contemporaries of the events, they had practical political experience, and in general they composed their histories not for profit but for reputation and from a sense of duty proper to public men.

Since they write about the same events that I do, I must not pass them over in silence, lest, because of the name of their country and the idea that naval matters especially belong to the Rhodians, readers eager to learn should follow them rather than me wherever I disagree with them.

Now to begin with, both of them describe the battle off Lade as no less serious than the battle off Chios, indeed as more active and more daring both in detail and in the overall conduct of the engagement, and they declare that the victory belonged to the Rhodians.

For my part, I could allow historians some inclination to favor their own countries, but not to make statements that are contrary to the facts.

Writers already commit enough errors from ignorance, errors that it is hard for any human being completely to avoid.

But if we deliberately write falsehoods, whether for the sake of country or friends or favor, how are we any different from those who make their living by such conduct?

Just as they ruin the credit of their compositions by measuring everything by gain, so political writers, drawn by hatred or affection, often fall into the same fault.

Therefore readers ought to watch carefully in this matter, and writers ought to be on their guard themselves.

# Book 16 Chapter 15: The Battle of Lade According to Zeno and Antisthenes

What I mean becomes clear from the facts themselves. These writers admit in their detailed account of the battle off Lade that two Rhodian quinqueremes were captured by the enemy with all their crews.

They also admit that when one ship in the battle hoisted a sail because it had been damaged and was taking on water, many of the nearby ships did the same and withdrew toward the open sea.

In the end, they say, the admiral was left with only a few vessels and was forced to do the same.

For the moment they were driven by conditions to anchor off the territory of Myndus, and on the next day they put out again and crossed over to Cos.

The enemy meanwhile secured the quinqueremes and, anchoring off Lade, took up quarters in the Rhodian camp.

They add that the Milesians, astonished by what had happened, crowned not only Philip but also Heracleides because of the expedition.

So after relating facts that are obviously the marks of defeat, they still declare, both in their detailed account and in their general conclusion, that the Rhodians were victorious.

And this although the actual dispatch sent at that time by the admiral to the council and prytaneis still remains in their own prytaneum, and it agrees not with the judgments of Antisthenes and Zeno but with mine.

# Book 16 Chapter 16: Zeno's Account of the Attack on Messene

Next after this they write about the treacherous breach of treaty against the Messenians.

In this section Zeno says that Nabis set out from Sparta, crossed the Eurotas near the river called Hoplites, and marched by the narrow road past Poliasium until he reached the region around Sellasia.

From there, he says, he made for Thalamae and by way of Pharae came to the river Pamisus.

I hardly know what one ought to say about this. The whole statement is as absurd as if someone were to say that a man set out from Corinth, crossed the Isthmus, and by the Scironian road immediately came to the Contoporia and then traveled past Mycenae to Argos.

For these places are not just slightly out of line with one another; they lie in opposite directions. The Isthmus and the Scironian road are east of Corinth, while the Contoporia and Mycenae lie toward the southwest.

So it is wholly impossible to get to the latter by way of the former.

The same thing is true in the case of Laconia.

The Eurotas and the region of Sellasia lie to the northeast of Sparta, while Thalamae, Pharae, and the Pamisus lie to the southwest.

So a man intending to go into Messenia by way of Thalamae ought not to go toward Sellasia, indeed ought not even to cross the Eurotas at all.
