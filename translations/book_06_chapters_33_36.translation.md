# Book 6 Chapter 33: Guard Duty and Other Assignments

Once the camp has been laid out, the tribunes gather everyone in it, free and slave alike, and administer an oath to each man individually.

Each swears that he will steal nothing from the camp, and that if he finds anything he will bring it to the tribunes.

From the maniples of principes and hastati in each legion they next assign two maniples to the open square in front of the tribunes' tents.

Since the Romans spend most of the daytime in that area, they are careful to have it constantly watered and kept clean.

Of the remaining eighteen maniples, each tribune receives three by lot, since each legion under this arrangement has three such maniples assigned to each of the six tribunes.

Each of those three maniples in turn performs the following services for its tribune: when the army encamps they pitch his tent and level the ground around it; if any part of his baggage has to be fenced off for protection, they do that as well.

They also provide him with two pickets, each of four men, some stationed in front of the tent and others behind it by the horses.

Since each tribune has three maniples, and each maniple contains more than a hundred men—not counting the triarii and velites, who are exempt from this duty—

and since the duty falls to each maniple only once every four days, the burden is light, while the arrangement gives the tribunes both the service they need and an impressive dignity.

The maniples of triarii are excused from attendance on the tribunes, but each maniple supplies a daily guard to the cavalry squadron quartered just behind it.

These men keep watch in general, but especially over the horses, so that they do not get entangled in their tethers, injure themselves, or break loose and plunge into the rest, throwing the camp into confusion.

Finally, one maniple out of the whole force takes turns each day in mounting guard around the consul, both to protect him from plots and to add splendor to his rank.

# Book 6 Chapter 34: Daily Orders and Watchwords

For digging the ditch and building the rampart, two sides of the camp are assigned to the allies, the sides by which their two wings are quartered, and two to the Romans, one for each legion.

Each side is divided maniple by maniple, and the centurions supervise the work section by section, while two tribunes oversee the entire side.

These same tribunes also direct the rest of the camp's business. They divide themselves into pairs, and each pair takes charge in turn for two months out of the six.

Whichever pair is on duty at the time supervises the operations in the field. The prefects of the allies divide their responsibilities in the same way.

At dawn the cavalry officers and centurions report to the tribunes, and the tribunes in turn go to the consul.

He gives them whatever instructions are needed, the tribunes pass these on to the officers, and the officers then convey them to the troops whenever the occasion requires.

The night watchword is distributed in the following manner.

From each class of troops, cavalry and infantry alike, one man is chosen from the tenth and last maniple, the unit quartered at the end of the street.

This man is excused from guard duty and presents himself each evening at sunset at the tribune's tent, where he receives the watchword written on a tablet; then he withdraws.

Returning to his own maniple, he hands over both tablet and watchword before witnesses to the commander of the next maniple, and that man does the same to the commander beyond him. So it passes from hand to hand until it reaches the first maniples, those nearest the tribunes, and these must return the tablet to the tribunes before dark.

If all the issued tablets are returned, the tribune knows that the watchword has passed through the whole line.

If one is missing, he can tell from the inscription where the failure occurred and immediately investigates, so that the man responsible receives the punishment he deserves.

# Book 6 Chapter 35: Night Watches

Their system for the night watches is as follows. The consul and his tent are guarded by the maniple assigned to that duty.

The tribunes' quarters and the cavalry squadrons are guarded by the men detailed from each maniple in the manner already described.

Each separate unit also posts its own guard from among its own men, while the remaining watches are assigned by the consul.

Usually there are three sentries at the quaestor's tent, and two at the tent of each legate and each member of the council.

The outer perimeter is guarded by the velites, who keep watch along the whole rampart by day, and who also place sentries at the gates, ten at each entrance.

As for the men appointed to the actual watches, the rear-rank officer of each company brings the one assigned to the first watch to the tribune in the evening, one man from each picket.

The tribune gives each of them a small wooden token marked for that watch, and with these they go off to their assigned posts.

The duty of making the rounds belongs to the cavalry. The first squadron commander in each legion must early in the morning order one of his rear-rank men to notify four young troopers of that squadron that they are to patrol.

In the evening that same man informs the commander of the next squadron that the duty passes to him on the following day.

That commander makes the same arrangements for the day after, and so the responsibility continues in rotation.

The four men selected from the first squadron draw lots for the different watches, then go to the tribune and receive written notice of how many watches they must inspect and which they are.

After this, the four take up their position beside the first maniple of the triarii, for the centurion of that maniple is responsible for sounding the bugle for each watch.

When the time comes, the man on whom the first watch has fallen by lot goes the rounds for that watch, taking some companions with him as witnesses.

He inspects the appointed guards—not only those on the rampart and at the gates, but also those stationed with the individual maniples and cavalry squadrons.

If he finds the men of the watch awake, he takes back their token; if he finds anyone asleep or absent from his post, he calls his companions to witness it and then moves on.

Those assigned to the later watches do the same.

The sounding of the bugle for each watch, so that the patrols and the sentries keep the same reckoning, belongs to the centurions of the first maniple of the triarii, one in each of the two camps, in daily rotation.

At daybreak each patrol brings the collected tokens back to the tribune. If all are returned, the guards are dismissed without accusation.

But if any patrol brings back fewer than the proper number, the missing picket is identified from the mark on the token.

Once the responsible watch is identified, the tribune summons the centurion, who brings forward the men assigned to that guard, and they are confronted with the patrolman.

If the fault clearly lies with the guards, the patrolman is immediately acquitted, since he is required to produce witnesses and did so. But if he failed to do that, the blame falls upon the patrolman himself.
