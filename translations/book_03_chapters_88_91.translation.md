# Book 3 Chapter 88: Hannibal Marches Through Iapygia

Hannibal, shifting his camp from place to place over short distances, lingered in the region along the Adriatic. Because he had a great supply of old wine, he washed down his horses with it and so cured them of their poor condition and their mange.

In much the same way he restored his men, healing the wounded and bringing the rest into good physical shape and a keen readiness for the tasks ahead.

Then, after passing through and laying waste the territories of Praetutia and Hadria, and further those of the Marrucini and the Frentani, he set out and made his march into Iapygia.

This region is divided under three names—its peoples being called Daunii, Peucetii, and Messapii—and the first district he invaded was Daunia.

Beginning there from Luceria, which is a Roman colony, he plundered the countryside.

After this he encamped near the place called Vibo, overran the territory of Arpi, and ravaged all of Daunia without opposition.

At this same time Fabius, after his appointment, sacrificed to the gods and then set out together with his master of the horse and the four legions that had been enrolled for the occasion.

Joining the forces that had come to his aid from Ariminum near Narnia, he relieved Gnaeus, the existing commander, of the land command and sent him off under escort to Rome, with instructions that, should the Carthaginians make any movement by sea, he should always come to the rescue as circumstances required.

Fabius himself, with his master of the horse, took over the forces and encamped facing the Carthaginians near the place called Aecae, keeping about fifty stades from the enemy.

# Book 3 Chapter 89: Fabius Defers Battle

Hannibal, realizing that Fabius had arrived and wishing to strike fear into his opponents by an immediate assault, led out his army, drew close to the Roman entrenchment, and formed up for battle. But after waiting for some time, with no one coming out against him, he again withdrew to his own camp.

For Fabius had resolved neither to expose himself to risk nor to stake everything on a battle, but to make the safety of those under his command his first and foremost aim; and he held firmly to this decision.

At the outset he was despised for it and gave occasion for talk that he had turned coward and was terrified of the danger. But in time he compelled everyone to admit and agree that no one could possibly have handled the circumstances then prevailing with greater sense or prudence.

And events soon bore witness in favor of his reasoning—and reasonably so.

For it happened that the enemy's forces had been continuously trained in warfare from their earliest years; that they had as their leader a man brought up among them and schooled from boyhood in operations in the field;

that they had won many battles in Iberia, and had twice in succession beaten the Romans and their allies; and, most important of all, that having given up everything else, they had one single hope of survival—in victory.

With the Roman army the very opposite of all this was the case.

For this reason Fabius was unable to commit to a decisive engagement, since his inferiority was plain to see. Instead, falling back in his calculations upon his own advantages, he kept to these and conducted the war by their means.

And the Romans' advantages were inexhaustible resources

and an abundance of manpower. And so, in the period that followed, he kept maneuvering alongside the enemy, and by his experience he would seize beforehand all the favorable positions.

Since he had ample supplies at his back, he never let his soldiers go out foraging or get separated from the camp at all, but kept them always concentrated and massed together, watching for the right places and moments.

And many of the enemy who, out of contempt for him, strayed from their own camp to forage he captured and destroyed in this manner.

He did this with a double purpose: at once to keep whittling down the enemy from their fixed numbers, and at the same time gradually to build up and restore the spirits of his own troops, which had been beaten down in the larger contests, through these successes in detail.

But to come down to a decisive trial of strength by mutual agreement he was in no way willing.

None of this, however, pleased Marcus, his master of the horse. Currying favor with the crowd, he disparaged Fabius to everyone, alleging that he was conducting affairs in an ignoble and sluggish way, while he himself was eager to take the risk and fight it out.

The Carthaginians, after laying waste the districts mentioned, crossed the Apennines and descended into the land of Samnium—a prosperous country untouched by war for many years—and found themselves amid such abundance of provisions that they could not exhaust the plunder by use or by destruction. They also overran the territory of Beneventum,

which is a Roman colony, and took the city of Venusia, which was unwalled and full of property of every kind in great quantity.

The Romans kept following continually behind, staying one or two days' march away, yet they were unable to come near or grapple with the enemy.

Therefore Hannibal, seeing that Fabius was plainly avoiding battle but was not on the whole giving up the open country, made a bold move into the plains around Capua, and within them to the place called Falernum,

convinced that one of two things would result: either he would force the enemy to fight, or he would make it clear to all that he was master of the field and that the Romans were yielding the open country to him.

Should that happen, he hoped the cities, struck with dismay, would be moved to revolt from Rome.

For up to that time, though the Romans had now been defeated in two battles, not a single city in Italy had gone over to the Carthaginians; they all kept their loyalty, even though some were suffering badly.

From this one may gauge the awe and esteem the Roman state inspired among its allies.

# Book 3 Chapter 91: Fertility and Beauty of the Plains Near Capua

And in fact Hannibal had good reason for arriving at these calculations.

For the plains around Capua are the most renowned in all Italy, both for their richness and their beauty and because they lie right on the sea and have at their disposal those trading ports into which sailors bound for Italy run from nearly every part of the inhabited world.

They also contain within them the most famous and most beautiful cities of Italy.

Their coastline is occupied by the people of Sinuessa, of Cumae, and of Puteoli, and besides these the Neapolitans, and lastly the nation of the Nucerians.

Of the interior, the parts toward the north are inhabited by the people of Cales and of Teanum, and those toward the east and south by the Daunii*

and the Nolans. In the very center of the plains lies Capua, the city that was once the most blessed of all.

The story told even by the mythographers about these plains is most plausible: these too are called the Phlegraean plains, just like others among the famous plains. And it is most likely that the gods quarreled over these in particular because of their beauty and their fertility.

Along with the advantages already mentioned, the plains are thought to be strong and altogether difficult to enter; for they are enclosed on one side by the sea and for the greater part by great mountains, continuous on every side, through which there are only three passes from the interior, narrow and hard to traverse:

one from Samnium, a second from Latium, and the last from the region around the Hirpini.

For this reason the Carthaginians, by encamping in these plains as in a theater, were bound to strike everyone with astonishment at the unexpected sight, to expose their enemies as cowards shrinking from battle, and to show themselves beyond dispute the masters of the open country.
