# Book 4 Chapter 85: Apelles Repeats his Accusation to Aratus Before Philip

At first Philip, having accepted these arguments, ordered that Aratus and his party be summoned, and that Apelles state these same charges in their presence.

When they had arrived, Apelles repeated what he had said before, boldly and in a menacing manner; and while the king still kept silent, he added something further of this kind:

"Since the king finds you, Aratus, so ungrateful and so utterly unreasonable, he has resolved to call the Achaeans together, to render an account of these matters before them, and then to withdraw again to Macedonia."

But the elder Aratus took him up and urged Philip, as a general principle, never to believe anything that was said hastily or without examination,

and that whenever some report reached him against one of his friends or allies, he should make a more careful investigation before accepting the slander; for such conduct was both kingly and advantageous in every respect.

Therefore he now demanded that, concerning the things alleged by Apelles, the king should summon those who had heard them, bring forward into the open the man who had reported them to him, and leave nothing undone of what was possible toward learning the truth, before making any statement about these matters to the Achaeans.

# Book 4 Chapter 86: Aratus is Cleared

The king was satisfied with what was said, and declared that he would not treat the matter lightly but would investigate it; and so for the time being the meeting was dissolved.

In the following days Apelles brought forward no proof of his allegations, while for Aratus and his party there occurred a coincidence of the following kind.

For the Eleans, at the time when Philip was ravaging their country, grew suspicious of Amphidamus and set out to arrest him and, after binding him, to send him off to Aetolia.

But he, perceiving their intention beforehand, withdrew at first to Olympia, and afterward, learning that Philip was occupied at Dyme with the disposal of the spoils, hurried to make his way across to him.

So Aratus and his friends, when they heard that Amphidamus had been driven out of Elis and was present, were overjoyed—since they were conscious of no wrongdoing—and, approaching the king, urged that he ought to summon Amphidamus;

for that man, to whom the words had been spoken, would know best about the charges and would make the truth plain, since he had fled from his home on Philip's account and now placed his hopes of safety, for the present, in the king.

Persuaded by these arguments, the king sent for Amphidamus and found that the accusation was false.

For this reason, from that day onward, he received and esteemed Aratus ever more and more, while toward Apelles he grew more distrustful; yet, being still held in the grip of his thorough regard for him, he was forced to overlook much of what Apelles did.

# Book 4 Chapter 87: Further Intrigues of Apelles

But Apelles in no way abandoned his purpose. Rather, he set about slandering Taurion, who had been appointed over affairs in the Peloponnese—

not by faulting him, but by praising him and asserting that he was the right man to spend his time in the field at the king's side; his real aim being to have someone else, of his own choosing, set over affairs in the Peloponnese.

For this is indeed a novel kind of slander that has been devised: to injure those around one not by blaming them, but by praising them.

And such malice, spite, and cunning have been invented above all and first by those who pass their lives about the courts, springing from their jealousy and greed toward one another.

In the same way he also kept biting at Alexander, who had been appointed over the king's personal attendants, whenever he found an opportunity, wishing both to bring the guard of the king's person under his own control and, in general, to overturn the arrangement left in place by Antigonus.

For Antigonus, while he lived, presided nobly over both the kingdom and his son, and at his death he likewise made good provision beforehand, with respect to the future, for all his affairs.

For he left a will in which he wrote to the Macedonians about how matters had been administered, and likewise gave instructions about the future—how and through whom each thing was to be handled—wishing to leave no occasion for the courtiers to engage in rivalry and faction with one another.

Among those who were then on campaign with him, Apelles himself had been left as one of the guardians, Leontius over the peltasts, Megaleas over the secretariat, Taurion over affairs in the Peloponnese, and Alexander over the king's personal attendants.

Now Leontius and Megaleas he had wholly under his own control, but Alexander and Taurion he sought to remove from their posts, eager to manage both these offices and all the others through himself and his own friends.

And this he would easily have accomplished, had he not raised up Aratus as an adversary to himself; but as it was, he quickly got a taste of his own folly and greed.

For the very thing he set out to do against those around him, this he himself suffered, and that in a very short time indeed.

But how and in what manner this came to pass we shall postpone for the present, and shall bring this book to a close; in what follows we shall try to report clearly on each of these events.

Philip, having made the arrangements described above, returned to Argos, and there spent the winter quarters with his friends, while he dismissed his forces to Macedonia.
