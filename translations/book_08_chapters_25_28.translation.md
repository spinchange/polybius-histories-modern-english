# Book 8 Chapter 25: Antiochus the Great at Armosata

After this they made a second expedition, handled matters in much the same way, and exchanged mutual pledges of good faith with Hannibal.

The agreement was that the Carthaginians would set the Tarentines free and would neither exact tribute from them in any form nor impose any other burden upon them; but once they had taken the city, the houses and lodgings occupied by Romans were to be open to the Carthaginians for plunder.

They also arranged upon a signal so that, whenever they came, the guards would promptly admit them into the camp.

Once this had been done, they were able to meet Hannibal repeatedly, sometimes going out of the city as though on a raid, at other times as though for hunting.

After putting these matters in order for the future, most of the conspirators watched for the proper opportunities, while they assigned Philemenus to manage the hunting expeditions.

For because of his excessive eagerness for the sport, he had the reputation of considering nothing in life more important than hunting.

So they entrusted it to him to win over first Gaius Livius, the officer set over the city, and then the men guarding the tower by the gate called the Temenid Gates, by means of the animals he brought in.

Having undertaken this task, partly by his own hunting and partly with game supplied to him through Hannibal, he continually brought animals in, giving some to Gaius and some to the men at the gate, so that they would open the wicket to him readily.

For he usually made his entrances and exits by night, pretending to fear the enemy, though in fact he was fitting his conduct to the design they had in hand.

When this habit of Philemenus with the gate guards had become so established that they no longer hesitated, but whenever he came near the wall and whistled by night the wicket was immediately opened for him,

they then watched for a day on which the Roman commander in the city was expected to spend the whole day with a large company in the place called the Museum near the marketplace, and fixed that day with Hannibal.

# Book 8 Chapter 26: The Hannibalian War — Tarentum

Hannibal had long since provided himself with the excuse of being ill, so that the Romans would not be surprised to hear that he remained for a considerable time in the same place.

At this moment he pretended illness even more strongly. His camp was three days' march from Tarentum.

When the proper time came, he selected from both cavalry and infantry those most distinguished for mobility and daring, about ten thousand in number, and ordered them to carry provisions for four days.

Then, making his camp break up just before dawn, he marched on at speed. He also chose about eighty Numidian horsemen and ordered them to ride thirty stades ahead of the army and scour the country on both sides of the road,

so that no one would catch sight of the main force, but anyone they encountered would either fall into their hands, or, if he escaped, would report in the city only that there was a raid by Numidians.

After the Numidians had advanced about one hundred and twenty stades, Hannibal took supper by a river that was hard to overlook and ran through ravines.

He assembled his officers and did not fully disclose the enterprise, but in general terms encouraged them, first, to show themselves brave men, since never before had greater prizes been set before them;

second, that each officer should keep the men under him together on the march and sternly rebuke any who left their own ranks;

and finally, that they should pay attention to the orders given and do nothing on their own initiative beyond what was commanded.

After speaking thus and dismissing the officers, he set the advance in motion just as darkness fell, eager to reach the wall around midnight, with Philemenus as guide, and having made ready for him a wild boar for the part he had to play.

# Book 8 Chapter 27: A Bargain Made with Hannibal

Gaius Livius, acting on the expectation fostered by the young men, spent the day with his usual companions in the Museum, and when the drinking was already at its height, toward sunset a report was brought that the Numidians were ravaging the countryside.

He concerned himself only with that, and after summoning some of his officers ordered half the cavalry to ride out before dawn and stop the enemy from plundering the land. This made him even less suspicious of the whole affair.

Nicon, Tragiscus, and the rest gathered together in the city at nightfall and waited for the return of Livius and his party.

Since they rose quickly from the banquet because it had begun early, the main body of the conspirators withdrew to a certain place and stayed there; but some of the young men met Gaius and his companions, in a loose disorderly group, joking among themselves as though they too were returning from a drinking party.

As Livius and his people were made even more unsteady by wine, laughter and playfulness sprang up on both sides the moment they met.

When they had escorted him home, Gaius went to his rest in a drunken state, as is natural for men who begin drinking in daylight, with no uneasy suspicion in his mind but full of cheerfulness and slack ease.

Nicon and Tragiscus then rejoined the young men they had left behind and, dividing themselves into three groups, watched the most favorable approaches to the marketplace, so that nothing happening either outside the walls or in the city itself would escape them.

They also took up position near the house of Gaius, knowing perfectly well that if any suspicion arose of what was coming, the first warning would be carried to Livius and that all countermeasures would begin from him.

When the return from the dinners and, in general, that sort of noise had now passed away, and the mass of the townspeople had fallen asleep, and the night was advancing while their hopes remained unshaken, they gathered together and moved forward to execute the plan.

# Book 8 Chapter 28: Tarentum Betrayed To Hannibal

The agreement between the young men and the Carthaginians was as follows.

Hannibal, approaching the city from the inland side on its eastern face near the so-called Temenid Gates, was to light a fire on the tomb which some called the tomb of Hyacinthus and others the tomb of Apollo Hyacinthus.

Then Tragiscus and his companions, when they saw this, were to answer with a fire from inside.

Once that signal had been completed, Hannibal's men were to extinguish their fire and advance slowly toward the gate.

With this arrangement settled, the young men passed through the inhabited quarter of the city and came to the tombs.

For the eastern side of Tarentum is full of tombs, because the Tarentines even now bury all their dead within the walls in obedience to an ancient oracle.

The god, they say, declared to them that it would be better and more profitable for them to dwell with the greater number.

Thinking that they would best obey the oracle if even their dead remained within the walls, they therefore still bury the departed inside the gates.

So the men already mentioned, after coming to the tomb of Pythionicus, waited to see what would happen.

When Hannibal and his party drew near and did what had been arranged, Nicon and Tragiscus took heart, displayed their own answering beacon, and when they saw the Carthaginians' fire again being put out, rushed at speed toward the gate,

wishing to get there first and kill the men stationed in the gate-tower, since it had been agreed that the Carthaginians should make their advance slowly and at a walk.

Everything went smoothly: the guards were caught before they were ready, some of the young men killed them, and others cut the bars.

The gates were quickly opened, and Hannibal's party arrived at exactly the right moment, having timed their march so well that they never once had to halt on the road to the city.
