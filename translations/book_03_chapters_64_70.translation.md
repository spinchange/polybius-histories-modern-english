# Book 3 Chapter 64: Scipio Crosses the Ticinus

Around these same days Publius, having by now crossed the river Po and having decided to push forward across the Ticinus, ordered the men skilled in such work to build a bridge over it, while he gathered the rest of his forces together and addressed them.

Most of what he said concerned the standing of their homeland and the deeds of their ancestors, but the part bearing on the present occasion ran as follows.

He told them that, even though they had as yet gained no firsthand experience of the enemy, the mere knowledge that they were about to risk battle against Carthaginians ought to give them an unquestionable confidence in victory.

In general, he said, it should be considered both outrageous and absurd that Carthaginians should dare to look Romans in the face—men who had so often been beaten by them, who had paid them so much tribute, and who for all these years had been all but their slaves.

And when, on top of all this, they now had some measure of proof of the men presently facing them—namely that they did not even dare to look them in the eye—what conclusion ought those who reason correctly to draw about the future?

Moreover, when their cavalry had clashed with the Romans' own cavalry near the river Rhône, they had not come off well; on the contrary, they had lost many of their men and fled in disgrace all the way back to their own camp;

and their general and his entire army, upon learning of the arrival of the Roman troops, had made a withdrawal that closely resembled flight and, against their own intentions, had out of fear taken the route over the Alps.

He said too that Hannibal was now present having ruined the greater part of his force, while what was left of it was useless and hard to employ because of the hardships it had endured; likewise he had lost most of his horses and rendered the rest worthless through the length and difficulty of the march.

By all this he tried to show that they had only to appear before the enemy.

Above all he urged them to take courage by looking to his own presence among them: for he would never have abandoned his fleet and the campaign in Spain, on which he had been sent, and come here in such haste, had he not seen, on entirely reasonable grounds, that this action was necessary for his homeland and that victory in it was assured.

When, both because of the speaker's credibility and because of the truth of what he said, the men were all eager for battle, he welcomed their enthusiasm and dismissed them, urging them in addition to be ready for whatever order was given.

# Book 3 Chapter 65: Cavalry Engagement on the Ticinus

On the very next day both armies advanced along the river on the side toward the Alps, the Romans keeping the stream on their left, the Carthaginians on their right.

On the second day, learning through their foraging parties that they were near one another, they encamped on the spot for the time being and stayed put.

But on the following day both sides took up their whole cavalry—and Publius his javelin-throwers from the infantry as well—and advanced through the plain, eager to scout out each other's forces.

As soon as they came near one another and caught sight of the dust rising up, they at once drew up for battle.

Publius, placing his javelin-throwers and the Gallic cavalry alongside them in front and stationing the rest in a single line, advanced at a walking pace.

Hannibal, drawing up his bridled cavalry and all the steady part of his horse in front, came to meet the enemy, while he held the Numidian cavalry ready on each wing for an encircling movement.

Since both the commanders and the cavalrymen on either side were keen for the fight, the first collision happened in such a way that the javelin-throwers had no chance even to let fly a first missile, but turned and fled at once back through the gaps to the rear of their own squadrons, terrified at the charge bearing down on them and afraid of being trampled by the oncoming cavalry.

The men who met face to face kept the contest evenly balanced for a long while;

for it was at once a cavalry fight and an infantry fight, because of the number of men who dismounted in the very midst of the battle.

When the Numidians had encircled them and fallen on their rear, the javelin-throwers on foot who had at first escaped the cavalry collision were now trampled down by the numbers and the onset of the Numidians;

while those who from the start had been fighting the Carthaginians face to face—having lost many of their own and destroyed still more of the Carthaginians—were thrown into disorder once the Numidians fell on them from behind. Most of them scattered in flight, but some rallied around their commander.

# Book 3 Chapter 66: Scipio Crosses the Po

Publius then broke camp and advanced through the plains toward the bridge over the Po, eager to get his troops across before the enemy could catch him.

For seeing that the country was level, that the enemy had the upper hand in cavalry, and that he himself was weighed down by his wound, he judged it necessary to bring his forces back to safety.

Hannibal at first supposed that they would risk a battle with their infantry as well; but when he saw that they had moved out of their camp, he pursued them as far as the first river and the bridge over it.

Finding most of the planks already pulled up, and the men guarding the bridge still left behind near the river, he took these prisoner—about six hundred in number—

but learning that the rest of the army had already gained a long lead, he turned back again in the opposite direction and made his way along the river, hurrying to reach a point on the Po that could be easily bridged.

After two days he halted and bridged the crossing with riverboats, ordered Hasdrubal to bring over the main body, and himself crossed at once and began to give audience to the envoys who had arrived from the neighboring districts.

For as soon as the victory had been won, all the Celts in the vicinity were eager—according to their original intention—to become his friends, supply him, and join the Carthaginians on campaign.

Having received those who came in a friendly way and brought his forces across from the far bank, he advanced along the river, marching in the opposite direction to his earlier route: for now he marched downstream, eager to close with the enemy.

Publius, having crossed the Po and encamped near the city of Placentia, which was a Roman colony, was at once tending both his own wound and the other casualties, and—reckoning that he had brought his forces into safety—kept quiet.

Hannibal, arriving on the second day after his crossing near the enemy, on the third day drew up his force in full view of his opponents.

When no one came out against them, he encamped, taking the distance between the camps to be about fifty stades.

# Book 3 Chapter 67: The Gauls Join Hannibal

The Celts serving alongside the Romans, seeing that the Carthaginians' prospects were now the more promising, arranged matters among themselves and watched for the right moment to make their move, each remaining in his own tent.

When the men within the rampart had taken their supper and gone to sleep, they let the greater part of the night pass and then, fully armed, around the morning watch fell upon the Romans encamped nearest to them.

They killed many of them and wounded not a few; and finally, cutting off the heads of the dead, they withdrew to the Carthaginians—about two thousand infantry and a little under two hundred cavalry.

Hannibal welcomed their arrival warmly, and at once, after encouraging them and promising each of them suitable rewards, sent them off to their own cities to report to their fellow citizens what had been done and to urge them toward an alliance with him.

For he knew that they would all be compelled to throw in their lot with him once they learned of the act of treachery their own countrymen had committed against the Romans.

At the same time the Boii also arrived and handed over to him the three men whom the Romans had sent out to distribute the land, and whom they had seized at the very beginning of the war by an act of treachery, as I have related above.

Hannibal, welcoming their goodwill, exchanged pledges of friendship and alliance with those present; but he gave the men back to them, instructing them to keep them under guard, so that through them they might recover their own hostages, in accordance with their original intention.

Publius, indignant at the act of treachery that had occurred, and reckoning that—since the Celts had long been hostile toward the Romans and these latest events had now taken place—all the surrounding Gauls would go over to the Carthaginians, decided that he must guard against what was coming.

Accordingly, when night came on, just before the morning watch he broke camp and made his march toward the river Trebia and the hills adjoining it, trusting in the strength of the terrain and in the allies who lived nearby.

# Book 3 Chapter 68: Tiberius Sempronius Joins Scipio

…the strength of the position and the allies who dwelt nearby. Hannibal, on learning of their withdrawal, at once sent off the Numidian cavalry and, not long after, the rest; following close behind these with his main force in person.

The Numidians, finding the camp deserted, set fire to it.

This proved a great benefit to the Romans; for had the Numidians followed close on their heels and caught up with the baggage train, many of them would have been killed by the cavalry on the open plains.

As it was, the greater part of them had already crossed the river Trebia; while of those left behind in the rearguard, some were killed and some taken alive by the Carthaginians.

Publius, then, having crossed the aforementioned river, encamped near the first range of hills.

Surrounding his camp with a ditch and palisade, he waited for Tiberius and the forces with him, and meanwhile tended himself carefully, eager—if he could—to take part in the coming battle.

Hannibal encamped on the spot, about forty stades from the enemy.

The mass of Celts who inhabited the plains, swept along by the Carthaginians' good prospects, supplied the army lavishly with provisions and were ready to share in every action and danger with Hannibal's men.

When word of the cavalry engagement reached those in Rome, they were taken aback that the outcome had run contrary to their expectations; yet they were not at a loss for excuses to keep what had happened from seeming a defeat.

Some blamed the general's rashness, others the deliberate treachery of the Celts, judging from their final defection.

In short, since the infantry legions remained intact, they reckoned that their hopes for the whole campaign were likewise intact.

So when Tiberius and the legions with him arrived and marched through Rome, the people imagined that the battle would be decided by his mere appearance on the scene.

When the soldiers had assembled at Ariminum according to their oath, the general took them up and led them forward, hurrying to join Publius's forces.

Having effected the junction and encamped beside them with his own forces, he let the men recover their strength—seeing that they had marched on foot from Lilybaeum to Ariminum for forty consecutive days—while he made all his preparations as for battle,

and he himself conferred carefully with Publius, both inquiring into what had already happened and considering with him the present situation.

# Book 3 Chapter 69: A Skirmish Near the Trebia

About the same time Hannibal, through an intrigue, seized the town of Clastidium, which was surrendered to him by the man entrusted with it by the Romans, a native of Brundisium.

Having become master of the garrison and the store of grain, he used the latter for his present needs, but took the captured men along with him unharmed,

wishing to give an example of his policy, so that those overtaken by circumstances should not be afraid and despair of finding safety at his hands.

He honored the traitor lavishly, eager to entice those placed in positions of authority toward the prospects offered by the Carthaginians.

After this, observing that certain Celts who lived between the Po and the river Trebia had made an alliance with him but were also sending messages to the Romans, persuaded that in this way they would secure their safety from both sides,

he sent out two thousand infantry and about a thousand cavalry, both Celts and Numidians, ordering them to overrun their territory.

When these had carried out the order and gathered a great deal of plunder, the Celts at once came to the Roman camp begging for help.

Tiberius, who had long been seeking an opening to take action, now had a pretext and sent out the greater part of his cavalry, and with them about a thousand javelin-throwers from the infantry.

These crossed the Trebia in haste and engaged the enemy in a fight over the plunder, and the Celts together with the Numidians were routed and began to withdraw to their own camp.

But the men posted in front of the Carthaginian camp quickly grasped what was happening and brought up the reserves from there to help those under pressure; when this happened, the Romans in turn were routed and fell back to their own camp.

Tiberius, seeing what was going on, sent out all his cavalry and javelin-throwers. When this clash occurred, the Celts again gave way and withdrew to their own place of safety.

But the Carthaginian general, not being prepared to settle the whole issue and believing that one should never bring on a decisive engagement without a deliberate plan or on any chance opportunity—which, it must be said, is the mark of a good commander—

at that point held back his men who had drawn near the Roman camp, forced them to halt and turn about, and prevented them from pursuing and grappling with the enemy, recalling them by means of his orderlies and trumpeters. The Romans, after holding their ground a short time, withdrew,

having lost a few of their own but destroyed more of the Carthaginians.

# Book 3 Chapter 70: Tiberius Sempronius Plans to Attack

Tiberius, elated and overjoyed at this success, was eager to settle the whole matter as quickly as possible.

Now it lay open to him to act on the present circumstances according to his own judgment, since Publius was ill; nevertheless, wishing to gain his colleague's support as well, he discussed these matters with him.

But Publius held the opposite view of the situation.

For he supposed that their legions would become better for wintering under arms; that the unreliability of the Celts would not hold to its loyalty while the Carthaginians remained idle and were forced to keep quiet, but would once again contrive some new move against them;

and besides this, that once he himself had recovered from his wound he would be able to render genuine service to the common cause.

For these reasons he urged Tiberius to stay with the present arrangements.

Tiberius knew that every one of these points was being stated truly and properly; but driven by his love of glory and trusting in his fortune beyond all reason, he was eager to decide the whole issue by himself, before Publius could be present at the battle and before the incoming consuls could take over the command—for that time was now at hand.

And so, choosing not the right moment dictated by circumstances but his own, he was bound to fail conspicuously in what was needed.

Hannibal, holding much the same view of the situation as Publius, was for that very reason eager—unlike him—to engage the enemy: first, because he wished to make use of the Celts' zeal while it was still fresh;

second, to engage the Roman legions while they were untrained and newly levied; third, to fight the battle while Publius was still unable to take part; and, most importantly, to be doing something and not to let the time slip away in vain.

For a man who has brought his armies into foreign territory and is attempting extraordinary ventures has just one means of safety: to keep continually renewing the hopes of his allies.
