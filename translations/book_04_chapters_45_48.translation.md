# Book 4 Chapter 45: Disadvantages of Byzantium On Land

What I am about to describe is the source of their difficulties. Since Thrace surrounds their territory on every side, running from one sea to the other, the Byzantines are locked in a perpetual and grueling war against the Thracians.

For they cannot, even by mounting a full campaign and defeating them, rid themselves of the war once and for all, because of the sheer number both of the common tribes and of the chieftains.

If they overcome one ruler, three others still more formidable march into their land.

Nor do they gain anything by giving way and submitting to tribute and treaties: for if they make a concession to one, they find by that very act five times as many enemies on their hands.

For this reason they are gripped by a perpetual and grueling war—for what is more precarious than a war with a barbarian neighbor on your own doorstep?

What is more terrible? Yet beyond wrestling with these utterly ruinous troubles on land, and apart from the other evils that attend any war, they also endure a kind of punishment worthy of Tantalus, as the poet describes it.

For though they possess the most fertile of lands, when they have worked it hard and the harvest comes in, outstanding in its abundance and quality, then the barbarians descend upon them and either destroy the crops or gather them up and carry them off.

And so, on top of the lost labor and expense, they look on at the devastation and, because the harvest was so fine, they cry out in indignation and bear what has happened with great bitterness.

Still, since they were accustomed to enduring the war with the Thracians, they kept it within its usual bounds and held to their original fair dealings with the other Greeks.

But when, on top of all this, the Gauls under Comontorius fell upon them as well—

# Book 4 Chapter 46: Byzantium, The Gauls, And Rhodians

—they were brought to the very brink of ruin. These Gauls had set out from their homeland together with Brennus; but having escaped the disaster at Delphi and reached the Hellespont, they did not cross over into Asia. Instead they stayed where they were, drawn to the region around Byzantium for its appeal.

Having defeated the Thracians and established Tyle as their royal seat, they brought the Byzantines into the gravest danger.

At first, during the raids carried out under Comontorius, their first king, the Byzantines kept buying them off with gifts of three thousand, or five thousand, and sometimes even ten thousand gold pieces, on the condition that they not ravage their land.

In the end the Byzantines were forced to agree to pay a yearly tribute of eighty talents, and this continued down to the reign of Cavarus, under whom the kingdom was dissolved and the whole Gallic people was wiped out, conquered in turn by the Thracians in a reversal of fortune.

It was during this period that the Byzantines, hard pressed by the tribute payments, at first sent embassies to the Greeks, asking them for help and joint support to meet the crisis surrounding them.

But since most of the Greeks paid no attention, the Byzantines, driven by necessity, set about—

# Book 4 Chapter 47: The Byzantines Institute a Toll

—levying a duty on those sailing into the Pontus. Since the Byzantines' collection of a toll on goods exported from the Pontus proved a serious loss and inconvenience to everyone, people regarded it as an outrage, and all the seafaring traders directed their complaints against the Rhodians, on the grounds that the Rhodians were considered the leading power at sea. It was out of this that the war arose,

the war I am now about to recount.

For the Rhodians,

roused to action partly by the injury done to themselves and partly by the loss falling on their neighbors, at first took their allies along and sent an embassy to the Byzantines, demanding that they abolish the transit toll.

But the Byzantines would not yield at all on the matter; they were convinced that they had justice on their side, on the strength of the rebuttal delivered before them by Hecatodorus and Olympiodorus to the Rhodian envoys—

for these men were at that time at the head of the Byzantine government—and so on that occasion the Rhodians went away having accomplished nothing.

On their return home, they voted for war against the Byzantines for the reasons stated above.

At once they dispatched envoys to Prusias, urging him too to join the war; for they knew that Prusias was already at odds, on several grounds, with the—

# Book 4 Chapter 48: Achaeus and Prusias I. of Bithynia

—Byzantines. The Byzantines, for their part, did much the same thing: they sent envoys both to Attalus and to Achaeus, asking them for help.

Now Attalus was eager to assist, but at that moment his influence carried little weight, since he had been hemmed in by Achaeus and confined to his ancestral dominion.

Achaeus, on the other hand, who controlled the territory on this side of the Taurus and had recently proclaimed himself king, promised to send aid.

His commitment to this course raised great hopes among the Byzantines, and produced the opposite effect—dismay—among the Rhodians and Prusias.

Achaeus was a kinsman of the Antiochus who had inherited the kingdom of Syria, and he came to hold the dominion just mentioned through circumstances such as these.

When Seleucus—the father of the aforementioned Antiochus—died, and Seleucus, the eldest of his sons, succeeded to the throne, Achaeus, because of his kinship, crossed the Taurus together with him, about two years before the events now being described.

For as soon as Seleucus the younger had succeeded to the kingdom and learned that Attalus had already brought the whole dominion on this side of the Taurus under his own control, he was spurred to defend his interests.

But after crossing the Taurus with a large army, he was treacherously murdered by Apaturius the Gaul and by Nicanor, and so lost his life.

Achaeus, as his kinship required, immediately avenged the murder by killing Nicanor and Apaturius, and took charge of the army and of affairs as a whole with prudence and magnanimity.

For although the opportunity lay open to him, and the impulse of the troops favored his placing the diadem on his own head, he chose not to do so; instead he kept the kingdom safe for Antiochus, the younger of the sons, and pressed on energetically to recover the whole region on this side of the Taurus.

But when affairs flowed his way beyond all expectation—since he had shut Attalus up within Pergamum itself and had become master of all the rest—he was carried away by his successes and at once ran off course.

Placing the diadem on his head and proclaiming himself king, he was at that time the weightiest and most formidable of all the kings and princes on this side of the Taurus.

And it was above all in him that the Byzantines placed their trust at that time when they took on the war against the Rhodians and Prusias.
