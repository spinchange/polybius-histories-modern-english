# Book 6 Chapter 37: The Tribunes' Court

When an accusation is brought, the tribunes at once convene a court, and if the accused is convicted he is punished by cudgeling.

The punishment works like this: the tribune takes a cudgel and merely touches the condemned man with it.

At that signal all the men in the camp attack him with clubs and stones, and most of them are killed on the spot inside the camp itself.

Even those who manage to escape are not really saved. How could they be? They cannot return to their own country, and no relative would dare receive such a man into his house. So anyone who falls into this misfortune is completely ruined.

The same penalty applies to the rear-rank officer and to the commander of the cavalry squadron if they fail to give the proper order at the proper time, the former to the patrols and the latter to the commander of the next squadron.

Because this punishment is so severe and unavoidable, the Roman night watches are kept with unfailing strictness.

The soldiers are answerable to the tribunes, and the tribunes to the consuls.

A tribune has authority to fine a man, seize his property as security, or have him flogged; among the allies the prefects have the same powers.

This cudgeling punishment is also inflicted on anyone who steals from the camp, bears false witness, is found guilty in his prime of shameful sexual misconduct, or has been punished three times for the same offense.

These are treated as crimes. But the following are classed as cowardice and military disgrace: falsely reporting brave deeds to the tribunes in order to win honors;

or, if men have been posted in reserve, abandoning the assigned position out of fear; likewise, if in the moment of danger a man throws away any of his weapons through fear.

That is why some men openly choose death at their post, unwilling to leave their station because they dread the punishment awaiting them.

Others, if they have dropped shield, sword, or some other piece of armor in battle, throw themselves recklessly at the enemy, hoping either to recover what they lost or to escape by death the certain shame and the insults of their family.

# Book 6 Chapter 38: Military Punishments and Rewards

If the same offense is committed by a larger body of men—for instance, if whole standards give way and abandon their ground under pressure—then they do not think it right to cudgel or execute them all.

Instead they have found a remedy that is both effective and terrifying. The tribune assembles the legion, brings the offenders into the middle, denounces them bitterly, and then selects by lot five, or eight, or sometimes twenty, always aiming at roughly a tenth of the men who were guilty of cowardice.

Those on whom the lot falls are put to death by cudgeling without mercy; the rest are given barley instead of wheat and are ordered to camp outside the rampart and outside the protection of the camp.

Since all face the same danger from the lottery, and since all alike are made an example of by the disgrace of barley rations, this is the strongest possible way both to inspire terror and to correct the failure that has occurred.

# Book 6 Chapter 39: Military Decorations

The Romans are also excellent at stirring young men to face danger bravely.

Whenever a battle has taken place and some men have distinguished themselves, the consul summons an assembly of the army, brings forward those whom he thinks have done anything notable, and first praises each man for his courage, mentioning any other honorable achievements of his life as well.

After that he grants rewards: to the man who has wounded an enemy, a spear; to the man who has struck him down and stripped him, a cup if he is an infantryman, horse-trappings if he is a cavalryman. In earlier times the spear alone was given.

These rewards are not given for deeds done in a pitched battle or in the capture of a city, but rather in skirmishes and similar situations where there was no strict necessity for a man to expose himself, and he took the risk deliberately and on his own initiative.

To those who are first over the wall in the storming of a city he gives a golden crown.

In the same way, the consul marks with gifts those who have shielded and saved any citizen or ally, and the tribunes require the men who were saved to crown their rescuer, whether willingly or, if necessary, by formal decision.

The man whose life was saved honors his rescuer for the rest of his life as if he were a father, and owes him every duty that a son owes a parent.

Such encouragement stirs not only those who witness it and stand present in the ranks, but also those who remain at home, to rivalry and zeal in the face of danger.

For those who win such rewards enjoy not only glory in the camp and immediate fame at home, but after returning to their own city they also take part in processions with special distinction, since only men honored by their generals for valor may wear these decorations.

They also display the spoils in the most prominent places in their houses, as signs and proofs of their own courage.

With such care given in the camp to rewards and punishments alike, it is no wonder that Roman warfare ends in brilliant and successful results.

An infantryman receives two obols a day in pay, a centurion double that, and a cavalryman a drachma.

The infantry ration is about two-thirds of an Attic medimnus of wheat per month. The cavalry receive seven medimni of barley and two of wheat.

Among the allies, the infantry receive the same amount, while the cavalry receive one and a third medimni of wheat and five of barley.

For the allies this ration is a free grant, but from the Romans the quaestor deducts at a fixed rate the cost of grain, clothing, and any additional weapons they may need, taking it out of their pay.

# Book 6 Chapter 40: The Army On the March

Their method of striking camp and marching is as follows.

At the first trumpet call everyone takes down the tents and packs the baggage; but no one may either strike or raise a tent before the tents of the tribunes and the consul have been dealt with.

At the second signal they load the baggage animals; at the third the first troops must advance and set the whole camp in motion.

In the vanguard they usually place the picked troops; next comes the right wing of the allies, and behind them their baggage animals.

After these follows the first Roman legion, with its own baggage behind it.

Then comes the second legion, followed by its own baggage and by the baggage of the allied wing assigned to the rear, for the left wing of the allies takes the last position in the march.

The cavalry sometimes ride behind their own divisions, and sometimes move alongside the baggage animals, keeping them together and providing them security.

If an attack is expected from the rear, the rest of the order remains much the same, except that the picked allied troops take over the rear instead of the vanguard.

On alternate days the legions and allied wings exchange the leading place, so that all alike share the advantage of reaching water and forage first.

In especially dangerous circumstances they use another order of march, when they are moving over open ground.

They then advance in three parallel columns of hastati, principes, and triarii, placing the baggage of the leading standards in front of all, the baggage of the second line behind the first, and the baggage of the third behind the second, so that baggage and fighting units alternate in succession.

With the column arranged in this way, if danger appears they wheel either to shield-side or spear-side, bring the standards clear of the baggage, and face the enemy.

Thus in a short time and by a single movement the heavy infantry take up a battle line, except when the hastati also need to wheel farther into position.

The baggage train and all those accompanying it then remain drawn up behind the line, in the proper place for safety in the coming danger.
