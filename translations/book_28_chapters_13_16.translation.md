# Book 28 Chapter 13: Marcius Declines Assistance from the Achaeans

Polybius and his colleagues found that the Romans had moved on from Thessaly and were encamped in Perrhaebia, between Azorus and Doliche.

So, because of the critical circumstances, they postponed their audience, but shared in the dangers involved in the advance into Macedonia.

But when the army had marched down into the region around Heracleum, it seemed that the time had come for an interview, since the general appeared to have accomplished the greatest part of what lay before him.

Taking this opportunity, they presented the decree to Marcius and made clear the purpose of the Achaeans: they had resolved, as a whole people, to share with him in the same struggles and dangers. Besides this, they pointed out that every request made of the Achaeans by the Romans during the present war, whether written or delivered by message, had been accepted without objection.

Marcius warmly approved the zeal of the Achaeans, but released them from the hardship and expense, since the situation no longer required assistance from allied forces. So the other envoys returned to Achaea,

but Polybius remained there and took part in the business at hand,

until Marcius, on hearing that Appius Cento was asking the Achaeans for five thousand soldiers to serve in Epirus, sent the aforesaid Polybius off, urging him to see that the troops were not granted and that the Achaeans did not incur so great an expense to no purpose; for Appius, he said, had no sound reason for asking for the soldiers.

Whether he did this out of concern for the Achaeans or because he wanted Appius to achieve nothing is hard to say.

At any rate, when Polybius withdrew to the Peloponnese, letters from Epirus had already arrived, and not long afterward the Achaeans had assembled at Sicyon, so he found himself in a very great difficulty.

When the deliberation was opened on the soldiers Cento was requesting, Polybius decided that he could not possibly disclose the private instructions Marcius had given him; yet to oppose the aid openly, when no clear reason appeared for doing so, was altogether dangerous.

Since the situation was awkward and full of complications, he used, for the immediate crisis, the senate's decree ordering that no one should comply with written demands from generals unless they acted in accordance with a decree of the senate.

And no such authority was attached to the letters.

Thus he carried the point, and the question was referred to the consul, and through this means the nation was relieved of an expense amounting to more than a hundred and twenty talents.

Yet he gave those who wished to slander him before Appius an opening, because he had cut short Appius's attempt to obtain the reinforcement.

# Book 28 Chapter 14: Treachery of the Cydonians

At this time the Cydonians committed a deed universally acknowledged to be dreadful and a flagrant violation of sworn faith.

For although many acts of that sort had taken place in Crete, what happened then seemed to surpass the ordinary measure even of such crimes.

They were bound to the Apolloniates not only by friendship, but by political union, and in general by partnership in all the rights that human beings regard as sacred; and concerning these obligations there stood a sworn treaty in the name of Zeus of Ida.

Yet in breach of that sworn agreement they seized the city of the Apolloniates, slaughtered the men, plundered their property, and, after dividing among themselves the women, children, city, and territory, kept possession of it.

# Book 28 Chapter 15: The Cydonians Ask Help from Eumenes

In Crete, the Cydonians were afraid of the Gortynians, because in the previous year they had narrowly escaped losing their city when Nothocrates and his party attempted to seize it. So they sent envoys to Eumenes, asking for help under the terms of their alliance.

The king selected Leon and sent him off in haste with three hundred soldiers.

When they arrived, the Cydonians handed over the keys of the gates to Leon and entrusted the city as a whole to him.

# Book 28 Chapter 16: Dissensions In Crete and Rhodes

At Rhodes the strife between rival parties kept growing more and more intense.

For when the senate's decree reached them, explaining that they should no longer pay attention to the commands of military leaders, but to the senate's own decrees, and when the majority approved the senate's foresight,

Philophron and Theaetetus and their associates seized on this opening and pressed the matter forward, saying that envoys ought to be sent to the senate, to Quintus Marcius the consul, and to Gaius, the commander of the fleet.

By that time it was already known to everyone which Roman magistrates were going to arrive in Greece.

So, though opposition was voiced, the proposal carried, and at the beginning of summer Agesilochus son of Hegesias was sent to Rome,

along with Nicagoras son of Nicander; while to the consul and the admiral they sent Agepolis,

Ariston, and Pasicrates, with instructions to renew their friendly relations with the Romans and to answer the accusations that some people were making against their city,

while Agesilochus and his colleagues were at the same time to speak about the export of grain.

What these men said to the senate, what answers the senate gave them, and how they returned after receiving every mark of goodwill, I have already recounted in the section on Italian affairs.

But on this point it is useful to remind the reader repeatedly, as I also try to do, because we are often compelled to report the audiences and negotiations of embassies before describing the circumstances of their appointment and dispatch.

For since in each year, while writing the events that properly belong to it, I try to bring together in one place a summary of the actions of each people, it is clear that this must necessarily happen in the course of the narrative.
