# Book 26 Chapter 1: Antiochus Epiphanes

Antiochus, though called Epiphanes, was nicknamed Epimanes from his conduct. Polybius says of him that he would sometimes slip away from the court, eluding his attendants, and then be seen wandering about aimlessly in some quarter of the city with only two or three companions.

Most often he was found at the silversmiths' and goldsmiths' shops, chatting and showing his enthusiasm for craftsmanship with the metalworkers and other artisans.

At other times he would mix with ordinary townsmen and converse with anyone he happened to meet, and he would even drink with visiting foreigners of the humblest sort.

If he learned that some young men were feasting together, he would appear among them without any warning, bringing horns and musicians with him in reveling fashion, so that most people, startled by the oddity of it, would get up and run away.

He would often lay aside his royal dress, put on a toga-like robe, and walk about the marketplace canvassing for office, shaking hands with some men and even embracing others as he asked for their vote, sometimes as though he were seeking the aedileship and sometimes the tribunate.

And when he had won the office, he would sit down on the ivory curule chair in the Roman manner, hear the business cases conducted in the marketplace, and decide them with great seriousness and eagerness. By this he left sensible people perplexed:

some thought him a simple, guileless man, while others thought him mad. His conduct in giving presents was similar. To some he gave knucklebones made from gazelle horn,

to others dates, and to others gold.

He would even encounter certain men by chance whom he had never seen before and give them unexpected gifts.

In the sacrifices offered by cities and in honors shown to the gods he surpassed all previous kings.

One may judge this from the Olympieion at Athens and from the statues around the altar at Delos.

He even bathed in the public baths when they were crowded with ordinary townsmen, with jars of the costliest perfumes brought in for him.

Once, when someone said, "How fortunate you kings are, to use such things and smell so sweet," he said nothing to the man; but on the next day, when that fellow was bathing, Antiochus came in and had a very large jar of the most expensive perfume, the kind called stacte, poured over his head,

with the result that all the bathers jumped up and rolled themselves in the perfume, then kept slipping and falling because it was so sticky, providing laughter for everyone, including the king himself.

# Book 26 Chapter 1a: Chapter 1a

Olympiad 151. Affairs of Antiochus.

Polybius, in the twenty-sixth book of the Histories, calls him Epimanes rather than Epiphanes because of his conduct. For he not only came down to associate with common townsmen, but even drank with visiting foreigners and with the very meanest people.

And if, he says, he became aware that some young men were feasting together anywhere, he would appear with horn-music and a band, so that most people, startled by the strangeness of it, would leap up and run away. He often even cast off his royal dress, put on a toga-like cloak, and walked about the marketplace.
