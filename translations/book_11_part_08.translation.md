# Book 11 Chapter 26: Scipio's Plan to Punish the Rebels

With this in mind they turned their attention to the matter of money.

When the tribunes had explained what had been decided, Publius asked the council what ought to be done next.

They resolved to announce a day on which the mutineers must appear, to grant terms to the mass of the men, but to punish the guilty leaders severely; these numbered thirty-five.

When the appointed day arrived and the rebels came in for the settlement and the payment of their arrears,

Publius secretly instructed the tribunes who had gone as envoys to meet the mutineers, and for each man to single out five of the ringleaders, greet them kindly as soon as they met, and invite them to their own quarters if possible, or at least to dinner or some similar gathering.

To the army with him he gave orders to prepare rations for a longer expedition within three days, as though they were to march with Marcus against Andobales.

This news made the mutineers more confident, for they supposed that power would lie chiefly in their own hands once they joined the general, since the other camps would then be separated from him.

# Book 11 Chapter 27: Suppression of the Mutiny

As the mutineers drew near the city, orders were given to the rest of the soldiers to march out at dawn on the next day with full equipment.

But the tribunes and prefects were instructed that, after this first movement, they should lay down the baggage, keep the soldiers in arms at the gate, divide them among all the gates, and take care that none of the mutineers got out.

Those detailed to meet them accordingly joined the newcomers and, following their instructions, courteously led away those who were responsible.

These officers had also been told that, once the men had eaten dinner, they were to seize the thirty-five, bind them, and keep them under guard, allowing no one from inside to go out except the messenger who was to report to the general from each group that the deed had been done.

When the tribunes had carried out their orders, the general at daybreak on the following day saw that the new arrivals were gathered in the marketplace and summoned the assembly.

As all ran together in the usual way at the signal, their minds were suspended in uncertainty, wondering what sort of appearance the general would present and what they would hear from him about the situation.

Publius then sent orders to the tribunes at the gates to bring the soldiers up under arms and surround the assembly, while he himself came forward and at his very first appearance threw them all into confusion.

For most of them still supposed him to be ill, and when, contrary to expectation, they suddenly saw him appearing strong and vigorous, they were struck with terror.

# Book 11 Chapter 28: Scipio Harangues the Mutinous Troops

He began his speech in something like these terms. He said he was amazed by what grievance had displeased them or what hopes had elated them so far as to attempt secession.

For there are three causes, he said, for which men dare to revolt against their country and their leaders: either they blame and resent those in authority, or they are dissatisfied with their present circumstances, or else, by Zeus, they reach after greater and fairer hopes.

"Now I ask which of these applies to you. Plainly, I suppose, you are angry with me because your rations and pay were not being given you.

But that was not a charge against me, for during my command none of your pay was lacking.

If the fault came from Rome, because old arrears were not now being settled—

was it right to bring this complaint by becoming deserters from your fatherland and enemies of the power that reared you, or to come to me in person, state the matter, and ask your friends to help and support you?

I think the latter was better. For men serving some master for wages may perhaps sometimes be excused for revolting against those who pay them, but men fighting for themselves and for their own wives and children cannot be excused at all.

It is much the same as if a man, claiming that his own father had wronged him in money matters, should come with arms to kill the one from whom he received life itself.

Or perhaps I laid upon you more hardships and dangers than on the others, while distributing the advantages and rewards more generously to others?

But you do not dare to say that, and even if you dared, you would not be able to prove it.

What then is the grievance for which you are dissatisfied with us and have now made this secession? That is what I want to learn, though I think none of you will either speak it or even conceive it."

# Book 11 Chapter 29: Scipio's Speech Continued

"No, not one of you. Nor can it be that you are troubled by the state of affairs. When has fortune flowed more favorably? When has Rome won greater advantages? When have those serving in the field had larger hopes than now?

But perhaps one of the faint-hearted will say that among our enemies the benefits appeared greater and their hopes larger and more secure. Among which enemies, then?

Among Andobales and Mandonius? And which of you does not know that these men first broke faith with the Carthaginians and revolted to us, and now, after once again violating their oaths and pledges, have made themselves our enemies?

A fine thing indeed—to become enemies of your own fatherland by trusting such men as these.

Nor did you place your hopes in yourselves, as though you would become masters of Iberia. For even if you had stood in line with Andobales, you were not strong enough to risk battle against us, much less if you had stood alone.

What then did you rely on? Was it perhaps the experience and courage of the leaders you have now chosen, or even the rods and axes carried before them? About these men it is not honorable even to say more.

No, my men, none of these things can serve you, nor can you state the slightest just complaint either against us or against your country.

Therefore I will make your defense before Rome and before myself on the basis of what all mankind agrees is just.

And that is this: every crowd is easily misled and easily led into anything. That is why the multitude and the sea are always liable to the same condition.

For just as the sea in its own nature is harmless to those who use it and remains at rest, but when violent winds fall upon it it seems to sailors to take on the character of the winds that whip it round,

so too the crowd always appears and becomes, for those dealing with it, whatever sort of leaders and advisers it happens to have.

Therefore I and all the officers of the camp now make peace with you and give our pledge that we will bear no grudge.

But toward those who were responsible we remain utterly unreconciled and hold that they must be punished as their offenses against their country and against us deserve."
