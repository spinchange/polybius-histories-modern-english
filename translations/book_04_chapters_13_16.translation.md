...in this way it turned out. When the Megalopolitans learned that the Aetolians were encamped near Methydrium, they came at the call of the trumpet, with their whole population, to give aid on the day immediately following the battle.

And the very men alongside whom, while still alive, they had hoped to face the danger against the enemy—these they were now forced to bury, men who had been killed by their foes.

So, having dug a trench in the plain of Caphyae and gathered up the dead, they performed the funeral rites for those unfortunate men with every mark of honor.

The Aetolians, meanwhile, having unexpectedly won their advantage by means of their cavalry and light-armed troops alone, from then on marched in complete safety straight through the middle of the Peloponnese.

During this time they made an attempt on the city of the Pellenians, overran the territory of Sicyon, and finally made their withdrawal by way of the Isthmus.

These, then, were the cause and the pretext from which the Social War arose; its formal beginning came from the decree that was afterward passed by all the allies.

-lip the king presiding. A few days later the general body of the Achaeans, gathered for their regular assembly, were bitterly hostile—both collectively and individually—toward Aratus, on the grounds that he was, by common agreement, the man responsible for the disaster just described.

And so, when his political opponents brought charges against him and presented vivid demonstrations of his guilt, the multitude grew still more angry and incensed.

For it seemed that his first plain error was this: that, before his term of office had even legally begun, he had seized upon a time that belonged to another in order to take up the very sort of enterprises in which he knew he had often failed.

His second error, and greater than this, was that he had disbanded the Achaeans while the Aetolians were still in the heart of the Peloponnese—and this especially when he had known in advance that Scopas and Dorimachus were eager to upset the established order and stir up war.

His third error was to engage the enemy as he did, with so few men and under no pressing necessity, when he could have withdrawn safely to the neighboring cities, mustered the Achaeans, and only then given battle to the enemy, if he judged that course to be wholly advantageous.

His last and greatest error was that, having decided to fight, he conducted matters so recklessly and so heedlessly that he abandoned the plains and the usefulness of his heavy infantry, and made the contest against the Aetolians on the hillsides with his light-armed troops alone—the very ground than which nothing could have been more useful or more suited to the Aetolians.

Nevertheless, as soon as Aratus came forward and reminded them of his earlier political career and of what he had accomplished before, and offered his defense on the charges—arguing that he was not responsible for what had happened, and asking their pardon if he had indeed overlooked something during the engagement, and urging that they should consider the whole matter not with bitterness,

but with humanity—the multitude so quickly and so magnanimously changed their minds that they became thoroughly displeased with those of his political opponents who had joined in attacking him, and resolved all subsequent measures in accordance with Aratus' judgment. These events, then, fell in the earlier Olympiad,

# Book 4 Chapter 15: Lukewarmness of the Allies

The Achaeans' resolutions were these: to send envoys to the Epirotes, the Boeotians, the Phocians, the Acarnanians,

and to Philip, and to make clear in what manner the Aetolians, in violation of the treaties, had already twice invaded Achaia under arms; to call upon them for aid in accordance with the agreements; and also to receive the Messenians into the alliance;

and that the general of the Achaeans should pick out five thousand foot and five hundred horse from among the Achaeans and go to the support of the Messenians, should the Aetolians set foot in their territory;

and to arrange with both the Lacedaemonians and the Messenians how many horse and foot were to be furnished by each for the common needs. When these measures had been resolved upon,

the Achaeans, bearing what had happened nobly, abandoned neither the Messenians nor their own purpose; and those appointed as envoys to the allies carried out their missions,

while the general picked out the men from Achaia in accordance with the decree, and arranged with the Lacedaemonians and the Messenians that each should furnish two thousand five hundred foot and two hundred and fifty horse,

so that the whole combined force for the needs that would arise should be ten thousand foot and a thousand horse.

The Aetolians, meanwhile,

when their regular assembly had come around, met and resolved to keep peace with the Lacedaemonians, the Messenians, and all the rest—acting in bad faith and wishing to ruin and injure the allies of the Achaeans;

but with the Achaeans themselves they voted to keep peace on condition that they withdraw from the alliance with the Messenians, and otherwise to make war—a thing utterly absurd.

For being themselves allies of both the Achaeans and the Messenians, if these two should maintain friendship and alliance with each other, they declared war on the Achaeans; whereas if the Achaeans chose enmity toward the Messenians, they would grant them peace separately—

so that their injustice could not even be brought within the bounds of reason, because of the perversity of their very schemes.

# Book 4 Chapter 16: The Aetolians and Illyrians Invade Achaia

The Epirotes and King Philip, on hearing the envoys, admitted the Messenians into the alliance;

and as for what the Aetolians had done, though they were indignant for the moment, they were not greatly astonished, since the Aetolians had done nothing extraordinary but only something in keeping with their usual ways.

For this very reason they were not angered for long, but voted to keep peace with them—so true is it that continual wrongdoing meets with more forgiveness than rare and unexpected wickedness.

The Aetolians, at any rate, behaving in this manner—constantly plundering Greece and waging undeclared wars upon many peoples—no longer even thought it worth their while to answer those who accused them, but actually mocked anyone who summoned them to arbitration over what had happened, or, by Zeus, over what they intended to do.

And the Lacedaemonians, though they had recently been set free through Antigonus and the generous zeal of the Achaeans, and were bound to do nothing hostile toward the Macedonians and Philip, sent secret messages to the Aetolians and concluded a covert treaty of friendship and alliance with them.

When the Achaean young men had already been picked out and assigned to the support of the Lacedaemonians and Messenians, Scerdilaidas, together with Demetrius of Pharos, sailed out from Illyria with ninety galleys beyond Lissus, in violation of the treaty with the Romans.

These men first put in at Pylos and, after making assaults upon it, were beaten off;

afterward Demetrius, taking fifty of the galleys, set out for the islands, and sailing around them exacted money from some of the Cyclades and plundered others;

while Scerdilaidas, making his voyage homeward, put in at Naupactus with forty galleys, persuaded by Amynas, king of the Athamanians, who happened to be his brother-in-law.

Having made an agreement with the Aetolians, through Agelaus, concerning the division of the spoils, he promised to join the Aetolians in invading Achaia together with them.
