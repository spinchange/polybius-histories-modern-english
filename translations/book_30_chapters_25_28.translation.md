# Book 30 Chapter 25: Chapter 25

This same king, when he heard about the games that Aemilius Paulus, the Roman general, had held in Macedonia, wanted to surpass Paulus in magnificence. So he sent envoys and sacred messengers to the cities to announce the games he would hold at Daphne, and this stirred great eagerness among the Greeks to come to him.

He began the festival with a procession arranged in the following way.

At the head marched five thousand men in Roman equipment, wearing chain-mail cuirasses, all in the prime of life. After them came five thousand Mysians.

Close behind them were three thousand Cilicians armed in the style of light infantry, wearing golden crowns.

After these came three thousand Thracians and five thousand Gauls. Next marched twenty thousand Macedonians, five thousand men with bronze shields, and another corps with silver shields; after them came two hundred and forty pairs of gladiators.

Behind these were the cavalry: one thousand Nisaean horsemen and three thousand of the regular cavalry. Most of them had harness plated with gold and wore golden crowns, while the rest had silver-plated harness.

After them came the cavalry known as the Companions, about one thousand strong, all with gold-plated harness. Next to them was the regiment of the Friends,

equal to them both in numbers and in splendor. After these came one thousand picked horsemen, followed by the corps called the Agema, thought to be the finest body of cavalry,

about one thousand strong. Last came the cataphract cavalry, aptly named because both horses and riders were covered with armor. They too numbered one thousand five hundred.

All those mentioned above had purple caparisons, and many had trappings embroidered with gold and figured designs.

After these came one hundred six-horse chariots and forty four-horse chariots, then a chariot drawn by elephants and one drawn by a pair of horses. Behind them came thirty-six elephants, fully caparisoned, one after another.

The rest of the procession is hard to describe, so it must be given only in summary. About eight hundred young men marched in it wearing golden crowns, there were about one thousand well-fed oxen, the sacred delegations numbered just under three hundred, and there were eight hundred elephant tusks.

The number of statues cannot be fully described. Images were carried of all the gods and divine beings spoken of or worshiped among mankind, and in addition of heroes as well, some gilded and others dressed in robes embroidered with gold.

Alongside all these were displayed the stories proper to each of them, presented according to the traditional accounts in lavish tableaux.

There also followed images of Night and Day, of Earth and Sky, and of Dawn and Midday.

One may judge the quantity of gold and silver plate from this: for one of the king's friends, Dionysius the secretary, had one thousand boys in the procession carrying silver vessels, and none of them weighed less than one thousand drachmas.

Six hundred royal pages also passed by carrying gold vessels. Then two hundred women sprinkled perfumes from golden jars.

After them came eighty women carried in litters with golden feet, and five hundred seated in litters with silver feet, all magnificently adorned. These were the most striking features of the procession.

# Book 30 Chapter 26: Chapter 26

When the games, gladiatorial combats, and hunts had been completed over a period of thirty days, during which he presented the spectacles, for the first five days everyone in the gymnasium was anointed from golden vessels with saffron ointment.

There were fifteen of these vessels, and an equal number for cinnamon and nard perfume. In much the same way, on the following days fenugreek, marjoram, and iris perfumes were brought in, all remarkable for their fragrance.

For the banquets, sometimes one thousand dining couches were laid out and sometimes one thousand five hundred, furnished with the utmost luxury.

# Book 30 Chapter 27: Chapter 27

After the games had been brought to an end, just when they were over, the envoys headed by Tiberius arrived, acting in effect as spies.

Antiochus received them with such tact and friendliness that not only did Tiberius and his colleagues suspect nothing serious or obstructive in his conduct regarding the affairs of Alexandria, but they even condemned those who said anything of the kind, because his welcome was so extraordinarily gracious.

Indeed, besides all his other courtesies, he even yielded the palace itself to the envoys, and in outward appearance almost his diadem as well, though this was not his real disposition,

but rather the exact opposite, since he was very unfriendly toward the Romans.
