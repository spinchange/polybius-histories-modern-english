# Book 31 Chapter 25: The Rhodians Lapse in Dignity

From this time onward, by continually proving themselves to one another in practical matters, they came to feel toward each other the affection of father and son, and of close kinsmen.

Scipio's first impulse and zeal for noble conduct was to recover a reputation for self-control and in this respect to surpass the rest of those of his own age.

And though this prize was great and hard to win, at that time in Rome it was easy to seize, because most men were rushing in the opposite direction.

Some of the young men were giving themselves over to boys, others to courtesans, and many to entertainments, drinking parties, and the extravagance connected with them, having quickly adopted during the war with Perseus the Greek looseness in such things.

So great a lack of self-mastery had fallen upon the youth in matters of this kind that many had bought a favorite for a talent, and many a jar of Pontic salt fish for three hundred drachmas.

Indignant at this, Marcus once said to the people that they could see most clearly from such things how far the constitution had declined, when handsome boys fetched a higher price than farms, and jars of preserved fish than teamsters.

This ambition first came into prominence at the time we are speaking of, first because, now that the Macedonian monarchy had been overthrown, the Romans thought their supremacy over the whole world beyond dispute,

and then because a great display of prosperity had arisen both in private life and in public affairs, from the wealth brought from Macedonia to Rome.

But Scipio, setting himself toward the opposite manner of life, resisting every desire, and making his conduct throughout consistent and harmonious, within perhaps his first five years made his reputation for discipline and self-control known to everyone.

After this he continually aimed to surpass the rest in greatness of spirit and integrity in money matters.

For this side of his character he had a fine foundation in his life with his natural father; he himself also had by nature good impulses toward what was right; and much besides came of itself to help forward this purpose.

# Book 31 Chapter 26: The Two Ptolemies

The first event was the death of the mother of his adoptive father, who was the sister of his natural father Lucius and the wife of his adoptive grandfather Scipio, the one called the Great.

When she left a large estate, and he became her heir, he was first to give proof in this matter of the principles on which he had chosen to live.

This woman, whose name was Aemilia, had maintained a magnificent establishment for women's public appearances, as was natural for one who had shared the life and fortune of Scipio.

For apart from the adornment of her own person and carriage, the baskets, cups, and other objects used in sacrifice, sometimes of silver and sometimes of gold, all accompanied her on her more splendid outings.

And along with these went the large number of maidservants and household attendants who followed in her train.

This entire establishment, immediately after Aemilia's funeral, Scipio gave to his mother, who had long since been separated from Lucius and whose means of support were less ample than her noble birth would suggest.

So, whereas before that time she had kept away from distinguished public appearances, when by chance there came a splendid and well-attended sacrifice, she went out with Aemilia's full equipment and provision, and with the same muleteers, team, and carriage besides.

As a result, the women who witnessed it were astonished at Scipio's kindness and generosity, and all stretched out their hands in prayer, calling down many blessings on him.

This would naturally seem admirable anywhere, but at Rome it seemed positively astonishing, since no one there simply gives any part of his private property to anyone else of his own free will.

This, then, was the first beginning of his reputation for nobility of character, and it advanced him greatly, since the female sex is both talkative and lavish in spreading whatever captures its interest.

# Book 31 Chapter 27: Ptolemy Physcon Invades Cyrene

After this, he was obliged to pay the remaining half of the dowries owed to the daughters of Scipio the Great, who were the sisters of his adoptive father.

For his father had agreed to give each of the two daughters fifty talents,

and of this amount their mother had immediately paid one half to their husbands, while at her death she left the other half still owing.

So Scipio had to discharge this debt to his father's sisters.

Now according to Roman law the money still owing as dowry had to be paid to the women in three yearly installments, after the furniture had first been sold within ten months, according to their custom.

But Scipio at once instructed his banker to pay each woman her twenty-five talents within those ten months.

When Tiberius and Scipio Nasica — for these were the husbands of the women just mentioned — came to the banker as soon as the ten months had expired and asked whether Scipio had given him any instructions about the money, and he told them to collect it and wrote out drafts to each of them for twenty-five talents, they said he must be mistaken.

For, they said, according to the law they should receive not the whole amount now, but only the third part at present.

But when he insisted that Scipio had given exactly those instructions, they believed him and went on to the young man, supposing that he did not understand the matter. Nor was this unreasonable.

For no one in Rome would pay fifty talents before the three years were up — indeed, not even a single talent before the appointed day.

So universal and so great among them all is their exactness in matters of legal claim, and at the same time their regard for the advantage of delay.

Nevertheless, when they approached him and asked why he had instructed the banker in this way, and he replied that the whole sum was to be paid to his sisters, they said that he did not understand, while at the same time showing a guardian's concern.

For under the laws, they said, he was still entitled for a considerable time to make use of the sums at issue.

But Scipio said that he was perfectly well aware of all this, yet that toward outsiders he observed the strictness of the law, whereas toward relatives and friends he acted simply and generously, as far as he could.

Therefore he told them to take the whole amount from the banker.

When Tiberius and the others heard this, they went away in silence, overwhelmed by Scipio's greatness of spirit and condemning their own pettiness, though they were second to none among the Romans.

# Book 31 Chapter 28: Gnaeus Merula Comes to Rome

Two years later his natural father Lucius died, leaving him and his brother Fabius as heirs to the estate; on this occasion Scipio did something noble and worthy of remembrance.

For Lucius, being left without children in his own house — since some had been given in adoption into other families, and the rest of the sons whom he had been rearing as successors to the family had all died — left his estate to these two.

Scipio, seeing that his brother was less well supplied with means, gave up his own share of all the property, whose total value was more than sixty talents, so that in this way Fabius might possess wealth equal to his own.

When this became widely talked about, he added to it another and even clearer proof of the principles by which he lived.

His brother wanted to give gladiatorial games in honor of their father, but was unable to bear the expense because of the amount of money required, and Scipio contributed half the cost from his own fortune.

And the total expense is not less than thirty talents, if a man puts on the show magnificently.

He was so far from reclaiming anything that he had previously given her, about which I have just spoken, that he handed over both those gifts and all the rest of his mother's property to his sisters, although by law none of it belonged to them.

So when the sisters once again took over Aemilia's display and retinue for their public appearances, the generosity and family affection of Scipio's character were once more brought freshly into notice.

Thus, prepared from early youth in this way, Publius Scipio advanced toward winning distinction for self-control and nobility of character.

By spending perhaps sixty talents — for he had given up that much of his private means — he obtained an acknowledged reputation for generosity, accomplishing his purpose not so much by the amount of money as by the timing of the gift and the gracious way in which he bestowed the favor.

His self-control, however, he acquired at no cost at all; by abstaining from many and various pleasures he gained bodily health and vigor,

and this stayed with him throughout his whole life, repaying him with many pleasures and fine rewards in return for the easy pleasures from which he had earlier abstained.
