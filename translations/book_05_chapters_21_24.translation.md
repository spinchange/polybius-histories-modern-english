# Book 5 Chapter 21: Lycurgus Goes Out to Resist

After giving instructions to the commanders and his friends about the coming danger, Lycurgus himself marched out of the city and occupied the ground around the Menelaeum, with no fewer than two thousand men in all.

He arranged with those in the city to keep watch, so that whenever he raised the signal they would quickly lead the troops out in front of the city from several points at once and draw them up facing the Eurotas, where the river comes nearest to the city.

Such were the measures of Lycurgus and the Lacedaemonians.

# Book 5 Chapter 22: Position of Sparta and Disposition of Troops

Sparta as a whole is circular in shape and lies on level ground, though in its several parts it includes uneven and hilly places of various kinds.

The river that runs past its eastern side is called the Eurotas, and for most of the year it is uncrossable because of its size.

So it happens that the hills on which the Menelaeum stands lie beyond the river, to the southeast of the city; they are rugged, hard to climb, exceptionally high, and they command the strip between the river and the city.

Through this strip the river just mentioned flows along the very foot of the hill, and the whole width there is no more than a stade and a half.

Through this Philip was necessarily obliged to make his return march, with the city and the Lacedaemonians, ready and drawn up for battle, on his left, and on his right the river and Lycurgus's men standing on the hills.

The Lacedaemonians had also devised the following in addition to these advantages: by damming the river upstream they diverted it into the ground between the city and the hills, and once that area had been soaked through, it was impossible for not only horses but even infantry to enter it.

So the only course left was to lead the army close under the foothills, thus exposing themselves to the enemy in a long marching column in which they could scarcely support one another.

Seeing this, Philip took counsel with his friends and decided that the most urgent necessity of the moment was first to drive Lycurgus and his men from the positions around the Menelaeum.

So he took the mercenaries and the peltasts, and with them the Illyrians as well, crossed the river, and advanced toward the hills.

Lycurgus, perceiving Philip's intention, prepared the troops with him and encouraged them for the coming danger, and he also gave the signal to those in the city.

When this was done, the magistrates responsible immediately led out the citizen troops according to the arrangement and drew them up in front of the wall, placing the cavalry on the right wing.

# Book 5 Chapter 23: Defeat of Lycurgus Near Sparta

When Philip came near Lycurgus's men, he first sent the mercenaries against them.

As a result, the Lacedaemonians had the advantage at the outset and fought with greater confidence, since both their equipment and the ground contributed greatly in their favor.

But when Philip brought up the peltasts to support those engaged, keeping them in reserve formation, and launched the Illyrians in a flank attack,

the mercenaries under Philip, encouraged by the support of the Illyrians and peltasts, were much more strongly emboldened for the fight, while Lycurgus's men, alarmed by the attack of the heavy-armed troops, gave way and fled.

About a hundred of them fell, and a little more than that number were taken prisoner; the rest escaped into the city. Lycurgus himself struck off by trackless routes and during the night made his way into the city with a few companions.

Philip occupied the hills with the Illyrians, and then, taking the light-armed troops and peltasts with him, returned to the main force.

At this time Aratus, leading the phalanx from Amyclae, had already come near the city.

So the king, after crossing the river, remained in support with the light-armed troops and peltasts, and also with the cavalry, until the heavy infantry had safely passed beneath the hills through the difficult ground.

When the troops from the city tried to attack the cavalry serving as the covering force, and the engagement became more general, the peltasts fought with courage.

And about this time Philip gained an acknowledged advantage, and after joining in the pursuit and chasing the Lacedaemonian cavalry to the gates, he then crossed the Eurotas safely and brought up the rear of his phalanx.

# Book 5 Chapter 24: Philip Proceeds to Tegea

As the day was already drawing on, he was forced to encamp where he was, so he made camp near the exit from the pass,

his officers having by chance chosen such a place as no one could have found a better for making an invasion into Laconia right by the city itself.

For at the beginning of the pass just mentioned, when one approaches Sparta from Tegea or generally from the inland country, there is a place at most two stades from the city and situated directly on the river.

Its side facing the city and the river is entirely enclosed by a great sheer cliff that is completely inaccessible; but the ground above these precipices is level, fertile, and well watered, and at the same time conveniently situated for the entrance and exit of forces.

So anyone encamped there and holding the hill above it would seem to be camping in safety because of the position relative to the city, and also in an excellent place, since he controlled the entrance and passage through the narrow defile.

Philip accordingly encamped there in safety, and on the next day sent the baggage on ahead, but drew up his army on the level ground where it was plainly visible to those in the city.

He remained there for a short time, and after that wheeled his line toward one flank and marched in the direction of Tegea.

When he reached the place where Antigonus and Cleomenes had joined battle, he encamped there.

On the following day, after inspecting the ground and sacrificing to the gods on each of the two hills, one called Olympus and the other Euas, he advanced, having strengthened his rearguard.

When he arrived at Tegea, he sold off all the booty, and after that marched through Argos and came with the army to Corinth.

While ambassadors from Rhodes and Chios were present there to discuss ending the war, he gave them audience and, pretending agreement, said that he was ready now as before to make peace with the Aetolians; then he sent them away, instructing them also to speak with the Aetolians about a settlement,

while he himself went down to Lechaeum and made preparations for a voyage, since he had some more substantial operations to carry out in the region of Phocis.
