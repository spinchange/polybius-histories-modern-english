# Book 3 Chapter 15: Hannibal Defies the Romans

The Saguntines kept sending embassies to Rome, partly because they were anxious for themselves and could see what was coming, and partly because they wanted the Romans to be aware of the steady success the Carthaginians were having in their Iberian affairs.

For a long while the Romans paid no attention to them, but at this point they dispatched envoys to investigate the situation.

At about the same time Hannibal, having subdued the peoples he had set out to subdue, came back with his forces to winter at New Carthage, which was in a sense the showpiece and royal seat of the Carthaginians in the regions of Iberia.

There he found the embassy from Rome, granted it an audience, and heard out what it had to say about the present circumstances.

The Romans, for their part, solemnly warned him to keep his hands off the Saguntines—since they were under Roman protection—and not to cross the river Ebro, in keeping with the agreement made in Hasdrubal's time.

But Hannibal—being young, full of warlike energy, successful in his ventures, and long since driven by his hatred of Rome—

answered the envoys by posing as the protector of the Saguntines. He charged the Romans with having, a short time before, when civil strife broke out in the city, taken on the role of arbitrators and unjustly put some of the leading men to death; he said the Carthaginians would not stand by while the Saguntines were treated treacherously like this, for it was Carthage's ancestral policy to overlook no one who was being wronged.

At the same time he sent word to Carthage asking what he should do, on the grounds that the Saguntines, trusting in their alliance with Rome, were committing injustices against some of the peoples subject to Carthage.

In short, he was thoroughly governed by unreason and violent passion. For this reason he did not make use of the genuine grounds for complaint, but took refuge in irrational pretexts—just as men typically do when, because of the impulses already lodged in them, they lose all regard for what is right.

How much better it would have been for him to take the view that the Romans ought to give back Sardinia to the Carthaginians, along with the tribute they had imposed at the same time—both of which the Romans had unjustly seized earlier when they took advantage of Carthage's difficulties—and that, failing that, he would go to war.

Instead, by saying nothing of the real, existing grievance and fabricating a non-existent one concerning the Saguntines, he gave the impression of starting the war not only without reason but, still worse, unjustly.

As for the Roman envoys, since they now clearly understood that war was inevitable, they sailed off to Carthage, intending to lodge the same protest there as well.

They expected, however, that they would be fighting the war not in Italy but in Iberia, and that they would use the city of Saguntum as their base of operations.

# Book 3 Chapter 16: War in Illyria

For this reason, the Senate, in adapting its plans to this situation, decided to secure its interests in Illyria, foreseeing that the war would be a major and protracted one, fought far from home.

For it so happened that at this time Demetrius of Pharos had forgotten the benefits he had previously received from the Romans, and—having come to despise them, first because of the threat surrounding them from the Gauls and then because of the threat from the Carthaginians—

placing all his hopes in the royal house of Macedonia (because he had fought alongside Antigonus and shared in the campaigns against Cleomenes), was plundering and subjugating the cities of Illyria that were subject to Rome, and had sailed beyond Lissus with fifty galleys, in violation of the treaty, and had ravaged many of the Cyclades islands.

In view of this, and seeing that the royal house of Macedonia was flourishing, the Romans were eager to secure their interests east of Italy, convinced that they had ample time to set right the folly of the Illyrians and to censure and punish the ingratitude and recklessness of Demetrius.

But they were mistaken in their calculations, for Hannibal forestalled them by taking the city of Saguntum.

And as a result it came about that the war was fought not in Iberia but right at Rome's doorstep and throughout the whole of Italy.

Nonetheless, with these considerations in mind, the Romans, just before the summer season, sent out Lucius Aemilius with an army to conduct operations in Illyria, in the first year of the one hundred and fortieth Olympiad.

# Book 3 Chapter 17: Hannibal Takes Saguntum

Hannibal, breaking camp with his army, advanced from New Carthage and marched on Saguntum.

This city lies on a spur of the mountain range that runs down toward the sea, where the borders of Iberia and Celtiberia meet, and it stands about seven stades from the sea.

Its inhabitants farm a territory that produces every kind of crop and is superior in quality to any in all of Iberia.

It was here that Hannibal pitched camp and set about the siege in earnest, foreseeing many advantages that would come from taking the city by storm.

In the first place, he reckoned he would deprive the Romans of their hope of basing the war in Iberia. Second, he was convinced that by terrifying everyone he would make the peoples already under his command more obedient, and the still-independent Iberians more cautious.

But the most important thing was that, leaving no enemy behind him, he could safely make his advance forward.

Apart from these considerations, he reckoned that he himself would gain plentiful supplies for his enterprises, that he would inspire eagerness in his forces through the profit each man stood to gain, and that he would win the goodwill of the Carthaginians at home through the spoils that would be sent to them.

With such calculations in mind, he pressed the siege vigorously, at times making himself an example to the troops by personally taking part in the labor of the works, and at other times encouraging the men and recklessly exposing himself to danger.

After enduring every kind of hardship and care, he finally took the city by storm in eight months.

Having become master of a great quantity of money, captives, and goods, he set aside the money for his own undertakings, in keeping with his original plan; the captives he distributed among his fellow soldiers according to each man's merit; and the goods he sent off at once, in their entirety, to the Carthaginians.

In doing this he was not disappointed in his calculations, nor did he fail of his original purpose. Rather, he made his soldiers keener to face danger, kept the Carthaginians ready to do whatever he asked, and afterward, thanks to the supplies he had laid in, accomplished many useful things himself.

# Book 3 Chapter 18: Demetrius Fortifies Dimale

At about this same time, as soon as Demetrius learned of the Romans' intentions, he immediately threw a strong garrison into Dimale, with appropriate supplies, while in the other cities he put to death those who opposed his policies and placed power in the hands of his own friends.

He then picked out six thousand of the most able-bodied men from his subjects and stationed them in Pharos.

When the Roman commander arrived in Illyria with his forces and saw that the enemy were confident in the strength of Dimale and in their preparations—and moreover believed the place to be impregnable—he decided to attack it first, wishing to strike terror into the enemy.

After addressing the various officers in turn and bringing up siege works at several points, he began the siege.

He took the place by storm in seven days, and at once crushed the spirit of all his opponents.

As a result, envoys immediately came from all the cities, surrendering themselves and placing themselves under the protection of Rome.

He accepted each on suitable terms and then sailed to Pharos against Demetrius himself.

Learning that the city was strongly fortified, that a large body of exceptional troops had been gathered into it, and that it was furthermore well stocked with supplies and other equipment, he began to suspect that the siege might prove difficult and drawn out.

So, foreseeing all this, he resorted on the spot to the following sort of stratagem.

Making his approach to the island by night with his whole army, he landed the greater part of his force at some wooded, low-lying places,

while at daybreak he sailed openly with twenty ships toward the harbor nearest the city.

When Demetrius and his men caught sight of the ships and, despising their small number, rushed out of the city to the harbor to prevent the landing.

# Book 3 Chapter 19: Illyrian War Successfully Finished

[The Illyrians rushed out] to prevent the enemy's landing. As soon as battle was joined and the fighting grew fierce, more and more men kept coming out of the city in support, until at last the whole force had poured out into the engagement.

At that point the Romans who had disembarked during the night joined the battle, having made their way through unobserved ground.

Seizing a strong hill between the city and the harbor, they cut off from the city those who had sallied out.

When Demetrius and his men realized what had happened, they gave up trying to stop those landing; rallying themselves and exhorting one another, they set off, having decided to risk a pitched battle against the troops on the hill.

The Romans, seeing the Illyrians' attack coming on vigorously and in good order, charged their companies into them with terrifying force.

At the same time, the men who had made the landing from the ships, seeing what was happening, pressed in from behind; and attacking from every side, they created no small confusion and disorder among the Illyrians.

So, with some of them in difficulty to their front and others to their rear, in the end Demetrius and his men were routed. Some of them fled toward the city, but most scattered across the island by trackless ways.

Demetrius, who kept galleys ready for whatever might happen, lying at anchor at certain deserted spots, made his escape to these. Boarding them when night came on, he sailed away and, against all expectation, made his way to King Philip, with whom he spent the rest of his life—

a man who possessed daring and boldness, but daring of an irrational and utterly undiscerning kind.

And so it came about that his death matched the character of his whole life.

For when, with Philip's approval, he undertook to seize the city of the Messenians, he was killed in a rash and reckless manner at the very moment of the attempt—matters about which I will give the details when I come to that period.

The Roman commander Aemilius, after taking Pharos at once by assault and razing it, gained control of the rest of Illyria, settled everything according to his own plans, and then, as the summer was now drawing to a close, returned to Rome, where he celebrated his entry with a triumph and amid universal acclaim.

For he was thought to have handled the affair not only skillfully but, still more, with manly courage.

# Book 3 Chapter 20: Rome's Reaction to the Fall of Saguntum

As for the Romans, when the news of the capture of Saguntum had already reached them, they certainly did not, by Zeus, then hold a debate about whether to go to war—as some historians claim, even setting down the speeches supposedly delivered on both sides—which is the most absurd thing imaginable.

For how could it be that the Romans, who a year earlier had declared war on the Carthaginians should they so much as set foot on Saguntine territory, would now, after the city itself had been taken by storm, gather to deliberate whether or not to go to war?

And how, and in what manner, can these writers, while introducing the astonishing detail of the gloom of the Senate, at the same time claim that the fathers brought their sons of twelve and older into the Senate House, and that these boys, though present at the deliberations, did not divulge any of the secrets even to their closest relatives?

None of this is either plausible or true in the slightest—unless, by Zeus, on top of everything else, Fortune granted the Romans this gift too: to have good sense the moment they were born.

About such writings as those of Chaereas and Sosilus there is no need to say anything more, for in my view they have the rank and value not of history but of barber-shop and street-corner gossip.

When the disaster that had befallen the Saguntines was reported to them, the Romans at once chose envoys and sent them in haste to Carthage,

presenting them with two options, one of which seemed, if the Carthaginians accepted it, to bring both disgrace and harm, while the other meant the beginning of great troubles and dangers.

For they either ordered that the general Hannibal and the councilors with him be handed over to the Romans, or they declared war.

When the Romans arrived, came before the Senate, and made these demands clear, the Carthaginians found the choice being put to them hard to bear.

Nonetheless, they put forward the ablest man among them and began to plead their case.

# Book 3 Chapter 21: Romans Demand Surrender of Hannibal

[The Carthaginians began] to argue their case. As for the agreement made with Hasdrubal, they passed over it in silence, on the grounds that it had never been made, and that even if it had, it was not binding on them, since it had been concluded without their authorization.

On this point they used a precedent drawn from the Romans themselves: they said that the treaty made by Lutatius during the war over Sicily, after it had already been agreed to by Lutatius, was later annulled by the Roman people on the grounds that it had been made without their authorization.

Throughout their argument they pressed and leaned hard on the final treaty, the one concluded in the war over Sicily.

In it, they said, there was no clause in writing concerning Iberia, but it was expressly laid down that the allies of each party should enjoy security from both.

They pointed out that the Saguntines were not at that time allies of Rome, and to prove this they read out the treaty aloud several times.

The Romans, however, refused outright to engage in legal argument, declaring that while the city of the Saguntines remained intact, the matter admitted of legal pleading and it was possible to settle the disputed points by discussion;

but now that the city had been treacherously destroyed, either the guilty parties had to be surrendered to them—whereby it would be clear to all that the Carthaginians had had no share in the wrongdoing, but that the deed had been done without their authorization—

or, if they were unwilling to do this and admitted that they shared in the wrongdoing, they had to take on the war as well. So the two sides argued in these rather general terms.

But it seems necessary to me not to leave this matter unexamined, so that neither those whose duty and concern it is to know the precise truth of these things may stray from the truth in deliberations of the utmost importance,

nor those who study these matters out of love of learning may go astray, misled along with the errors and partisan ambitions of the historians; but rather that there may be some agreed-upon survey of the rights that existed between the Romans and the Carthaginians toward one another, from the beginning down to our own times.
