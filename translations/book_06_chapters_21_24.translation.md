# Book 6 Chapter 21: Fourfold Division of the Legionaries

Once the enrollment has been completed in the manner described above, the tribunes responsible in each legion assemble the men who have been selected, and from the whole body they pick out the single most suitable man.

They then make him swear that he will obey his commanders and carry out their orders to the best of his ability.

All the rest then come forward one by one and take the oath, simply declaring that they will do everything just as the first man did.

At the same time, the holders of the consular office send instructions to the magistrates of the allied cities of Italy from which they wish allied troops to serve alongside them, specifying the number required, the day, and the place at which the selected men must present themselves.

The cities then carry out a selection and administer an oath very much like the one described, appoint a commander and a paymaster, and send the men out.

Meanwhile the tribunes at Rome, after administering the oath, assign each legion a day and a place at which the men must present themselves without their arms, and for the time being they dismiss them.

When the men arrive on the appointed day, the tribunes sort them out: the youngest and poorest they assign to the skirmishers (velites), those next in age to the so-called hastati, those in the prime of life to the principes, and the oldest to the triarii.

For these are the divisions among the Romans, and this is their number, distinguished by name, by age, and also by their equipment, in each legion.

They divide them in such a way that the oldest, called triarii, number six hundred, the principes twelve hundred, the hastati an equal number to these, and the remainder, the youngest, form the skirmishers.

If the legion numbers more than four thousand, they make the division proportionally, except for the triarii, whose number always stays the same.

# Book 6 Chapter 22: Arms of the Youngest Roman Soldiers

The youngest soldiers are ordered to carry a sword, javelins, and a round shield (parma).

The parma is sturdily constructed and large enough to give protection: being circular in shape, it has a diameter of three feet.

The soldier is also equipped with a plain helmet, and at times he puts a wolf's skin or something of the kind over it, both for protection and as a mark of recognition, so that the officers of each unit can see whether he is fighting bravely in the front of danger or not.

The shaft of the javelin is about two cubits in length and roughly a finger thick, while its head is a span long and beaten out so fine and sharpened to such a degree that it inevitably bends on the first impact, so that the enemy cannot throw it back; otherwise the weapon would be of use to both sides alike.

# Book 6 Chapter 23: Arms of the Other Soldiers

To the second group by age, those called the hastati, they gave orders to carry the full panoply.

The Roman panoply consists, first, of a long shield (scutum)—its curved surface two and a half feet wide, four feet long, and the thickness at the rim a palm's breadth.

It is made of two layers of board fastened together with bull's-hide glue, then covered on the outer surface first with canvas and after that with calf's skin.

Around the rim, both on the upper and the lower parts, it has an iron edging, by which it is protected against the downward strokes of swords and against wear from being rested on the ground.

An iron boss is also fitted to it, which wards off the heavier blows of stones, pikes, and violent missiles generally.

Along with the shield they carry a sword, which is worn on the right thigh and is called the Spanish sword (gladius hispaniensis).

It has an excellent point and can deliver a powerful cut with either edge, because its blade is strong and rigid.

In addition to these they carry two heavy javelins (pila), a bronze helmet, and a greave. Of the pila, some are thick and some thin.

Of the heavier ones, the round-shafted have a diameter of a palm's breadth, and the square-shafted measure a palm on each side. The thin ones resemble moderate-sized hunting spears, and they are carried together with the ones already mentioned.

The wooden shaft of all of them is about three cubits long, and to each is fitted a barbed iron head equal in length to the wood.

They make the attachment and fitting of the head so secure that they sink it into the wood as far as the middle and fasten it down with a row of clasps, so that in use the iron breaks sooner than the binding works loose, even though at the base, where it joins the wood, its thickness is three half-fingers; so great is the care they take over the fastening.

On top of all this they adorn themselves with a circlet of feathers and three upright purple or black plumes,

about a cubit high; with these set on the crown of the head along with the rest of the arms, the man appears twice his own height, and his look is handsome and terrifying to the enemy.

Most of the soldiers, after adding a bronze plate a span square—which they place in front of the chest and call a heart-guard (pectorale)—have their full equipment complete.

But those rated above ten thousand drachmas (asses) wear, instead of the heart-guard and along with the rest, a coat of chain mail (lorica).

The principes and triarii are armed in the same manner, except that instead of the heavy javelins the triarii carry thrusting spears (hastae).

# Book 6 Chapter 24: Officers of Infantry and Cavalry

From each of the groups mentioned above, except the youngest, they elect ten company commanders (centurions) by merit. After these they make a second selection of ten more.

All of these they have titled company commanders, and the first one chosen is also a member of the council of war. These officers in turn select an equal number of rear-rank men (optiones).

Next, together with the company commanders, they divide each age-group into ten parts, except for the skirmishers, and they assign to each part of the selected men two leaders and two rear-rank officers.

The skirmishers they distribute in equal proportion across all the parts according to their total number.

Each part they called a unit (ordo), a company (manipulus), and a vexillum, and the leaders they called centurions and ordinum ductores.

These officers in turn select for each company, from the men left over, two of the most vigorous and noble men to serve as standard-bearers.

They reasonably appoint two leaders for each unit: since it is uncertain both what a leader may do and what may happen to him, and since the demands of war allow no excuse for delay, they never want a company to be left without a leader and commander.

When both are present, the first one chosen commands the right part of the company, and the second leads the men on the left of the unit; when one is absent, the one who remains commands them all.

They want their company commanders to be not so much bold and fond of danger as men fit to command—steady, and rather of a deep spirit—men who will not rush into attack or open the battle on their own initiative, but who, when overpowered and hard pressed, will hold their ground and die in defense of their post.
