# Book 7 Chapter 17: The Sack of Sardis

When everything was ready for them, as soon as the moon had gone down, Lagoras and his party came secretly to the cliffs with their ladders and hid themselves under an overhanging ledge.

When day came, and the guards had left that place, and the king, according to custom, had sent some men to their reserve posts while bringing the main body out into the hippodrome and drawing them up, at first what was happening aroused no suspicion in anyone.

But once the two ladders had been set in place, and Dionysius went up first by one while Lagoras went up by the other, excitement and movement spread through the camp.

For those climbing the overhanging brow toward the cliff were out of sight for the people in the city and for Achaeus and his men in the citadel, but from the camp the daring of those who were climbing and pressing on was plainly visible.

Therefore the soldiers there stood speechless, amazed by the strangeness of the thing and at the same time, as they foresaw and feared what would happen, filled alike with dread and delight.

The king, seeing the stir throughout the whole camp and wishing to draw off both his own people and those in the city from what was actually under way, led his force forward and attacked the gates on the opposite side, called the Persian Gates.

Achaeus, seeing from the citadel that the movement among the opposing forces was different from what was usual, was greatly perplexed, distressed, and utterly unable to understand what was happening.

Still, he sent men to meet the attack at the gate; but because they had to make their descent by a narrow and precipitous route, the relief was slow in arriving.

Aribazus, who had been posted in the city, rushed in all innocence to the gates where he saw Antiochus attacking; he sent some men up onto the wall, while others he let out through the gate, urging them to keep the attackers off and engage the enemy hand to hand.

# Book 7 Chapter 18: Sardis Destroyed

At this very moment the party with Lagoras, Theodotus, and Dionysius crossed the cliffs and came to the gate below them.

Some of them fought with the men coming to meet them, while others cut through the bars. At the same time the men stationed outside for this purpose rushed up and did nearly the same thing.

The gates were quickly opened, the two thousand entered, and they took possession of the area around the theatre.

Once this happened, all those who had left the walls and the gate called the Persian Gate—the point to which Aribazus and his men had earlier hurried with reinforcements—rushed off in haste to bring support against those who had burst in.

And while this was happening, because the gate was standing open during the withdrawal, some of the king's men burst in as well, following close behind the men who were retreating.

When these men gained control of the gate, others were now entering in an unbroken stream, while others cut through the neighboring gates.

After only a brief struggle against those who had entered, Aribazus and all the people in the city turned and fled toward the citadel.

When that happened, the men with Theodotus and Lagoras remained at the positions by the theatre, acting with intelligence and practical skill as a reserve for the whole operation, while the rest of the force poured in from every side and took the city.

And from then on, some killed everyone they met, some set the houses on fire, and others rushed to plunder and profit, so that the destruction and sack of the city became complete.

Thus Antiochus became master of Sardis.
