For just as now, when Philip listened to Aratus, he kept faith with the Messenians in the matter of the citadel, and, as the saying goes, applied a small remedy to the great wound created by the earlier slaughter,

The same reasoning applies also to the affairs in Crete. For there too, when he used Aratus as his guide in the larger policy, he not only committed no injustice, but did not even offend any of the islanders; he kept all the Cretans under his hand and drew all the Greeks into goodwill toward himself by the dignity of his conduct.

But then again, once he followed Demetrius and became responsible for the misfortunes of the Messenians just described, he lost at once both the goodwill of his allies and the trust of the rest of the Greeks.

So great is the weight for young kings, both toward the ruin and toward the restoration of their rule, that lies in the choice and judgment of the friends who attend them—a matter about which most men, I do not know how, are careless and give not even the slightest forethought.

# Book 7 Chapter 15: The War of Antiochus with Achaeus

Around Sardis there were unceasing and continuous skirmishes and dangers by night and by day, as the soldiers on both sides devised every kind of ambush, counter-ambush, and surprise attack against one another; to write out the details would be not only useless but altogether too long.

In the end, when the siege had already entered its second year, Lagoras the Cretan, a man with considerable experience in warfare, observed that the strongest cities generally fall most easily into the hands of enemies because of the carelessness of those who live in them, whenever, trusting in their natural or man-made strength, they neglect guard duty and grow wholly slack.

He had also recognized that captures tend to be made precisely at the strongest points, the very places the enemy thinks beyond hope.

So at that time, seeing that the prevailing opinion about the strength of Sardis had led everyone to despair of taking it by such an operation, and that they had only one hope left, namely to master the city through famine,

he paid all the more attention and explored every possible course, eager to seize on some opening of that kind.

When he noticed that the wall near the place called the Saw—this is the point joining the citadel and the city—was left unguarded, he conceived this hope and plan.

He discovered the negligence of the guards from the following sign.

The place was extremely precipitous, and below it lay a ravine into which the dead from the city and the carcasses of horses and pack animals were thrown; and so there was always a gathering there of vultures and other birds.

Observing this, the man I mentioned noticed that when the birds had eaten their fill they regularly settled and rested on the cliffs and on the wall, and from this he concluded that the wall was necessarily unguarded and remained deserted for most of the time.

After that he approached by night with care and examined the possible routes of access and the positions for ladders.

The king immediately did as he asked, and the men mentioned, after coming to an understanding and sharing all the details among themselves, waited for a moonless night in the hours before dawn.

Once they had obtained such a night, on the very day on which they intended to act, late in the afternoon they selected from the whole camp fifteen of the strongest men in body and spirit, who were to carry the ladders, climb up with them, and share the danger.

After these they chose another thirty men to wait in reserve at some distance, so that when the first party had crossed over and reached the gate nearby, these men should rush up from outside and try to break through the hinges and the gate-fastening, while the others from within dealt with the bar and the bolt-pins.

They also appointed two thousand men to follow after these, who, once they had burst in, were to seize the area around the theatre, which was favorably situated against both those coming from the citadel and those coming from the city.

And so that the selection of the men might not arouse any suspicion of the truth, the king spread the story that the Aetolians were about to force an entry into the city through a certain ravine, and that energetic precautions had to be taken in view of that report.
