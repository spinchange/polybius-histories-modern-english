# Book 5 Chapter 13: The Return From Thermus

Philip, then — to resume the thread after that digression — gathered up as much as he could carry or transport from Thermus and set out on the march. He returned by the same route he had taken in coming, putting the plunder and the heavier weapons in front, while leaving the Acarnanians and the mercenaries to bring up the rear.

He hurried as fast as possible through the rough country, because he expected the Aetolians to fall on the rear guard, trusting to the strength of the terrain. And that is exactly what happened almost at once. The Aetolians, who had come to support the place and had gathered in a force of nearly three thousand, did not dare approach while Philip was on the higher ground; they kept themselves hidden in various concealed spots under the leadership of Alexander of Trichonium. But as soon as the rear guard started moving, they rushed straight into Thermus and began pressing the men at the back of the column.

As the rear was thrown into confusion, the Aetolians attacked with even greater fury and launched their charges more confidently, relying on the ground. Philip, however, had foreseen what would happen. On the descent he had placed the Illyrians and the best of the peltasts under cover of a hill.

When these men suddenly rose and charged the enemy as they came on, the rest of the Aetolians fled in wild disorder over country with no road and no track. About one hundred and thirty were killed, and almost as many more were captured.

After this success, the rear guard quickly burned Pamphium, got through the narrow passes in safety, and joined the Macedonians. Philip had already encamped near Metapa and was waiting there for the rear guard to come up.

The next day he demolished Metapa and marched on, then encamped near the place called Acrae.

On the following day he advanced again, laying waste the countryside as he went, and camped near Conope, where he stayed through the next day.

The day after that he struck camp once more and marched along the Achelous as far as Stratus. After crossing the river, he drew up his force beyond the range of missiles.

# Book 5 Chapter 14: Skirmish at Stratus

He was testing the men inside the city, since he had heard that the Aetolians had gathered in Stratus a force of about three thousand infantry, four hundred cavalry, and five hundred Cretans.

But since no one dared come out, he set out again and led the advance guard forward, continuing the march as if for Limnaea and the ships.

Then, as the rear guard had just passed the city, a few Aetolian horsemen came out at first and began harassing the men at the back.

After that, when a larger body of Cretans had issued from the city and some Aetolian horsemen had joined their own cavalry, the fighting became more general, and the rear guard was forced into danger by the sudden turn of events.

At first the struggle was evenly matched on both sides. But when Philip's mercenaries were supported by Illyrians, the Aetolian cavalry and mercenaries gave way and fled in scattered groups.

Most of them were pursued by the king's men right up to the gates and the walls, and about a hundred were cut down.

After this action, the garrison stayed quiet, while the rear guard safely rejoined the camp and the ships.

Philip pitched camp early in the day and offered thanks to the gods for the favorable outcome of his enterprise. He also sent for the officers, intending to entertain them all at a banquet.

For he felt that he had led his army into a dangerous country, one that no one had ever dared to enter before; yet not only had he marched into it with an army, he had also completed everything he set out to do and returned in safety.

He was in high spirits over this and was occupied with receiving the officers. But Megaleas and Leontius were deeply resentful at the king's success.

# Book 5 Chapter 15: Megaleas and Crinon Fined

They were already under suspicion, because both the king and the others noticed that they showed less pleasure than the rest at what had happened.

But as the drinking went on and the company lost all restraint, they were compelled to go along with the others for a while; before long they revealed their real selves.

When the gathering broke up, they wandered about in drunken confusion looking for Aratus.

When they met him on his way back, they first insulted him, and then tried to pelt him with stones.

As more people rushed to help both sides, the camp was thrown into uproar and confusion. When the king heard the shouting, he sent men to investigate the matter and stop the disturbance.

Aratus, when they arrived, explained what had happened and produced the people standing by as witnesses; then he withdrew to his own tent. Leontius, however, somehow slipped away in the confusion.

The king sent for Megaleas and Crinon. When he understood what had happened, he rebuked them sharply.

But they did not submit in the least; instead they answered back, saying that they would not stop their campaign against Aratus until they had paid him back.

Angered by this remark, the king at once required them to provide security for a fine of twenty talents and ordered them to be taken into custody.

# Book 5 Chapter 16: Arrival at Leucas

The next day he called Aratus back and encouraged him to keep his nerve, promising that he would see the matter settled as it should be.

Leontius, when he learned what had happened to Megaleas, came to the king's tent with some peltasts, convinced that his youth would frighten the king and quickly force him to regret what he had done.

When he came into his presence, he asked who had dared lay hands on Megaleas and who had dared send him off to prison.

But when the king firmly replied that he himself had given the order, Leontius was struck dumb. After uttering a cry of anger and distress, he went off in a rage.

The king then put to sea with the whole fleet and, after crossing the gulf, soon anchored at Leucas. He gave orders to those assigned to supervise the spoils not to delay in carrying out that work, and then called his friends together and held a hearing in the case of Megaleas.

Aratus brought out the story of Leontius and his party from the beginning, describing in detail the massacre they had carried out at Argos after the separation from Antigonus, their arrangement with Apelles, and their attempt to block the action against the Palaeans.

He supported every charge with evidence and with witnesses. Since Megaleas and his side could make no reply, Philip's friends unanimously condemned them.

Crinon remained in custody, while Leontius stood surety for Megaleas's fine.

So the scheme of Apelles and Leontius ended very differently from what they had first hoped. They had thought that by frightening Aratus and isolating Philip they would be able to do whatever suited them best; instead, everything turned out in the opposite way.
