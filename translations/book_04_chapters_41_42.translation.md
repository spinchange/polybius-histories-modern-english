# Book 4 Chapter 41: Flow of the Danube into the Black Sea

What is happening is clear. Since the Danube empties from Europe into the Pontus through several mouths, the result is that opposite it, at a distance of nearly a thousand stades, there has formed a long bank, lying a day's sail out from the shore [the bank that exists there now], built up from the silt carried out through the mouths.

Ships sailing into the Pontus, while still apparently running through open water, are caught off guard and run aground on these places at night. Sailors call them the Breasts.

As for why the deposit does not build up right against the shore but is instead pushed far out, this must be reckoned the cause.

For just so far as the currents of the rivers prevail through the force of their flow and drive back the sea, exactly that far must the soil and everything else carried along by the streams be pushed forward too, finding no rest or fixed position at all;

but once the currents are spent by the depth and great mass of the sea, then it is natural that the silt, now sinking downward by its own nature, comes to rest and settles into a fixed position.

This is why, in the case of violent and great rivers, the deposits build up far out, while the water close to the land is deep; whereas with smaller, gently flowing rivers the sandbanks form right at their mouths.

This becomes especially evident during heavy rains: for then even the most ordinary streams, once they overpower the surf at their mouths, drive the silt out to sea to such an extent that the distance is proportional in each case to the force of the streams pouring in.

And as to the size of the bank just described, and in general the quantity of stones, timber, and earth carried down by the rivers, there is no reason whatever to be skeptical —

for that would be foolish — when we see before our own eyes how some chance torrent in a short time will often gouge out and cut through sheer places, carry off every kind of timber, earth, and stone, and pile up such mounds that at times it alters a place and makes it unrecognizable in a brief span of time —

— these same places. From all this it is not reasonable to wonder how rivers of such size and such character, flowing continuously, accomplish some of the effects described and in the end fill up the Pontus.

For by right reasoning it appears not merely probable but inevitable that this should come to pass.

A sign of what is to come is this: by just the degree to which the Maeotis is now fresher than the Pontic sea, by that same degree the Pontus is plainly seen to differ from our own sea.

From this it is clear that, whenever the time it takes for the Maeotis to be filled bears the same ratio to the time required as the size of the one basin bears to the other, then the Pontus too will come to be shallow, fresh, and marsh-like, just as the Maeotic lake is.

Indeed, we must suppose this will happen even sooner, in proportion as the streams of the rivers emptying into it are greater and more numerous.

Let this, then, be our answer to those who are inclined to disbelieve that the Pontus is now being silted up, and that one day it will be filled, and that so vast an open sea will become a lake and a marsh.

And let it be said all the more for the sake of the falsehoods and marvel-mongering of seafarers, so that we may not be forced, like children, to gape at everything that is said out of inexperience, but instead, having some traces of the truth, may be able from them to judge to some degree whether what is reported by various people is true or the opposite.
