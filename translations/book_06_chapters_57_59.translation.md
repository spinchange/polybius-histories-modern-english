# Book 6 Chapter 57: Conclusion: Dangers Ahead for Rome

That everything which exists is subject to decay and change hardly needs further argument, for the necessity imposed by nature is enough to establish that truth.

And since every kind of constitution naturally decays in two ways, one from external causes and one from causes that grow within itself, the external mode is impossible to define with certainty, but the internal follows a set order.

I have already explained what form of constitution naturally comes first, what comes second, and how they pass into one another,

so that those able to connect the beginning with the end of the present discussion can already predict the future for themselves. In my judgment the result is plain.

For when a state has survived many great dangers and afterwards reached undisputed supremacy and power, it is obvious that as prosperity settles into it over time, private lives become more luxurious and men grow more ambitious than they should in the pursuit of office and other objects.

As these tendencies advance, the beginning of decline will come from love of office, from resentment at loss of status, and, along with these, from arrogance and luxury in private life.

The people will be the agents through whom the change gains its name, once they come to think that some men wrong them out of greed while others puff them up with flattery because they are hunting office.

For then, in anger and making every decision under the sway of passion, the people will no longer be willing to obey or even to share power equally with their leaders, but will want the whole or the greater part for themselves.

And when that happens, the constitution will take on the finest of names—freedom and democracy—but in reality it will become the worst of things, mob-rule.

Since I have now discussed the composition, growth, zenith, present condition, and differences—both for better and for worse—of the Roman constitution as compared with others, I here bring this account of the constitution to its conclusion.

# Book 6 Chapter 58: Even Hannibal Acknowledges the Spirit of the Romans

Now, returning to the connected course of the history at the point from which I digressed, I will briefly recall one action, so that I may make the excellence and power of the Roman constitution at that time clear not by argument alone but by deeds, as if a master craftsman were displaying one specimen of his work.

After Hannibal won the battle at Cannae and captured the eight thousand Romans who were guarding the camp, he took them all alive and allowed them to send envoys home to negotiate their ransom and salvation.

They selected ten of their most distinguished men, and after making them swear that they would return to him, Hannibal sent them off.

But one of those chosen, after already leaving the camp, said that he had forgotten something and turned back; after retrieving what he had left behind he set out again, thinking that by his return he had kept faith and released himself from the oath.

When the envoys reached Rome, they begged and urged the senate not to begrudge the captured men their safety, but to let each of them save himself by paying three minae and returning to his family, for Hannibal, they said, was willing to grant that.

They also argued that the prisoners deserved rescue, since they had neither shown cowardice in the battle nor done anything unworthy of Rome, but had merely been left to guard the camp and, after all the others had perished in battle, had been overtaken by circumstances and surrendered to the enemy.

Yet the Romans, though they had suffered terrible losses in battle, had been deprived of almost all their allies, and could expect before long to face danger for their very fatherland,

still, after hearing these pleas, neither yielded in the face of disaster in any way unworthy of themselves nor overlooked anything that sound judgment required.

They saw Hannibal's purpose: by this act he meant both to get money and to strip the opposing troops of their eagerness for battle by showing that even the defeated still had hope of safety.

So far were they from doing what was requested that they counted neither pity for the men's relatives nor the future use they might have of greater importance.

Instead, by refusing to ransom the prisoners, they proved Hannibal's calculation and his hopes empty, and they laid down for their own people the rule that in battle they must either conquer or die, since for the defeated no other hope of safety remained.

Accordingly they dismissed the nine envoys, who returned voluntarily in accordance with their oath, but the one who had tried by a trick to escape his oath they bound and sent back to the enemy.

Thus Hannibal was not so much delighted by his victory over the Romans in battle as shaken and astonished by the steadiness and greatness of spirit the Romans showed in their deliberations.
