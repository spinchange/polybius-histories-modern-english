# Book 4 Chapter 53: War In Crete

…the end. At about this time the Cnossians sent envoys to the Rhodians and persuaded them to dispatch the ships under Polemocles' command, together with three more undecked vessels which they were to launch and send in addition.

When this had been done and the ships had arrived in Crete, the people of Eleutherna, suspecting that Polemocles and his men had put to death their fellow-citizen Timarchus as a favor to the Cnossians, first proclaimed a right of reprisal against the Rhodians, and after that went to open war.

The Lyttians too, a little before this time, met with an irreparable disaster. For the affairs of Crete as a whole were at that time in roughly the following state.

The Cnossians, having come to an understanding with the Gortynians, had brought the whole of Crete under their power except the city of the Lyttians; and since this city alone refused to submit, they set about making war on it, eager to destroy it utterly, as an example and a source of terror to the rest of the Cretans.

At first, then, all the Cretans together waged war against the Lyttians; but when rivalry arose out of trivial occasions—as is the way with the Cretans—they fell into faction against one another.

The Polyrrhenians, the Ceraïtae, and the Lappaeans, and with them the Horii together with the Arcadians, with one accord broke away from their friendship with the Cnossians,

and resolved to make common cause with the Lyttians. Among the Gortynians, the older men chose the side of the Cnossians, while the younger chose that of the Lyttians, and so they fell into faction with each other.

The Cnossians, since this upheaval among their allies had come upon them unexpectedly, called in a thousand men from Aetolia in accordance with their alliance.

As soon as this had happened, the older men of the Gortynians seized the citadel and brought in the Cnossians and the Aetolians; and some of the young men they expelled, and others they killed,

and they handed the city over to the Cnossians. At about the same time, when the Lyttians had marched out in full force into enemy territory, the Cnossians, perceiving what had happened, seized Lyttos while it was stripped of its defenders.

They sent off the children and the women to Cnossus, and after burning the city, razing it to the ground, and ravaging it in every possible way, they returned home.

The Lyttians, arriving back at their city from the expedition and seeing what had befallen it, were so overcome with grief in their hearts that not one of those present had the courage even to enter his homeland;

but all of them, after marching around it in a circle, and after wailing aloud and lamenting again and again the fate of their fatherland and their own, turned back and withdrew once more to the city of the Lappaeans.

The Lappaeans received them kindly and with all eagerness; and these men, who in a single day had become cityless strangers instead of citizens, joined their allies in making war against the Cnossians.

Thus Lyttos—a colony and kinswoman of the Lacedaemonians, the most ancient of the cities of Crete, and by common consent the constant nurse of the bravest men among the Cretans—was utterly and unexpectedly swept away.
