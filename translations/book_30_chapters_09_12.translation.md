# Book 30 Chapter 9: Polyaratus of Rhodes

In sheer folly and cowardice, Polyaratus far surpassed Deinon.

For when Popilius ordered King Ptolemy to send Polyaratus to Rome, the king, out of consideration both for his country and for Polyaratus himself, decided not to send him to Rome, but chose instead to send him to Rhodes, since that was also what Polyaratus was asking.

So he provided a small boat, handed Polyaratus over to a certain Demetrius, one of his friends, and sent him off. He also wrote to the Rhodians about the transfer.

But when Polyaratus put in at Phaselis during the voyage and conceived whatever notion it was, he took suppliant boughs in his hands and fled to the public hearth.

If anyone had asked him what he actually wanted, I am persuaded that he could not even have said. For if he wanted to reach his own country, what need was there for suppliant boughs? That was already the destination intended by the men escorting him.

And if he wanted to go to Rome, then whether he wished it or not, that was bound to happen. What other possibility was left? There was no other place that could receive him safely.

Even so, when the people of Phaselis sent to Rhodes and asked them to come fetch Polyaratus and take charge of him, the Rhodians wisely decided to send an unarmed escort vessel,

but they forbade its commander to take Polyaratus aboard, because the men from Alexandria had been ordered to convey him on to Rome.

When the ship arrived at Phaselis, Epichares, who was in command of it, refused to receive the man on board.

But Demetrius, who had been appointed by the king to conduct his transfer, ordered him to get up and sail on, and the people of Phaselis backed him, because they were afraid that otherwise some blame from the Romans would fall on them.

So Polyaratus, overwhelmed by the situation, went back aboard the boat with Demetrius. But during the departure, seizing a favorable moment,

he ran off and took refuge again, this time in Caunus, and there likewise begged the Caunians to help him.

When they too pushed him away because they were aligned with Rhodes, he sent messages to the people of Cibyra, begging them to receive him into their city and send him an escort.

For he had some claim on that city because the sons of Pancrates the tyrant had been brought up under his care.

They were persuaded and did what he asked, but when he arrived in Cibyra he threw both himself and the Cibyratae into even greater difficulty than before, when he had been at Phaselis.

For they neither dared to keep him with them, because they feared danger from Rome, nor were they able to send him on to Rome, because they had no experience in maritime matters, being entirely an inland people.

In the end they were forced to send envoys to Rhodes and to the Roman commander in Macedonia, asking them to take charge of the man.

And when Lucius wrote to the Cibyratae to keep Polyaratus under careful guard and send him to Rhodes, and to the Rhodians to arrange transport by sea so that he might be conveyed safely into Roman hands, and both parties obeyed these written instructions,

in this way Polyaratus finally came to Rome, after making as public a spectacle as he could of his own folly and cowardice, and after being handed over, through his own stupidity, not only by King Ptolemy but also by the people of Phaselis, the Cibyratae, and the Rhodians.

Why, then, have I gone on at such length about Polyaratus and Deinon?

Not in order to seem to trample on their misfortunes. That would be altogether shameful. Rather, by making their folly plain, I hope to better prepare those who fall into similar crises to think and decide well.

# Book 30 Chapter 10: Statue-bases for Perseus Used by Aemilius

Nothing shows more clearly at once the swiftness and instability of Fortune than when a man finds that the very things he thought he was laboring hardest to prepare for his own honor have in fact been made ready for his enemies.

For Perseus was having columns made, and Lucius Aemilius, finding them unfinished, completed them and set up his own statues on them.

He admired the position of the city and the advantageous site of the acropolis for commanding both the regions inside the Isthmus and those beyond it.

After noting the strength of Sicyon and the importance of the city of Argos, he came to Epidaurus.

Having long been eager to see Olympia, he set out there.

Polybius:

Lucius Aemilius entered the sacred precinct at Olympia, and when he saw the statue he was astonished. He said that Pheidias alone seemed to him to have reproduced the Zeus of Homer, because although he had formed great expectations of Olympia, he had found the reality greater than what he had expected.

# Book 30 Chapter 11: The Greek Prisoners In Italy

The Aetolians had long been accustomed to make their living from plunder and other such lawless practices.

And as long as they were free to prey upon and loot the Greeks, they drew their livelihood from that, regarding every land as enemy territory.

But afterward, when the Romans took control of affairs and they were cut off from support from outside, they turned upon one another.

Even before this, in their civil war, there was no sort of outrage they did not commit.

And not long before, after having tasted one another's blood in the massacres at Arsinoe, they were ready for anything, their minds so brutalized that they would not even allow their leaders to offer advice.

That is why conditions in Aetolia were full of disorder, lawlessness, and murder, and nothing they did was carried out with calculation or settled purpose. Everything was done blindly and in confusion, as if some violent storm had burst upon them.

# Book 30 Chapter 12: Epirus Also In Turmoil

The people of Epirus were acting in much the same way.

For to the extent that most of them were more moderate than the people of Aetolia, to that same extent their leader was more impious and lawless than anyone else.

For I do not think there ever has been, or ever will be, a man more savage or more reckless than Charops.
