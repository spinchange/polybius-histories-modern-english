# Book 20 Chapter 5: Continued Decline of Boeotia

The main stages of the Boeotians' gradual decline into folly came about among them in the following way.

Immediately after the defeat mentioned above, they abandoned the Achaeans and attached their nation to the Aetolians.

But when, some time later, the Aetolians in turn went to war with Demetrius, the father of Philip, the Boeotians abandoned them as well; and when Demetrius came into Boeotia with an army, they made no trial of the dangers of resistance but submitted themselves wholly to the Macedonians.

Yet, since a faint flicker of their ancestral glory still lingered, there were some who were displeased with the present state of affairs and with the policy of obeying the Macedonians in everything.

For this reason there came to be strong political opposition between these men and the party of Ascondas and Neon, the ancestors of Brachylles; for these were the men who at that time leaned most strongly toward Macedonia.

In the end, however, the party of Ascondas gained the upper hand, owing to a turn of events of the following kind.

After the death of Demetrius, Antigonus, who was acting as Philip's guardian, was sailing on some business toward the outermost parts of Boeotia, in the direction of Larymna, when an unexpected ebb of the tide left his ships stranded on dry land.

Just at this moment a report had reached the Boeotians that Antigonus intended to overrun their territory. Neon, who was then commander of the cavalry and was leading all the Boeotian horse about with him to guard the country, came upon Antigonus and his men while they were at a loss and helpless because of what had happened.

And though he had it in his power to inflict serious harm on the Macedonians, he decided, contrary to their expectation, to spare them.

This action of his pleased the rest of the Boeotians, but it did not please the Thebans at all.

Antigonus, however, when the tide flowed back a little later and his ships were lifted free, felt deep gratitude toward Neon for not having joined in attacking them during their misfortune; and he himself completed the voyage to Asia on which he had set out.

And so afterward, when he had defeated the Spartan Cleomenes and become master of Lacedaemon, he left Brachylles as governor of the city, repaying him in this way for the good service done by his father Neon. From these favors the house of Brachylles came to be restored to no small degree.

Nor was this the only consideration Antigonus showed them; rather, continuously—now he himself, now Philip—supplying them with funds and constantly lending them strength, they quickly overcame the rival political party in Thebes and forced everyone, except a very few, to side with Macedonia.

Such, then, was the beginning that the affairs of the house of Neon took, and of its alliance with the Macedonians—

—and of the increase of its fortune. Meanwhile the public affairs of the Boeotians had fallen into such a wretched condition that for nearly twenty-five years justice had not been administered among them, neither in private contracts nor in public charges.

Instead, some of the magistrates would order the dispatch of garrisons and others would proclaim national levies, and in this way they continually obstructed the course of justice; and some of the generals even made payments out of the public funds to the poor among the people.

From this the masses were taught to attach themselves to such men and to secure offices for them—men through whom they could expect to pay no penalty for their crimes and debts, and from time to time to gain some share of public property through the favor of those in office. The man who contributed most to this state of things was

Opheltas, who was forever devising some new scheme that seemed for the moment to benefit the many, but was bound in the end, by general admission, to ruin them all.

Along with these things followed another unfortunate fashion. Those who died childless no longer left their property to their relatives, as had earlier been the custom among them, but assigned it to feasting and drinking and made it common property for their friends.

And many even of those who had children would distribute the greater part of their estate to their dining clubs, so that there were many Boeotians who had more dinners in the month than there were days allotted to the month.

For this reason the Megarians, hating such a state of affairs and remembering their former membership in the federation alongside the Achaeans, once more turned away toward the Achaeans and their party.

For the Megarians had originally been part of the Achaean political community from the time of Antigonus Gonatas; but when Cleomenes took up a position on the Isthmus, being cut off they attached themselves to the Boeotians with the consent of the Achaeans.

A little before the times now under discussion, becoming dissatisfied with the Boeotian government, they once again turned back toward the Achaeans.

The Boeotians, enraged at what they regarded as contempt, marched out against the Megarians in full force under arms.

And when the Megarians took no notice at all of their arrival, the Boeotians, thus provoked, set about besieging the city and launching assaults on it.

But when a panic fell upon them, together with a rumor that Philopoemen had arrived with the Achaeans, they left their scaling-ladders against the wall and fled headlong back to their own land.

# Book 20 Chapter 7: Fortune and Degeneracy of the Boeotians

With their politics in such a condition, the Boeotians somehow slipped through, by good luck, even the crises of the times of Philip and of Antiochus.

In the period that followed, however, they did not escape; rather, as if Fortune were deliberately exacting requital, she seemed to come down heavily upon them—matters of which we shall make mention in what follows. —

The majority put forward, as the pretext for their estrangement from the Romans, the killing of Brachylles and the campaign that Titus made against Coronea on account of the murders of Romans that were taking place on the roads;

but in truth they were diseased in their souls because of the causes already described.

For when the king drew near, the leading men of the Boeotians went out to meet him; and after joining him and conversing in a friendly manner, they conducted him into Thebes. —

Antiochus, the one called the Great, whom the Romans brought down—as Polybius records in his twentieth book—came to Chalcis in Euboea and celebrated his marriage there. He was fifty years old and had taken upon himself the two greatest of undertakings: the liberation of the Greeks, as he himself proclaimed it, and the war against the Romans.

Having fallen in love with a young Chalcidian girl in the very midst of the war, he set his heart on marrying her, being a heavy drinker and one who delighted in drunken revels.

She was the daughter of Cleoptolemus, one of the men of distinction, and she surpassed all other women in beauty.

And while celebrating his marriage there in Chalcis, he spent the whole winter on the spot, taking not the slightest thought for the business at hand; and he gave the girl the name Euboea.

So when he was defeated in the war, he fled to Ephesus with his new bride.

And out of his whole army, except for the five hundred or so who were around the king, scarcely anyone escaped; and even of the ten thousand soldiers whom—on Polybius' authority—we have recorded that the king brought across with him into Greece, only a tiny number survived.
