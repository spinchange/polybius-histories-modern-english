# Book 9 Chapter 1: Extract from the Preface

These, then, are the most notable actions included in the Olympiad already mentioned, and in the four-year span which, as we say, must be counted as an Olympiad. I will try to explain them in two books.

I am not unaware that my work has something austere about it, and that because of the uniform character of its plan it is fitted for, and judged by, only one class of readers.

For almost all other historians, or at least most of them, draw many people to their books by making use of every branch of history.

Genealogical writing attracts the lover of such tales; accounts of colonies, city-foundations, and kinships among peoples attract the inquisitive and antiquarian reader, as one may see in Ephorus; political readers are drawn by narratives of the deeds of nations, cities, and dynasts.

I, however, have directed myself solely to this last kind of subject and have arranged the whole of my work around it. So, as I said before, I have made it especially suited to one kind of reader, but have also made the reading less entertaining for the majority.

Why I rejected the other parts of history and chose instead to write only about actions, I have explained more fully elsewhere; still, there is no harm in briefly reminding my readers of it here once again, simply to make the point clear.

# Book 9 Chapter 2: Why Focus on Actions

Since many writers, and in many different ways, have already treated genealogies, myths, colonies,

and also kinships and city-foundations, any man who now takes up those subjects must either present what belongs to others as if it were his own—which is the most disgraceful course of all—or else, if he refuses to do that, plainly waste his labor by working over matters that earlier writers have already explained well enough and handed down to posterity.

For these reasons, and for many others besides, I left such themes aside.

Pragmatic history, on the other hand, deserved approval first because events are always producing something new and therefore require a fresh account; one generation cannot simply report the actions that come after it.

Secondly, this kind of history is the most useful of all—and though it always was so, it is especially so in our own day, now that experience and the arts have advanced so far that eager students can handle whatever circumstances arise almost methodically, as if by rule.

Therefore I aimed not so much at giving pleasure to casual readers as at benefiting those who pay serious attention; and so, leaving the rest aside, I devoted myself to this branch of history.

Those who read my work with care from beginning to end will be the surest witnesses to the truth of what I say here.

# Book 9 Chapter 3: The Hannibalian War

Hannibal surrounded Appius's camp on every side and at first confined himself to skirmishing and testing the Romans, wanting to draw them out into battle.

But since no one responded, what followed came to resemble a siege. The cavalry charged in squadrons and, with loud shouting, hurled their javelins into the camp, while the infantry advanced in companies and tried to tear down the palisade.

Even so, he could not move the Romans from their fixed resolve. Their light-armed troops beat back the men attacking the rampart, while the heavily armed soldiers, sheltering themselves from the storm of missiles with their armor, stayed in formation by their standards.

Hannibal, dissatisfied with the whole situation because he could neither break into the city nor draw the Romans out, began considering what he ought to do next.

And in my opinion these events would have seemed puzzling not only to the Carthaginians, but to anyone else who heard of them.

For who would not have found it hard to believe that the Romans, after being defeated in many battles by the Carthaginians and not daring any longer to meet them in open combat, still could neither be forced to give way nor induced to abandon the open country?

Up to this time they had merely shadowed Hannibal, always keeping to the foothills; but now they had taken their stand on the plains and in the most conspicuous region of Italy were besieging its strongest city, even while the enemy pressed them on every side—an enemy whom they could not even bear to face in pitched battle.

Meanwhile the Carthaginians, though they were winning battles without interruption, at some moments were in difficulties no less than the defeated side.

It seems to me that the cause lay partly in the deliberate policy of both sides, since both had recognized that Hannibal's cavalry formation was the reason why the Carthaginians won and the Romans were beaten.

That is why, after the battles, the defeated Roman camps naturally followed at a distance: they moved through terrain where the enemy cavalry could do them no harm.

And for the same reason, what happened around Capua at that time followed naturally for both sides alike.

# Book 9 Chapter 4: The Siege of Capua

For the Roman army did not dare to come out and fight because it feared the enemy cavalry,

but it remained boldly inside its camp, clearly understanding that the cavalry by which it had been beaten in battle would there be powerless to hurt it.

The Carthaginians, on the other hand, quite naturally could not remain encamped for long with their cavalry, because the Romans had destroyed all the fodder in the neighboring country for this very purpose, and it was impossible for so many horsemen and so many pack animals to bring hay and barley on their backs from a great distance.

Yet they also did not dare, if camped without their cavalry, to assault enemies protected by ditch and palisade, since even apart from the cavalry the danger of an evenly matched engagement seemed uncertain enough.

Besides this, Hannibal was anxious that the incoming consuls might arrive, pitch camp against him, and put him in severe difficulty by cutting off his supplies.

Reasoning from all this, Hannibal concluded that it was impossible to lift the siege by direct force and adopted another plan.

He thought that if he made his march in secret and suddenly appeared in the region around Rome, he might perhaps achieve some useful result against the city itself by frightening the inhabitants with the unexpectedness of his arrival.

And if not that, he would at least force Appius either to raise the siege in haste to help his country, or to divide his forces—making both the relieving troops and those left behind easier to defeat.
