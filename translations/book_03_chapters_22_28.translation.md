# Book 3 Chapter 22: Treaties between Rome and Carthage

The first treaty between the Romans and the Carthaginians, then, was made in the year of Lucius Junius Brutus and Marcus Horatius, the first men appointed consuls after the abolition of the monarchy—the same men by whom the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol came to be consecrated.

This was thirty years, less two, before Xerxes' crossing into Greece.

I have set the treaty down below, having translated it as accurately as was possible. For so great has the difference become—even among the Romans themselves—between their present language and the ancient one, that the most intelligent of them can scarcely make out some passages, and only after close study.

The treaty runs roughly as follows: "On these terms there shall be friendship between the Romans and the allies of the Romans, and the Carthaginians and the allies of the Carthaginians:

"The Romans and the allies of the Romans shall not sail beyond the Fair Promontory, unless forced to by storm or by enemies.

"If anyone is driven there by force, he shall not be permitted to buy or take anything except what is needed for the repair of his ship or for sacrifices,

[7;8]

"and he shall depart within five days. For those who come for trade there shall be no duty,

"except the fee paid to a herald or clerk. Whatever is sold in their presence shall be owed to the seller on the public credit—that is, whatever is sold either in Libya or in Sardinia.

"If any Roman comes to the part of Sicily over which the Carthaginians rule, the Romans shall in all respects have equal rights.

"The Carthaginians shall do no wrong to the people of Ardea, Antium, Laurentum, Circeii, or Tarracina, nor to any other of the Latins

"who are subject to Rome. And if any are not subject, they shall keep their hands off their cities;

"and if they do take one, they shall hand it back to the Romans undamaged. They shall build no fort in Latin territory. If they enter the country as enemies, they shall not spend the night within it."

# Book 3 Chapter 23: Division of Territory

Now the Fair Promontory is the one that lies out in front of Carthage itself, toward the north.

Beyond it, to the south, the Carthaginians hold that the Romans must on no account sail with warships—because, as it seems to me, they did not want them to become acquainted with the regions around Byzacium or the Lesser Syrtis, the places they call the Emporia, on account of the fertility of the land.

And if anyone is driven there by force of storm or of enemies and needs any of the necessities for sacrifices or for the repair of his ship, these he may take, but nothing beyond them, they hold; and those who have come to anchor there must of necessity depart within five days.

But to Carthage, and to all the part of Libya on the near side of the Fair Promontory, and to Sardinia and the part of Sicily the Carthaginians rule, the Romans are permitted to sail for trade, and the Carthaginians pledge their public credit to guarantee their rights.

From this treaty they make it clear that they speak of Sardinia and Libya as their own; but concerning Sicily they expressly draw the opposite distinction, making the treaty only about those parts of Sicily that fall under Carthaginian dominion.

In the same way the Romans too make the treaty only about Latin territory itself, and make no mention of the rest of Italy, since it did not fall under their authority.

# Book 3 Chapter 24: Treaties Between Rome and Carthage

After this they made another treaty, in which the Carthaginians have additionally included the Tyrians and the people of Utica.

To the Fair Promontory, Mastia and Tarseium are also added, beyond which they hold that the Romans must neither raid nor found a city.

The treaty runs roughly as follows: "On these terms there shall be friendship between the Romans and the allies of the Romans, and the Carthaginians, the Tyrians, and the people of Utica, and their

"allies. The Romans shall not raid, nor trade, nor found a city beyond the Fair Promontory, Mastia, and Tarseium.

"If the Carthaginians take in Latium some city that is not subject to the Romans, they may keep the property and the men,

"but shall hand back the city. If any Carthaginians capture any people with whom the Romans have a peace set down in writing, but who are not subject to them, they shall not bring them into the harbors of the Romans; and if such a one is brought in and a Roman lays claim to him,

"he shall be released. In the same way the Romans too shall not do this.

"If a Roman takes water or supplies from any land over which the Carthaginians rule, with these supplies he shall do no wrong to any with

"whom the Carthaginians have peace and friendship.

"In the same way neither shall a Carthaginian do so; and if he does, he shall not be prosecuted by private vengeance. But if anyone does this,

"the offense shall be treated as a public one. In Sardinia and Libya no Roman shall either trade or found a city *** except so far as to take on supplies or repair a ship; and if a storm drives him in,

"he shall depart within five days. In the part of Sicily the Carthaginians rule, and in Carthage, he may do and sell everything that is permitted to a citizen.

"In the same way a Carthaginian too may act in Rome."

Again in this treaty they press their claim still harder over Libya and Sardinia, treating them as their own property and shutting off every landing-place from the Romans;

but concerning Sicily they make the opposite explicit, speaking only of the part subject to them.

In the same way the Romans too, concerning Latium: they hold that the Carthaginians must do no wrong to the people of Ardea, Antium, Circeii, and Tarracina. These are the cities that enclose the Latin territory along the sea, and it is about this territory that they make the treaty.

# Book 3 Chapter 25: The Third Treaty

Furthermore, the Romans made yet one final treaty, at the time of Pyrrhus' crossing, before the Carthaginians had taken up the war over Sicily.

In it they keep everything else according to the existing agreements, but the following clauses are added to them:

"If they make a written alliance with Pyrrhus, both shall make it on such terms that it remain possible to come to one another's aid in the territory of whichever is being attacked.

"Whichever of the two has need of help, the Carthaginians shall provide the ships, both for the voyage out and for the return; but each side shall provide the pay for its own men.

"The Carthaginians shall also aid the Romans by sea, if there is need; but no one shall compel the crews to disembark against their will."

The oath that had to be sworn was of the following kind.

In the case of the first treaties, the Carthaginians swore by the gods of their fathers, and the Romans by Jupiter Stone, in accordance with some ancient custom; but in the case of this last one, by Mars and Quirinus.

The oath by Jupiter Stone is as follows. The man who makes the sworn pledges over the treaty takes a stone into his hand, and, after he has sworn on the public faith, says these words:

"If I keep my oath, may good things come to me; but if I think or do otherwise, then, while all others are kept safe each in his own country, under his own laws, in possession of his own livelihood, sacred things, and tombs, may I alone be cast out, just as this stone now is."

And having said this, he throws the stone from his hand.

# Book 3 Chapter 26: Treaties Between Rome and Carthage

Such being the case—and these treaties being preserved even now, on bronze tablets, beside the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus in the treasury of the aediles—

who would not, with good reason, be astonished at the historian Philinus? Not because he was ignorant of these treaties—for that is no wonder, since even in our own day the oldest men among both the Romans and the Carthaginians, those reputed to take the keenest interest in public affairs, were ignorant of them—

but [astonished] at where or how he found the nerve to write the very opposite: that there existed treaties between the Romans and the Carthaginians under which the Romans were bound to keep away from the whole of Sicily,

and the Carthaginians from Italy, and that the Romans overstepped these treaties and oaths when they made their first crossing into Sicily—when no such written document had ever come into being or existed at all.

For he says this expressly in his second book. I touched on these matters in the preliminary part of my own work, but postponed treating them in detail until this point, because a good many people too have been led astray from the truth in this matter through trusting Philinus' account.

Yet if on this score someone finds fault with the Romans over the crossing into Sicily—because they took the Mamertines into friendship at all, and afterwards came to their aid when they asked, men who had broken faith treacherously not only with the city of the Messenians but also with that of the Rhegians—he might reasonably seem to have grounds for displeasure.

But if anyone supposes that they made the crossing in violation of their oaths and treaties, he is plainly mistaken.

# Book 3 Chapter 27: The Fourth Treaty

When, then, the war over Sicily had been brought to its conclusion, they made another treaty, in which the main written provisions were these:

"The Carthaginians shall withdraw from the whole of Sicily and from all the islands

"lying between Italy and Sicily. The allies of each side shall have security from the other.

"Neither side shall impose anything, nor erect any public building, nor enlist mercenaries, nor take into alliance the allies of the other, in the other's territories.

"The Carthaginians shall pay over, within ten years, two thousand two hundred talents, and shall give a thousand at once.

"The Carthaginians shall give back to the Romans, without ransom, all the prisoners."

After this again,

when the Libyan war had ended, the Romans, having gone so far as a formal decree of war against the Carthaginians, made the following supplementary terms:

"The Carthaginians shall withdraw from Sardinia and shall pay over a further twelve hundred talents," just as we stated above.

And in addition to the foregoing, a final agreement was reached with Hasdrubal in Iberia, "on the condition that the Carthaginians should not cross the river Ebro

"under arms." Such were the mutual obligations between the Romans and the Carthaginians from the beginning down to the times of Hannibal.

# Book 3 Chapter 28: No Reasonable Pretext for Rome to Claim Sardinia

Just as, then, we find that the Roman crossing into Sicily was not made in violation of their oaths, so too, in the case of the second war—in consequence of which they made the treaty concerning Sardinia—one could find neither a reasonable pretext nor a reasonable ground for the Roman action,

but must admit, by common acknowledgment, that the Carthaginians were compelled, contrary to all justice, by the pressure of circumstances, both to withdraw from Sardinia and to pay over the sum of money mentioned above.

For the charge brought by the Romans in this matter—that the Carthaginians had wronged their seafarers during the Libyan war—had already been disposed of at the time when, having recovered from the Carthaginians all who had been brought in, they in return, as a favor, gave back without ransom the prisoners they themselves held.

The details of this I have set out in the book before this one.

Such being the case, it remains to determine, by careful examination, regarding the war of Hannibal, to which of the two sides the responsibility for it is to be assigned.
