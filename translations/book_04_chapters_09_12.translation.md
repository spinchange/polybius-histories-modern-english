# Book 4 Chapter 9: Dorimachus Consents To Depart

When the men of military age had assembled under arms at Megalopolis in accordance with the decree of the Achaeans —

for it was from this point that I digressed — and when the Messenians once again came before the assembly and begged them not to look on while they were so flagrantly being wronged in violation of the treaty, declaring also their wish to join the common alliance and their eagerness to be enrolled together with the rest,

the leaders of the Achaeans rejected the request for alliance, saying that it was not possible to admit any new member apart from Philip and the other allies.

For the alliance that had been concluded through Antigonus during the period of the Cleomenic war was still binding by oath on them all — on the Achaeans, the Epirotes, the Phocians, the Macedonians, the Boeotians, the Acarnanians, and the Thessalians.

They said, however, that they would march out and come to the Messenians' aid, provided that the envoys present handed over their own sons as hostages to the city of the Lacedaemonians, as a guarantee that they would not make a settlement with the Aetolians without the consent of the Achaeans.

The Lacedaemonians too were encamped on the borders of Megalopolitan territory, having marched out in accordance with the alliance, though they held the position of reserves and onlookers rather than active allies.

Aratus, having settled matters with the Messenians in this way, sent word to the Aetolians, making the decisions known and ordering them to withdraw from Messenian territory and not to set foot in Achaea; otherwise, he warned, he would treat any who entered it as enemies.

When Scopas and Dorimachus heard what was said and learned that the Achaeans had mustered, they judged that it was to their advantage for the moment to comply with the orders.

So they immediately dispatched letter-carriers both to Cyllene and to Ariston, the general of the Aetolians, requesting that the transport vessels be sent to them with all speed from Elis to the island called Pheias.

Two days later they themselves struck camp, laden with booty, and advanced as if toward Elis. For the Aetolians always cultivated the friendship of the Eleans, so that through them they might gain a foothold for their raids and piratical expeditions out of the Peloponnese.

# Book 4 Chapter 10: Aratus Dismisses the Achaean Troops

Aratus waited two days and, naively believing that they would make their return by the route they had indicated, dismissed all the rest of the Achaeans and the Lacedaemonians to their homes,

but keeping three thousand foot and three hundred horse together with the soldiers under Taurion, he advanced along the road to Patrae, intending to march parallel to the Aetolians.

When Dorimachus and his men learned that Aratus was keeping pace with them and remaining under arms — partly fearing that they might be attacked while they were preoccupied with embarking on the ships, and partly being eager to provoke the war —

they sent their booty off to the boats, assigning enough capable men suited to its transport, with instructions to those sent out that the goods should be brought to Rhium, where they intended to make their embarkation.

They themselves at first lay in wait, attending to the dispatch of the booty, and after that changed direction and advanced as if toward Olympia.

But hearing that Taurion with the aforementioned force was in the neighborhood of Cleitor, and reckoning that even so they would not be able to make the crossing from Rhium without danger and a clash,

they decided it was to the advantage of their cause to engage with Aratus's men as quickly as possible, while they were still few in number and unsuspecting of what was coming,

supposing that, if they routed them, they could plunder the country in advance and so make the crossing from Rhium secure, during the time the Achaean levy was hesitating and deliberating about mustering again,

while if Aratus's men were alarmed and avoided battle, unwilling to engage, they could effect their withdrawal without danger whenever it seemed advantageous to them.

Methydrium in Megalopolitan territory. The Achaean commanders, perceiving the presence of the Aetolians, handled the situation so badly that they left no room for greater folly.

For having turned back from Cleitor, they encamped near Caphyae.

When the Aetolians made their march from Methydrium past the city of Orchomenus, the Achaean leaders led their troops out into the plain of Caphyae and drew them up there, using as a protective barrier the river that flows through it.

But the Aetolians — both because of the difficult ground in between (for in front of the river there were also several ditches hard to cross) and because of the apparent readiness of the Achaeans for battle — lost their nerve for attacking the enemy according to their original intention,

and instead made their march in good order toward the passes leading up to Olygyrtus, content if no one would attack them and force them to risk an engagement.

But Aratus and his officers — when the Aetolian vanguard was already beginning its ascent toward the passes, while the cavalry were bringing up the rear across the plain and approaching the place called Propus at the foot of the hills — sent out the cavalry and the light-armed troops, putting Epistratus the Acarnanian in command, with orders to fasten upon the enemy's rearguard and harass them.

And yet, if an engagement had to be risked at all, they ought not to have come to grips with the rear, when the enemy had already crossed the level ground, but with the vanguard, the moment it had pushed forward into the plain.

For in that way the whole battle would have taken place on flat, level ground, where the Aetolians happened to be at their most ineffective, owing to their equipment and their whole order of arms, while the Achaeans were at their most effective and powerful, for the opposite reasons just stated.

As it was, abandoning the ground and circumstances suited to themselves, they descended into conditions that favored the enemy. The outcome, accordingly,

matched their plans. For as the light-armed troops fastened upon them, the Aetolian cavalry kept their formation and withdrew toward the lower slopes, eager to join up with their own infantry.

But Aratus and his men, neither seeing clearly what was happening nor reckoning properly what would result from it, the moment they saw the cavalry retiring, supposed that they were in flight,

and so sent out the heavy-armed troops from the wings, ordering them to go to the aid of their own light-armed men and link up with them, while they themselves wheeled the army into column and led it forward at a run and in haste.

The Aetolian cavalry, meanwhile, having crossed the plain, as soon as they joined their infantry drew back and halted under cover of the lower slopes,

while they massed their infantry on the flanks and called them up, the men readily running back at the shouting and constantly reinforcing them as they arrived from the line of march.

And when they judged that they were now a match for the enemy in numbers, they closed up and charged the Achaean cavalry and light troops fighting at the front. Being more numerous, and making their assault from higher ground, they fought for a long time, and finally routed those drawn up against them.

As these men gave way and fled, the heavy-armed troops coming to their aid, arriving in disorder along the line of march and scattered — some at a loss over what was happening, others colliding head-on with the fleeing men during the withdrawal — were forced to turn back and do the same.

The result was that those actually defeated in the engagement numbered no more than five hundred, while those in flight numbered more than two thousand.

And since the affair itself taught the Aetolians what they had to do, they followed close behind, employing their shouting in an overbearing and relentless way.

As the Achaeans made their withdrawal toward their heavy infantry, on the assumption that these were standing fast in safety in their original formation, the flight at first was orderly and free from danger.

But when they saw that these troops too had abandoned the safety of their position and were strung out and broken up on the march, some of them at once dispersed and made their retreat in disorder to the neighboring towns,

while others, colliding head-on with the phalangites advancing toward them, had no need of the enemy at all, but threw themselves into a panic and forced one another into headlong flight. They directed their flight, during the withdrawal,

as I said before, toward the towns; for Orchomenus and Caphyae, being close by, were the salvation of many. Had this not been so, they would all have been in danger of being destroyed in an unexpected disaster.
