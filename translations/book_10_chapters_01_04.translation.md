# Book 10 Chapter 1: The Hannibalian War — The Recovery of Tarentum

The distance from the strait and the city of Rhegium to Tarentum is more than two thousand stades, and as a result this whole side of Italy has no harbor at all except those at Tarentum.

It faces the Sicilian Sea, leans toward the regions opposite Greece, and contains both the most populous non-Greek peoples and the most distinguished Greek cities.

For the Bruttians, the Lucanians, and parts of the Daunians live in this quarter of Italy, as do the Calabrians and many other peoples besides.

Likewise among the Greek cities, Rhegium, Caulonia, Locri, Croton, Metapontum, and Thurii occupy this coast.

So anyone sailing either from Sicily or from Greece to any one of the places just named is necessarily driven to put in at the harbors of Tarentum, and it is in that city that exchanges and commercial business are carried on with all who inhabit this side of Italy.

One may judge the convenience of the place from the prosperity once enjoyed by the people of Croton. Though they had only summer anchorages and a very limited place for landing, they seem to have acquired great wealth for no other reason than the natural advantage of their position—yet it is not even worth comparing that with the harbors and site of Tarentum.

Tarentum was also well placed in relation to the harbors of the Adriatic, and formerly it was even more so.

For from the Iapygian promontory as far as Sipontum, everyone who crossed over from the opposite shore and made landfall in Italy used to make Tarentum the crossing-point and used that city as a trading center for exchange and transfer of goods.

At that time Brundisium had not yet been founded.

That is why Fabius, attaching great importance to the enterprise, set all other matters aside and concentrated his planning on this one region.

# Book 10 Chapter 2: Publius Cornelius Scipio Africanus in Spain, B.C. 210-206. His Character

Since I am about to relate the deeds performed by Publius in Iberia, and indeed the whole series of actions he accomplished in his life, I think it necessary first to prepare my readers by explaining the man's character and natural bent.

For because he became almost the most illustrious man of all his predecessors, everyone wants to know what sort of man he was and what kind of nature or training enabled him to carry through so many and such great achievements.

Yet they are forced into ignorance and false opinions because those who tell his story have trifled with the truth.

That what I now say is sound will become clear from my own account to anyone able to notice what was finest and boldest in his actions.

All the other writers present him as some favorite of fortune, a man who for the most part succeeded unexpectedly and by pure chance in the designs he undertook.

They imagine such men to be more divine and more wonderful than those who act rationally in each case, not understanding that of the two, the one is merely fortunate while the other deserves praise.

Good luck may belong even to ordinary men, but what deserves praise belongs only to men of sound judgment and intelligence; and those are the men we ought to regard as most godlike and most beloved by the gods.

To my mind Publius had a nature and purpose very like Lycurgus, the lawgiver of the Spartans.

For we should not suppose that Lycurgus organized the Spartan constitution out of superstition and by paying attention to every oracle from Delphi, any more than we should think that Publius won so great an empire for his country by relying on dreams and omens.

Rather, both men saw that most people neither readily accept what is extraordinary nor dare to face danger without some hope derived from the gods.

So Lycurgus continually attached the authority of the Pythia to his own plans and thereby made his ideas more acceptable and more trustworthy.

Publius acted in much the same way, always creating among the common people the impression that his plans were formed under some divine inspiration, and by this he made those under his command bolder and readier to face the dangers of action.

But that he did everything with calculation and foresight, and that the outcomes of his enterprises always came about according to reason, will be clear from what follows.

# Book 10 Chapter 3: Scipio's First Brilliant Success

That he was generous and high-minded is generally admitted; but that he was keen in intelligence, self-possessed, and wholly intent in mind upon the object before him, no one would readily grant except those who lived with him and saw his character in full daylight, so to speak.

One of these was Gaius Laelius, who from youth to the end of his life shared in all his actions and conversations. It was he who gave me this view of Publius, because what he said seemed both likely in itself and consistent with the man's actual deeds.

He said that Publius' first notable exploit came when his father fought the cavalry engagement against Hannibal near the river called the Padus.

At that time, apparently, he was in his eighteenth year and was serving his first campaign in the field. His father had assigned him a troop of picked horsemen for protection; but when in the crisis of the battle he saw his father surrounded by the enemy with only two or three cavalrymen beside him and dangerously wounded, he first tried to urge those with him to go help his father.

When they shrank back in fear because of the number of enemies massed around him, he himself, it seems, charged recklessly and boldly into the men encircling his father.

Then, once the others too were compelled to charge in, the enemy, struck with panic, opened their ranks, and Publius the elder, saved beyond all expectation, was the first to hail his son as his savior in the hearing of all.

After winning an acknowledged reputation for courage through this exploit, he henceforth always gave himself without reserve to personal danger whenever his country placed the hopes of all upon him. That is the mark not of a commander trusting to luck, but of one with a mind fit to lead.

# Book 10 Chapter 4: His Election To the Aedileship

After this, when he had an elder brother, Lucius, and Lucius was standing for the aedileship—which among the Romans is considered almost the most distinguished office open to a young man—

and when it was customary for two patricians to be chosen, while on this occasion several candidates were seeking the office, Publius for a long time did not dare to stand for the same magistracy as his brother.

But as the election drew near, judging from the temper of the crowd that his brother would not easily gain the office, and seeing that the people felt great goodwill toward himself, he concluded that Lucius could achieve his aim only if the two of them made their bid together in full agreement. So he devised the following plan.

He saw his mother going about the temples and sacrificing to the gods for his brother, full of expectation about the outcome; and she was the only parent whose feelings mattered to him at the time—

for his father had by then sailed to Iberia after being appointed general for the operations already described. So he told his mother that he had now seen the same dream twice.

In the dream, he said, he and his brother had both been elected aediles and were coming up from the Forum toward their house, when she met them at the doorway and embraced and kissed them.

She at once reacted as women often do and cried out, "If only I might live to see that day!"

"Mother," he said, "shall we put it to the test?" When she agreed, supposing that he would never really dare but was only joking in the moment—for he was still very young—he asked her at once to prepare for him a splendid toga, since that is the customary dress for candidates seeking office.
