# Book 34 Chapter 1: Chapter 1

Those historians who, in the course of a general history, set out separately the geography of the continents did as Ephorus and Polybius did.

Polybius says that, on Greek matters, Eudoxus gave a good account, but Ephorus the best account of city foundations, kinships, migrations, and founders.

“But we,” he says, “will describe the world as it now is, dealing with the position of places and their distances from one another.”

For that is the subject most properly belonging to geography.

And yet, Polybius, by introducing sweeping judgments about distances not only in lands outside Greece but also within Greece itself, you make yourself answerable in some cases to Posidonius, in others to Artemidorus, and in others to many more besides.

Polybius the historian also composed a book entitled *On the Habitation under the Equator*. This region lies in the middle of the torrid zone.

He says that these places are inhabited and that their climate is more temperate than that of the regions lying at the edges of the torrid zone. Some of his evidence comes from reports by men who had observed those inhabited regions and confirmed what was seen there; other arguments he draws by reasoning from the natural motion of the sun.

For the sun lingers a long time around the tropic circles, both as it approaches them and as it moves away, so that to perception it remains near the tropics for almost forty days.

For this reason, since it lingers over the regions lying beneath the tropics, those regions must be scorched and become uninhabitable because of the intensity of the heat.

But from the equatorial circle its departure is rapid. It is therefore reasonable that the regions lying under the equator should have a milder climate, since the sun does not linger at the point overhead but quickly moves away from it.

For all who live between the tropic circles stand in the same relation to the sun’s passage overhead; but it remains longer over those who live near the tropics.

For that reason, the regions beneath the equator, though they lie in the middle of the torrid zone, are in fact more temperate than the settlements near the outer edges of that zone, which lie under the tropic circles. Polybius makes six zones in all,

two beneath the arctic circles, two between those and the tropics, and two between those and the equator.

But Polybius is not right in this, when he marks out zones by the arctic circles, making two under them and two between them and the tropics.

If, as Eratosthenes says, the region beneath the equator is temperate—as Polybius agrees, adding also that it is the highest region, and therefore receives abundant rainfall because the northern clouds driven by the etesian winds strike the heights there—then it would be much better to make this a narrow third temperate zone than to introduce zones beneath the tropics.

Posidonius objects to Polybius on the ground that he says the equatorial region is the highest.

And after him there was great disagreement about the number of the zones. Some made them six, as Polybius and Posidonius did, by dividing the torrid zone into two.

# Book 34 Chapter 2: Chapter 2

To build an empty tale of marvels on no basis of truth at all is not Homer’s way.

For, as one would expect, falsehood is more persuasive when it includes some elements of truth.

That is what Polybius also says when he undertakes to explain the wanderings of Odysseus.

And Polybius is right in the way he interprets those wanderings.

He says that Aeolus, because he indicated the routes for sailing out through the regions of the strait—where the currents run in both directions and departure is difficult because of the tides—came to be called the keeper of the winds and was thought of as a king,

just as Danaus, who pointed out the reservoirs in Argos, and Atreus, who recognized the sun’s course as opposite to that of the heavens, came to be represented as seers and priest-kings.

So too the priests of the Egyptians, the Chaldaeans, and the Magi, because they surpassed other men in wisdom, received authority and honor among earlier generations.

In the same way each of the gods is honored as the discoverer of some useful thing.

Starting from this principle, Polybius does not allow Aeolus to be treated as a merely mythical figure, nor Odysseus’ entire wandering as pure fiction. Rather, he says that a small amount of myth has been added, just as it has been added to the story of the Trojan War,

but that the main setting of the whole story is Sicily, both in the poet and in the other writers who describe the local features of Italy and Sicily.

Nor does he approve Eratosthenes’ remark that one will discover where Odysseus wandered when one finds the cobbler who stitched up the bag of the winds.

He also says that Homer’s words fit what happens at Scyllaeum and the hunting of the great fish there, where Scylla herself is described as

raging around the rock,

fishing for dolphins and sea-dogs and whatever larger creature she may catch.

For tuna, swimming in schools along the coast of Italy, whenever they are driven off course and prevented from reaching Sicily, fall in with larger animals such as dolphins, sharks, and other sea-creatures,

and from feeding on these the hunters called galeotes grow fat—fish which, he says, are also called swordfish or sea-dogs.

The same thing happens during the flooding of the Nile and of other waters, just as it does in a fire when woodland is burning: the animals gather together in flight from fire or flood and become prey for the stronger creatures.

# Book 34 Chapter 3: Chapter 3

After saying this, he describes the hunting of the galeotes, which takes place around Scyllaeum.

A single lookout is stationed over many small two-oared boats lying in wait, with two men in each boat.

One man rows, while the other stands on the prow holding a spear whenever the lookout signals the appearance of a swordfish, for the creature travels with a third of its body above the water.

When the boat comes alongside, the spearman strikes at close range and then pulls the shaft back out of the body, leaving only the harpoon-head in the fish.

For the point is barbed and is deliberately fitted loosely to the shaft, and a long line is attached to it. They let this line run with the wounded fish until it is exhausted from its struggling and attempts to escape.

Then they drag it ashore or lift it into the boat, if its body is not too large.

And even if the shaft falls into the sea, it is not lost. It is made from oak and fir together, so that when the oak end sinks by its weight, the rest remains afloat and easy to recover.

Sometimes it even happens that the rower is wounded straight through the little boat because of the great size of the fish’s sword and the boar-like violence of the creature in the hunt.

From such facts, he says, one may infer that Homer placed Odysseus’ wandering around Sicily, because he connected Scylla with this kind of fishing, which is especially characteristic of Scyllaeum,

and because what is said about Charybdis resembles the actual behavior of the strait.

But the line “for three times she spews it up” instead of “twice” is either a copyist’s error or a geographical mistake.

And Meninx too agrees with what is said about the Lotus-Eaters.

# Book 34 Chapter 4: Chapter 4

If some point does not agree with reality, one should explain it by changes over time, by ignorance, or by poetic license, which is composed of history, vivid presentation, and myth.

The aim of history is truth, as when in the Catalogue of Ships the poet mentions the features proper to each place, calling one city rocky, another farthest out, another rich in doves, and another beside the sea.

The aim of vivid presentation is immediacy, as when he introduces men fighting; the aim of myth is pleasure and wonder.

But to invent everything is neither convincing nor Homeric. For everyone regards his poetry as a work of thought, not as Eratosthenes says when he tells us not to judge poems by their meaning or to look for history in them.

It is more plausible to take the line, “From there for nine days I was carried by destructive winds,” to refer to a short distance—for destructive winds do not keep a straight course—than to send Odysseus out into the outer ocean as though steady fair winds were blowing all the while.

And if, he says, we reckon the distance from Malea to the Pillars as 22,500 stades, and suppose that this was covered at an even rate in nine days, the voyage would come to 2,500 stades each day.

Who, then, has ever recorded that someone sailed from Lycia or Rhodes to Alexandria—a distance of 4,000 stades—and arrived on the second day?

And to those who ask how Odysseus could have come to Sicily three times and yet never once sailed through the strait, he replies that later sailors also all avoided that passage. Such is his argument.
