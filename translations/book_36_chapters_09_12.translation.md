# Book 36 Chapter 9: Chapter 9

In matters concerning the Carthaginians, after the Romans had defeated them, and in matters concerning the so-called Pseudo-Philip in Greece, there were many and varied opinions; at first these opinions concerned the Carthaginians, and afterward again the Pseudo-Philip.

The judgments and discussions about the Carthaginians were in fact divided and uncertain.

Some approved of the Romans, saying that they had acted wisely and practically in securing their supremacy.

For, they argued, it was sound policy for men who think far ahead to remove the danger hanging over them and to make their rule secure for their own country by taking back a city that had often disputed control with them and might still, if circumstances allowed, dispute it again.

Others opposed this view, saying that the Romans had not set out with that intention to acquire supremacy, but had gradually slipped into a lust for power like that of the Athenians and Spartans, moving more slowly than those states but arriving at the same end through the appearances they displayed.

For, they said, in the past they had always made war openly and continued until they had prevailed and forced their opponents to admit that they must obey Rome and do what was ordered.

But now, they argued, they had first shown their real purpose by their handling of Perseus and by uprooting the kingdom of the Macedonians from its foundations, and had now completed that same design in the case of the Carthaginians.

For since nothing irreparable had happened on account of them, they had resolved in a harsh and irreversible way against them, willing to accept and endure anything in order to carry out what had been ordered.

Others again said that the Roman nation was in general a statesmanlike people, and that this was one of their distinguishing traits and something of which the Romans rightly prided themselves: namely, that they fought their wars in a straightforward and honorable way, not using night attacks or ambushes, and rejecting everything that came about by deceit or trickery, claiming for themselves only those dangers that were open and face-to-face.

But now, they said, they had managed everything concerning the Carthaginians by deceit and trickery, advancing little by little with one proposal and then another concealment, until they had taken away from the allies every hope of helping them.

This was more fitting, they said, for a monarchic style of action than for a political and Roman one, and it came close, by right reason, to impiety and breach of faith. Some men, however, argued against this too.

For if the Romans had handled things this way before the Carthaginians had surrendered themselves and placed their country under Roman control, gradually revealing one demand and concealing another, they would reasonably have seemed guilty of the charges brought against them.

But since the Carthaginians had themselves surrendered, so that the Romans might decide whatever seemed best about them, and since the Romans then, within that authority, issued and ordered what they thought fit, the act could no longer be called close to impiety or even, scarcely, to breach of faith; and some said it was not injustice at all.

For there are three classes of wrong into which every accusation naturally falls, and the Romans' conduct did not fit any of them.

For impiety is wrongdoing against the gods, parents, or the dead; breach of faith is acting contrary to sworn and written agreements;

and injustice is what is carried out contrary to law and custom. But the Romans were guilty of none of these things in the present case: they had not sinned against the gods, the parents, or the dead; nor had they violated oaths or agreements — on the contrary, they accused the Carthaginians of violating them.

Nor did they overthrow laws or customs or private trust: for when the Carthaginians had handed themselves over, the Romans had simply done what they wished, since those surrendering were no longer obeying orders, and had thus brought necessity upon them.

# Book 36 Chapter 10: Chapter 10

So much, then, was said about the Romans and the Carthaginians.

As for the Pseudo-Philip, at first the story seemed hardly believable: a sort of airborne Philip had appeared in Macedonia, contemptuous not only of the Macedonians but also of the Romans, and having no reasonable basis at all for his claim.

For the real Philip, as everyone knew, had died in Alba in Italy, about eighteen years old, two years after Perseus.

But three or four months later, when a report arrived that he had defeated the Macedonians in battle beyond the Strymon in the district of the Odomantians, some accepted the report, though most still disbelieved it.

Not long afterward another report arrived, saying that he was defeating the Macedonians in battle on this side of the Strymon and had control of all Macedonia; and when letters and envoys came from the Thessalians to the Achaeans asking for help because danger threatened them too, the event seemed astonishing and incredible.

For there was no appearance of probability or any reasonable explanation for what had happened. Such, then, were the feelings about these matters.

# Book 36 Chapter 11: Chapter 11

When letters from Manilius reached the Achaeans in the Peloponnese, informing them that they would be doing well to send Polybius of Megalopolis at once to Lilybaeum, since he was needed for public business, the Achaeans decided to send him in accordance with the consul's instructions.

But we thought it right, in many ways, to obey the Romans, and so set aside all other concerns and sailed off when summer was beginning.

When we reached Corcyra and found there letters from the consuls that had arrived for the Corcyraeans, in which they explained that the Carthaginians had already handed over their hostages to them and were entirely ready to obey their orders,

we thought that the war was over and that we no longer had any need at all, and so we sailed back again to the Peloponnese.

# Book 36 Chapter 12: Chapter 12

One should not be surprised if at times we refer to ourselves by name, and at other times by more general expressions, as when we say, "after I had said this"

or again, "when we had agreed." For since we are so deeply involved in the events that are later to be narrated, it is necessary to use references to ourselves, so that we do not keep repeating our name and become tedious through tautology, nor again say "I" and "for my part" at every turn and fall into an irritating manner.

Rather, by using all such forms and changing among them as the occasion requires, we should avoid, as far as possible, the heavy and burdensome habit of speaking too much about ourselves.

For such speech is naturally unwelcome, though often necessary when there is no other way to make the matter clear.

And indeed, as it happened, we were helped in this respect by a kind of fortunate accident: up to our own time no one, so far as we know, had inherited precisely the same name as ours.
