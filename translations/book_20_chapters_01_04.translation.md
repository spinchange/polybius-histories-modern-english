# Book 20 Chapter 3: Epirus and Elis Ask Antiochus for Help

While Antiochus was staying at Chalcis and winter was beginning, envoys came to him: from the Epirote nation, the embassy headed by Charops, and from the city of the Eleans, the embassy headed by Callistratus.

The Epirotes urged him not to drag them prematurely into the war against the Romans, since he could see that they lay directly in the path facing Italy ahead of all the rest of Greece.

If, however, he could himself take up a forward position to defend Epirus and secure their safety, they said they would receive him into both their cities and their harbors;

but if he judged it best not to do this for the present, they asked him to pardon them for being afraid of a war with the Romans.

The Eleans, for their part, urged him to send help to their city, since, now that the Achaeans had voted for war, they were wary of an attack from them.

The king answered the Epirotes that he would send envoys to discuss with them what was in their common interest,

while to the Eleans he dispatched a thousand foot soldiers, putting Euphanes the Cretan in command.

# Book 20 Chapter 4: The Decline of Boeotia

The Boeotians had for a long time now been in a poor condition, one that stood in stark contrast to the former vigor and renown of their state.

For these people, having won great glory and great power in the days of the battle of Leuctra, somehow—I cannot say how—steadily kept losing both of the qualities just mentioned in the years that followed, under the leadership of Abaeocritus.

And from that time on they not only kept losing them, but, turning altogether to the opposite course, they obscured their earlier reputation as far as they were able.

For when the Achaeans had stirred them up to war against the Aetolians, they adopted the same policy as the Achaeans and formed an alliance with them, and thereafter waged continual war against the Aetolians.

But when the Aetolians invaded Boeotia with an army, the Boeotians marched out in full force, and—although the Achaeans had assembled and were about to come to their aid—they did not wait for their arrival but engaged the Aetolians.

Being defeated in the battle, they were so crushed in spirit that, from that campaign on, they no longer dared to lay claim to any honorable distinction at all, nor did they share with the other Greeks in any common undertaking or contest decided by joint resolution.

Instead, having given themselves over to feasting and drunkenness, they grew slack not only in their bodies but in their souls as well.
