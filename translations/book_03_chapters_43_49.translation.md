# Book 3 Chapter 43: Hannibal's Troops Begin to Cross

When the fifth night came, the detachment that had crossed over earlier set out from the far bank at dawn and advanced along the river itself against the barbarians on the opposite side.

Hannibal, meanwhile, with his soldiers ready, pressed on with the crossing. He had filled the larger boats with the cavalry who carried light shields, and the dugout canoes with his most agile infantry.

The larger boats took the upstream position, nearest the current, while the lighter ferry-craft were stationed below them, so that the boats would absorb the main force of the current and the canoes could be carried across the channel more safely.

They planned to tow the horses swimming behind the sterns of the boats, three or four at once, with a single man on each side of the stern steering them by leading-reins, so that a considerable number of horses could be brought across all together in this very first crossing.

When the barbarians saw what the enemy was undertaking, they poured out of their stockade in scattered groups and without order, convinced that they could easily prevent the Carthaginians from landing.

But the moment Hannibal saw that his own men on the far side were now drawing near—signaling their presence by smoke, as had been arranged—he ordered everyone to embark at once, and the men in charge of the ferry-craft to push hard against the current.

This was quickly done. The men in the boats raced against one another with shouting and struggled against the force of the river,

while both armies stood on either bank along the edges of the river: his own men sharing in the struggle and following along with shouts of encouragement, and the barbarians facing them singing their war-chant and challenging them to the fight. The whole scene was striking and full of tension.

At this moment, with the barbarians having left their tents, the Carthaginians from across the river fell upon them suddenly and unexpectedly: some of them set fire to the camp, while the greater number rushed against those who were guarding the crossing.

The barbarians, taken aback by this turn of events, split apart—some hurried back toward the tents to defend them, while others fought back and battled against their attackers.

Hannibal, with everything falling out just as he had intended, immediately rallied the first men coming ashore, encouraged them, and joined battle with the barbarians.

The Celts, thrown into disorder and caught off guard by what was happening, were quickly routed and took to flight.

# Book 3 Chapter 44: Hannibal Addresses His Men

Now master both of the river-crossing and of the enemy, the Carthaginian general first turned at once to bringing over the men who had been left on the far bank.

Having ferried all his forces across in a short time, he encamped that night beside the river itself.

On the next day, hearing that the Roman fleet had anchored near the mouths of the river, he picked out five hundred of his Numidian cavalry and sent them off to find out where the enemy were, how many they were, and what they were doing.

At the same time he also selected the men suited to manage the crossing of the elephants.

He himself then gathered his forces and brought forward the petty kings of Magilus's company—for these men had come to him from the plains around the Padus—and through an interpreter he made clear to the troops the decisions these chiefs had reached.

Of what was said, the points that did most to give the men confidence were, first, the plain fact that these envoys were present, drawing them on and promising to share in the war against Rome;

second, the trustworthiness of their pledge that they would guide the army through country where, lacking none of the necessities, they could make the march into Italy both quickly and safely;

and besides this, the richness and great extent of the land they would reach, and also the eagerness of the men alongside whom they were going to wage their battles against the forces of Rome.

So the Celts, having spoken to this effect, withdrew.

After them Hannibal himself came forward. First he reminded the troops of what they had already accomplished: in those exploits, he said, they had undertaken many bold and dangerous feats and had failed in none, so long as they had followed his judgment and counsel.

Next he urged them to keep their courage, seeing that the greatest part of the work was now done, since they had mastered the crossing of the river and had seen with their own eyes the goodwill and eagerness of their allies.

For this reason, he said, they ought not to worry about the details, which were his concern; rather, by obeying his orders, they should prove themselves brave men, worthy of their past deeds.

When the men responded with approval, showing great drive and eagerness, he praised them, prayed to the gods on behalf of them all, and dismissed them, ordering them to look after themselves and to make their preparations quickly, since the march would begin the next day.

# Book 3 Chapter 45: Skirmish between Reconnoitring Parties

When the assembly had broken up, the Numidians who had been sent ahead to reconnoiter came back, having lost most of their number, while the rest had fled headlong.

For not far from their own camp they had clashed with the Roman cavalry, who had been sent out by Publius on the same task, and both sides showed such fierce determination in the engagement that the Romans and Celts lost about a hundred and forty horsemen, and the Numidians more than two hundred.

After this, the Romans, in their pursuit, came close to the Carthaginian stockade; and once they had reconnoitered it, they turned back and hurried off to report the enemy's presence to their general. On reaching their camp, they made their report.

Publius at once loaded his baggage onto the ships, broke camp with his whole army, and advanced along the river, eager to come to grips with the enemy.

Hannibal, on the day right after the assembly, at first light stationed all his cavalry toward the sea, holding them in reserve, and set his infantry force in motion out of the stockade to begin the march.

He himself waited behind for the elephants and the men who had been left with them.

# Book 3 Chapter 46: The Crossing of the Elephants

The way they got the animals across was something like this. They fastened together a number of rafts, firmly built, and lashing two of these tightly to each other, they anchored both into the bank at the point where one entered the river, the pair together having a width of about fifty feet.

To these they joined others, attaching them on the outer side and extending the structure of the linked rafts out into the channel.

The side facing the current they secured with cables running to the land, tying them to the trees growing along the bank, so that the whole structure would hold together and not be pushed downstream.

When they had made the entire projecting bridge of rafts about two hundred feet long, they attached to the outermost rafts two more that had been built especially large. These last were bound tightly to each other, but were connected to the rest in such a way that their fastenings could be easily cut. Several towlines were attached to them,

by which the boats were to keep them from being carried downstream and, holding them firmly against the current, were to tow them across and ferry the animals over on them.

After this they piled a great deal of earth onto all the rafts, until by heaping it up they had made the surface match—level and the same color as the path leading over dry land down to the crossing.

The animals were used to obeying their Indian drivers right up to the water, but would no longer dare to step into it at all. So they led them along the earthen causeway, putting two females in front, whom the other animals followed obediently.

Once the elephants had been brought onto the outermost rafts, the men cut the fastenings by which these were attached to the rest, hauled in the towlines with the boats, and quickly drew the animals and the rafts beneath them away from the causeway.

When this happened, the animals were thrown into panic and at first kept turning around and rushing in every direction; but, hemmed in on all sides by the current, they grew afraid and were forced to stay where they were.

In just this way, as a pair of rafts was repeatedly attached to the others, most of the animals were carried across on them.

A few, however, in the middle of the channel flung themselves into the river out of fear. Of these, their Indian drivers all perished, but the elephants survived.

For by the strength and great length of their trunks, lifting them above the water and breathing through them, while at the same time blowing out any water that got in, they held out, making most of their way across upright along the riverbed.

# Book 3 Chapter 47: Previous Histories of this March either False or Inconsistent

Once the animals had been ferried across, Hannibal took the elephants and the cavalry and advanced, leading them as a rearguard along the river, away from the sea and toward the east, making his march as though for the interior of Europe.

The Rhone has its sources above the head of the Adriatic Gulf, on the slopes of the Alps that incline westward toward the north; it flows toward the winter sunset and empties into the Sardinian Sea.

For much of its course it runs through a deep valley, to the north of which dwell the Ardyes, a Celtic people, while its entire southern side is bounded by the northern foothills of the Alps. The plains around the Padus,

about which I have already spoken at length, are separated from the valley of the Rhone by the high ridges of those same mountains, which take their beginning near Massalia and extend all the way to the head of the whole Adriatic.

It was these ridges that Hannibal now crossed, leaving the region of the Rhone, to descend into Italy.

Some of those who have written about this crossing of the Alps, in their wish to astonish their readers with marvelous tales about the country I have described, fall without realizing it into the two faults most foreign to any history: for they are driven both to tell falsehoods and to write things that contradict themselves.

On the one hand they present Hannibal as an unrivaled general in daring and foresight, yet at the same time they plainly show him to be utterly without reason;

and on the other, being unable to reach any conclusion or find any way out of their falsehoods, they drag gods and sons of gods into what is meant to be factual history.

For having assumed that the strongholds and ruggedness of the Alpine mountains are such that not only horses and armies, and with them elephants, but not even lightly equipped foot-soldiers could easily get through; and likewise having sketched for us a wilderness in those regions so complete that, unless some god or hero had met Hannibal's men and shown them the roads, they would all have lost their way and perished—by these assumptions they admittedly fall into both of the faults I have mentioned.

# Book 3 Chapter 48: Absurd Premises of Other Historians

These are the errors into which they fall. For, in the first place, what general could be shown to be more devoid of reason than Hannibal, what commander more inept,

if, leading forces so large and resting his greatest hopes of overall success on them, he knew—as these writers claim—neither the roads nor the country, nor where in the world he was marching, nor against whom,

and, in the end, not even whether his undertaking was at all feasible?

What men who have utterly failed and are at their wits' end in every way will not bring themselves to do—namely, to plunge with an army into country they have not scouted—this is just what these authors attribute to Hannibal, who held the greatest hopes for his enterprise undiminished.

In the same way, what they say about the desolation, and likewise about the ruggedness and difficulty of the country, makes their falsehood plain.

For they failed to inquire into the fact that the Celts living along the Rhone river had, not once or twice before Hannibal's arrival—and not in the distant past but quite recently—crossed the Alps with large armies, drawn up against the Romans and fought alongside the Celts who inhabit the plains around the Padus, just as I have shown in the earlier parts of this work;

and besides this, they did not know that a very large population happens to live in the Alps themselves. Ignorant of every one of these facts, they say that some hero appeared and helped point out the roads to them.

By this they naturally fall into the same predicament as the writers of tragedy. For with all of them too the resolutions of their plots require a god and a stage-machine, because they take their initial premises to be false and improbable;

and the historians must suffer and do the like, bringing on heroes and gods appearing on the scene, once they have laid down beginnings that are implausible and false. For how is it possible to set a reasonable end upon unreasonable beginnings?

Hannibal, at any rate, did not act as these writers describe; rather, he handled his plans in these matters in a very practical way.

For he had clearly investigated both the excellence of the country into which he intended to descend and the hostility of its peoples toward the Romans; and for the difficult stretches in between he made use of local guides and leaders who were going to share in the same hopes as himself.

I speak about these things with confidence, because I have inquired about the events from the very men who were present at the time, and have inspected the places and made the journey through the Alps myself, for the sake of knowledge and of seeing them with my own eyes.

# Book 3 Chapter 49: Scipio Is Too Late To Stop Hannibal

Publius, however, the Roman general, arrived at the river-crossing three days after the Carthaginians had broken camp, and finding that the enemy had already set off, he was utterly astonished,

since he had been convinced that they would never dare to take this route into Italy, on account of the numbers and the faithlessness of the barbarians who inhabited the region.

But seeing that they had in fact dared to do so, he hurried back to his ships and, on reaching them, began embarking his forces.

He sent his brother off to manage affairs in Iberia, while he himself turned back and sailed for Italy, eager to outpace the enemy by way of Etruria and reach the crossing of the Alps before them.

Hannibal, having marched for four straight days from the crossing, came to the place called the Island, a district full of people and rich in grain, which takes its name from this very feature.

For the Rhone on one side, and the river called the Isara on the other, flow along each of its flanks and bring its shape to a point where they meet.

It is similar in size and shape to what is called the Delta in Egypt, except that in that case the sea closes off one side and joins the mouths of the rivers, whereas in this case the closing side is formed by mountains hard to approach, hard to enter, and—one might almost say—wholly inaccessible.

On arriving in this district, Hannibal found two brothers quarreling over the kingship and confronting each other with their armies.

When the elder appealed to him and called on him to help win and secure the throne for him, *** he agreed, since the advantage this would bring him for the present was almost self-evident.

And so, joining in the attack and helping to drive out the other brother, he received much support from the victor:

for not only did this prince generously supply his army with grain and other provisions, but he also replaced all the old and worn weapons, refitting the whole force at just the right moment,

and besides this, by furnishing most of the men with clothing and footwear, he proved of great usefulness for the crossing of the mountains.

Most important of all, when they were anxious about the march through the Gauls called the Allobroges, he brought up the rear with his own force and secured them a safe passage until they neared the crossing of the Alps.
