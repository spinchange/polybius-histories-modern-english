# Book 3 Chapter 43: Hannibal's Troops Begin to Cross

When the fifth night had come, the detachment that had crossed earlier set out from the far bank at dawn and advanced along the river itself against the barbarians on the opposite side.

Hannibal, meanwhile, with his soldiers ready, was poised to begin the crossing. He had loaded the larger boats with the shield-bearing cavalry and the dugout canoes with the most agile of his infantry.

The larger boats took up position upstream and against the current, while the lighter craft were stationed below them, so that, with the larger boats absorbing most of the force of the stream, the passage across the channel would be safer for the canoes.

They planned to tow the horses swimming behind the sterns of the boats, with one man on each side of the stern steering three or four at once by their lead-ropes, so that a sufficient number of horses could be ferried across together with the very first crossing.

The barbarians, watching the enemy's maneuver, poured out of their stockade in disorder and scattered groups, convinced that they would easily prevent the Carthaginians from landing.

But as soon as Hannibal saw that his own men on the far side were now drawing near—they had signaled their arrival with smoke, as had been arranged—he gave the order for everyone to embark at once, and for those manning the craft to push hard against the current.

This was done quickly, and the men in the boats raced one another with loud shouts, struggling against the force of the river.

The two armies stood drawn up along the banks of the river on either side: Hannibal's own men sharing in the struggle and following its course with shouts, while the barbarians facing them chanted their war cries and dared them to the fight. The whole scene was astonishing and charged with suspense.

At this moment, when the barbarians had abandoned their tents, the Carthaginians from the far side fell upon them suddenly and unexpectedly. Some of them set fire to the encampment, while the greater number rushed against those who were guarding the crossing.

The barbarians, finding the situation utterly unexpected, split: some hurried to the tents to defend them, while others fought back and grappled with the attackers.

Hannibal, with events falling out just as he had planned, immediately formed up the first men as they landed, rallied them with encouragement, and engaged the barbarians.

The Celts, both because of their disorder and because of the unexpected turn of events, were quickly routed and broke into flight.

# Book 3 Chapter 44: Hannibal Addresses His Men

The Carthaginian commander, having now mastered both the crossing and his opponents, at once turned to ferrying over the men still left on the far bank.

When he had brought all his forces across in a short time, he made camp for that night beside the river itself.

The next day, hearing that the Roman fleet had anchored off the mouths of the river, he picked out five hundred Numidian cavalry and sent them off to discover where the enemy were, how many they numbered, and what they were doing.

At the same time he also detailed suitable men to handle the crossing of the elephants.

Then he himself gathered his forces and brought forward the chieftains in Magilus's company—for these had come to him from the plains around the Padus (Po)—and through an interpreter explained to the troops the decisions these men had reached.

Of what was said, the strongest grounds for confidence among the rank and file were, first, the plain fact of the presence of these envoys, who were urging them on and pledging to share in the war against Rome;

second, the trustworthiness of their promise that they would guide the army through such country that, without lacking any of their necessities, they would make their march into Italy both swiftly and safely;

and besides this, the richness and the vast size of the land they were going to reach, and furthermore the eagerness of the men alongside whom they were about to wage their battles against the Roman forces.

So, having spoken to this effect, the Celts withdrew.

After them Hannibal himself came forward and first reminded the troops of their past achievements: in these, he said, though they had undertaken many bold actions and dangers, they had failed in none, so long as they had followed his judgment and counsel.

Next he urged them to be of good courage, seeing that the greatest part of their task had been accomplished, since they had now mastered the crossing of the river and had seen with their own eyes the goodwill and eagerness of their allies.

For this reason he told them not to trouble themselves about the details, as those were his concern, but to obey his commands and so prove themselves brave men, worthy of their past deeds.

When the men responded with approval and showed great drive and enthusiasm, he praised them, prayed to the gods on behalf of them all, and dismissed them, ordering them to look after themselves and to make their preparations with all speed, since the march would begin the next day.

# Book 3 Chapter 45: Skirmish between Reconnoitring Parties

When the assembly had broken up, the Numidians who had been sent ahead to reconnoiter returned, having lost most of their number, while the rest had fled headlong.

For not far from their own camp they had collided with the Roman cavalry that Publius had dispatched on the same errand, and both sides showed such fierce determination in the clash that of the Romans and Celts about a hundred and forty horsemen were killed, and of the Numidians more than two hundred.

After this, the Romans, having pressed close to the Carthaginian stockade in their pursuit and reconnoitered it, turned back and hurried away to report the enemy's presence to their commander; and when they reached their own camp, they delivered their report.

Publius at once loaded his baggage onto the ships, set out with his whole army, and advanced along the river, eager to engage the enemy.

Hannibal, on the day following the assembly, stationed all his cavalry at first light facing toward the sea, holding them in reserve, and moved his infantry force out of the stockade onto the march.

He himself waited behind for the elephants and the men who had been left with them.
