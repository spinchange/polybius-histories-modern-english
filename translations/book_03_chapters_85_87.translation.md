# Book 3 Chapter 85: Roman Reaction

So that affair was brought to its conclusion. Hannibal, once the men who had surrendered under terms had been brought back to him, along with the rest of the prisoners as well, gathered them all together — more than fifteen thousand of them.

He began by making clear that Maharbal had no authority to grant the men who had surrendered their safety without his own consent. After this he delivered an indictment against the Romans.

When he had finished with that, he distributed all the captives who were Romans among the units of his army to be held under guard, but he released all the allies, without ransom, to return to their own homes,

repeating the same declaration he had made before: that he had come to wage war not against the Italians but against the Romans, on behalf of the freedom of the Italians.

He then let his own forces recover their strength, and he buried the most distinguished of the dead from his own army, about thirty in number; for in all about fifteen hundred had fallen, most of whom were Celts.

Having done this, he began to deliberate with his brother and his friends about where and how he ought to press his advance, since he was now confident about the whole campaign.

When news of the disaster that had occurred reached Rome, the leaders of the state were unable to disguise or play down what had happened, because of the sheer scale of the calamity, and they were forced to tell the people what had taken place, calling the citizens together into an assembly.

And so, the moment the magistrate announced to the crowds from the Rostra, "We have lost a great battle," such a panic broke out that, to those who had been present on both occasions, the event then seemed far greater than at the actual moment of the battle itself.

And this was natural enough. For having had no experience, for many years, either of the word or of the fact of an acknowledged defeat, they bore the reversal with no moderation and no composure.

The Senate, however, did not do so: it held to the reasoning that the situation demanded, and deliberated about the future — how, and what, each group ought to do.

# Book 3 Chapter 86: A Second Disaster in Etruria

At the time of the battle, Gnaeus Servilius, the consul stationed forward in the region of Ariminum —

this lies on the side of Italy facing the Adriatic, where the plains of Gaul join the rest of Italy, not far from where the mouths of the Po empty into the sea —

having heard that Hannibal had invaded Etruria and was encamped opposite Flaminius, intended to join all his own legions to him. But, unable to do so because of the weight of his army, he hurriedly sent Gaius Centenius ahead with four thousand cavalry, wishing them to arrive in advance of his own coming, should circumstances require it.

Hannibal, when the enemy's relief force was reported to him after the battle, sent out Maharbal with the spearmen and a portion of the cavalry.

These troops fell upon Gaius's men and, in the very first engagement, destroyed nearly half of them, and after driving the rest together onto a certain hill, on the following day took them all prisoner.

In Rome, with the report of the battle now three days old, and the suffering throughout the city at that very moment, so to speak, at its most inflamed, when this further reversal also came on top of it, not only the populace but the Senate itself was thrown into dismay.

For this reason they set aside the annual conduct of public business and the election of magistrates, and undertook to deliberate more seriously about the present situation, judging that affairs and the surrounding circumstances called for a commander with absolute authority.

Hannibal, now thoroughly confident about the whole campaign, decided against approaching Rome for the present, and instead advanced through the countryside, plundering it without resistance, making his march toward the Adriatic.

Passing through the country called Umbria and that of Picenum, he reached the Adriatic region on the tenth day,

having taken so much plunder that the army could neither drive nor carry off the spoils, and having killed a great many people along the way;

for, just as in the storming of cities, on this occasion too an order had been given to kill any grown men who fell into their hands. This he did because of the

innate hatred toward the Romans that was rooted in him. During this period, encamping beside the Adriatic in a country surpassingly rich in every kind of produce, he took great pains over the recovery and care of his men, and no less of his horses.

For since their winter quarters in the region of Gaul had been spent in the open, and what with the cold and the lack of oil for grooming, and on top of that the subsequent march and hardship through the marshes, nearly all the horses, and likewise the men, had developed what is called scurvy and a wretched condition of that sort.

So, having gained control of a prosperous country, he put the horses back into good condition, and restored the bodies and the spirits of his soldiers. He also re-equipped the Libyans in the Roman fashion with select weapons, since he had become master of so much captured gear.

At this time he also sent men by sea to report to Carthage about what had happened; for this was the first time he had reached the sea since he made his invasion into Italy.

On hearing this, the Carthaginians rejoiced greatly, and gave much energy and forethought to supporting in every possible way both their cause in Italy and their cause in Iberia.

The Romans appointed as dictator Quintus Fabius, a man outstanding in judgment and of fine natural endowment. Indeed, even down to our own day the members of this household are called the Maximi — that is, "the Greatest" — on account of that man's successes and achievements.

The dictator differs from the consuls in this respect: each of the consuls is attended by twelve axes,

but this officer by twenty-four; and whereas the consuls in many matters require the Senate's cooperation to carry out their plans, this man is a commander with absolute power, upon whose appointment all the magistracies in Rome are immediately dissolved, except the tribunes of the people.

We shall, however, give a more precise account of these matters elsewhere. Together with the dictator they appointed Marcus Minucius as master of the horse. This officer is placed under the commander-in-chief, but becomes, as it were, his deputy in the holder's absences on other business.
