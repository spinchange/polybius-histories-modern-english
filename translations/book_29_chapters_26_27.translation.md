# Book 29 Chapter 26: Antiochus Renews the War

Forgetting what he himself had written and said, Antiochus resumed the war against Ptolemy.

So true are Simonides' words: "It is hard to be good."

For it is easy enough to have impulses toward what is noble, and even to hold to them for a while; but to remain steady through every trial, and never to put anything above honor and justice, is difficult indeed.

# Book 29 Chapter 27: Popilius Makes Antiochus Stop the War

When Antiochus had come up with the intention of seizing Pelusium from Ptolemy,

the Roman commander Popilius met him. The king greeted him from a distance by voice and extended his right hand; but Popilius, having the tablet ready at hand on which the Senate's decree was written, held it out to him and ordered Antiochus to read it first,

as I think, because he did not consider it right to exchange the sign of friendship before he knew the attitude of the man receiving him — whether he was to be a friend or an enemy.

When the king had read it and said that he wished to consult his friends about the matter, Popilius did something that seemed harsh and entirely overbearing:

with the vine-rod he happened to have in his hand, he drew a circle around Antiochus and ordered him to give his answer about the contents of the letter before stepping outside that ring.

The king, startled by what had happened and by the display of authority, hesitated briefly and then said that he would do everything the Romans asked. Then Popilius and his companions took his hand and all greeted him warmly.

The order contained in the letter was that the war with Ptolemy should be ended at once.

So a fixed number of days was granted him, and within that time he withdrew his forces into Syria, in pain and anger, though at present submitting to the situation.

Popilius and his companions then set matters in order at Alexandria and urged the kings to live in peace with one another; at the same time they ordered them to send Polyaratus back to Rome.

Then they sailed off to Cyprus, intending also to drive the forces stationed there out of the island without delay.

When they arrived and found that Ptolemy's generals had been defeated in battle and that the situation in Cyprus in general was in confusion, they quickly had the army withdraw from the country and remained there until the forces had sailed off to Syria.

In this way the Romans preserved Ptolemy's kingdom, which was all but ruined.

Thus Fortune so shaped the affairs of Perseus and the Macedonians that the outcome in Alexandria and in the whole of Egypt was decided by it: that is, by the earlier decision of what should be done with Perseus.

For if that had not taken place and been settled, I do not think Antiochus would have obeyed the orders given him.
