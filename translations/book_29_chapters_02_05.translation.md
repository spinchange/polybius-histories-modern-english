# Book 29 Chapter 2: Gaius Popilius Laenas Sent to Alexandria

Because the senate learned that Antiochus had become master of Egypt and had come close to taking Alexandria as well,

and because it judged that the growth of this king's power touched its own interests, it appointed as envoys Gaius Popilius and his colleagues,

to put an end to the war and, more generally, to inspect the state of affairs.

Such, then, was the situation in Italy.

# Book 29 Chapter 3: Genthius Forms an Alliance with Perseus

II. War against Perseus.

When Hippias and his companions, whom Perseus had sent as envoys to Gentius about the alliance, arrived before winter,

and explained that the king was ready to undertake war against the Romans if he were given three hundred talents and the proper guarantees for the whole agreement,

Perseus, when he heard this and judged Gentius' cooperation necessary, chose Pantauchus, one of his chief friends,

and sent him off with instructions first to settle the matter of the money and to exchange sworn pledges for the alliance, then to take whatever hostages from Gentius Pantauchus thought fit and send them on, and to receive from Perseus whatever hostages Gentius should specify in the written terms, and besides this to arrange the transport of the three hundred talents.

Pantauchus at once set out and, arriving at Meteon in the land of the Labeatae, met Gentius there and quickly brought the young king round to sharing Perseus' hopes.

When the oaths for the alliance had been sworn and put into writing, Gentius immediately sent the hostages whom Pantauchus had specified, and with them Olympion to receive from Perseus the oaths and the hostages, while others were to take charge of the money.

Along with this, Pantauchus persuaded Gentius also to send envoys of his own, who, together with those being sent by Perseus, would go to Rhodes to negotiate a common alliance.

For if that were accomplished, and the Rhodians too entered the war, he declared that the Romans would be altogether easy to defeat.

Gentius, persuaded by these appeals, appointed Parmenion and Marcus and sent them out with instructions that, once they had received the oaths and hostages from Perseus and agreement had been reached about the money, they were to go on embassy to Rhodes.

# Book 29 Chapter 4: Genthius Joins Perseus

So all these men proceeded to Macedonia. But Pantauchus stayed at Gentius' side and kept reminding and pressing the young king not to delay his preparations, but, while ready, to seize positions, cities, and allies in advance; above all he urged him to prepare for a struggle at sea.

For since the Romans were completely unprepared in this sphere, both on the coasts of Epirus and in Illyria, everything proposed would be accomplished without a fight by Gentius himself and the forces he sent out.

So Gentius, persuaded by these arguments, busied himself with preparations both by land and by sea.

Perseus, meanwhile, when the envoys and hostages from Gentius arrived in Macedonia, set out from the camp by the river Elpeius with all his cavalry and went to meet them at Dion,

and when he met them he first gave the oaths for the alliance in the presence of all the cavalry. For he greatly wished the Macedonians to know clearly of Gentius' cooperation, hoping that with this additional advantage they would be more confident.

Next he received the hostages and handed over his own to Olympion and his companions. The most distinguished among them were Limnaeus son of Polemocrates and Balacrus son of Pantauchus.

After this he sent those who had come for the money on to Pella to receive it there, while he dispatched the envoys bound for Rhodes to Thessalonica, to Metrodorus, instructing them to hold themselves ready for the voyage. That embassy also succeeded in persuading the Rhodians to join the war.

After arranging these matters, he sent Herophon as envoy to Eumenes, a man whom he had employed on such missions before, and Telemnastus the Cretan to Antiochus,

telling him not to overlook the opportunity and not to suppose that Roman arrogance and harshness concerned Perseus alone,

but to understand clearly that unless he too now took a hand, preferably by ending the war, but if not by helping Perseus, he would soon experience the same fortune himself.

# Book 29 Chapter 5: Difficulty of Explaining the Intrigues of Perseus and Eumenes

In dealing with this matter I was at a loss what I ought to do. For to write in detail and with exact precision about such negotiations as the kings carried on secretly with one another seemed open to criticism and altogether unsafe,

while on the other hand to pass over completely what appeared to have been the most decisive element in this war, and the thing by which many of the later difficulties found their causes made clear, seemed to me a sign of sheer idleness and total lack of courage.

And so I was led to write briefly what seemed likely to be true, and the probabilities and indications by which I came to this judgment, since I was present at the time and was more struck than most people by each of the events that took place.
