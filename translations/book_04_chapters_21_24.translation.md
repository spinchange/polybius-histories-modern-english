# Book 4 Chapter 21: Why the Ancient Arcadians Turned to Music

It seems to me that the men of old introduced these practices not for the sake of luxury or abundance, but because they observed, on the one hand, that everyone worked the land with their own hands and that their way of life was, in a word, toilsome and harsh; and on the other hand, they saw the severity of character that goes with such a life because of the coldness and gloom that prevail throughout most of the region—conditions to which all of us human beings are naturally bound to conform.

For it is by this cause, and no other, that, in our distinctions by nation and by broad geographical separation, we differ so widely from one another in character, in physical form, and in coloring, and likewise in most of our customary pursuits.

Wishing, then, to soften and temper the willfulness and harshness of their nature, they introduced all the things mentioned above, and in addition established the custom of common assemblies and frequent sacrifices shared alike by men and women, and also choral dances of girls together with boys.

In short, they devised every means, eager to tame and gentle the unyielding quality of the soul through the cultivation of these habits.

The people of Cynaetha neglected these things entirely—and that although they needed such help more than anyone, since they hold by far the harshest air and territory in all Arcadia—and instead gave themselves over to their mutual feuds and rivalries.

In the end they grew so savage that no Greek city has ever been the scene of greater or more continual acts of impiety.

Here is a sign both of the Cynaethans' misfortune in this regard and of the displeasure of the other Arcadians toward such conduct:

at the time when the Cynaethans, after committing their great massacre, sent an embassy to the Spartans, every other Arcadian city they entered on the road immediately ordered them out by herald;

and the Mantineans, after the envoys had departed, even performed a purification, carrying sacrificial victims around the city and the whole of their territory.

Let this much, then, be said by me so that the common character of the Arcadians may not be slandered on account of a single city, and likewise so that no one among the inhabitants of Arcadia may suppose that the cultivation of music is carried so far among them merely for the sake of indulgence and so be tempted to neglect this branch of education;

and also for the sake of the Cynaethans themselves, that, if ever god grants them better fortune, they may humanize themselves by turning to education, and above all to music; for only in this way could they ever be rid of the savagery that then prevailed among them.

Now that we have set out what came to mind concerning the Cynaethans, let us return once more to our digression.

# Book 4 Chapter 22: Philip V. In the Peloponnese

So the Aetolians, having done such things throughout the Peloponnese, returned home safely.

Philip, coming with an army to the aid of the Achaeans, arrived at Corinth. Finding that he had come too late, he sent dispatch-bearers to all the allies, urging each of them to send delegates with all speed to Corinth to deliberate about the common interest.

He himself broke camp and advanced toward Tegea, having learned that the Spartans had fallen into bloodshed and turmoil against one another.

For the Spartans, being accustomed to being ruled by a king and to obeying their leaders in all things, had recently been set free through Antigonus; and now, with no king among them, they were in a state of faction with one another, since they all assumed they had an equal share in the government.

At first, two of the ephors kept their views concealed, while the other three made common cause with the Aetolians, convinced that because of his youth Philip would not yet be able to deal effectively with affairs in the Peloponnese.

But when, contrary to their expectation, the Aetolians made a swift withdrawal from the Peloponnese, and Philip arrived from Macedonia even more swiftly,

the three distrusted one of the other two, Adeimantus, because he was privy to all their schemes but did not very much approve of what was being done; and they were anxious that, once the king drew near, he would reveal everything that was going on to Philip.

For this reason, after conferring with some of the young men, they issued a proclamation that all those of military age should go in arms to the sanctuary of Athena of the Brazen House, on the pretext that the Macedonians were advancing on the city.

When, owing to the suddenness of it, a crowd had quickly gathered, Adeimantus, displeased at what was happening, came forward and tried to exhort and instruct them, saying:

"These proclamations and these musters under arms ought to have been ordered earlier, at the time when we were hearing that the Aetolians, who were our enemies, were drawing near the borders of our land—not now, when we learn that the Macedonians, our benefactors and saviors, are approaching with their king."

But while he was still pressing this argument, the young men who had been suborned fell upon him and stabbed him to death, and with him Sthenelaus, Alcamenes, Thyestes, Bionidas, and a good many other citizens.

Having done this, the ephors now in control of affairs at once sent men to denounce to Philip those who had been killed and to urge him to hold back his approach until the city's affairs, after the disturbance that had occurred, had returned to their settled state—and meanwhile to be assured that it was their fixed intention to maintain toward the Macedonians everything that was just and friendly.

These men, meeting the king when he was already near Mount Parthenium, addressed him in accordance with their instructions.

When he had heard them out, he urged the envoys to make their return home with all speed and to inform the ephors that he would continue his march without a break and make his camp at Tegea, and that he thought they ought, as quickly as possible, to send him men of sufficient standing to confer with him about the present situation.

When those who had met him had carried out his command, the leaders of the Spartans, after hearing the king's message,

sent ten men to Philip. These men went to Tegea and entered the king's council; with Omias at their head, they accused the party of Adeimantus of being responsible for the disturbance,

and promised that they themselves would do everything for Philip that pertained to the alliance, and would show themselves second to none in goodwill toward him among those reckoned to be his true friends.

So the Spartans, having spoken these things and others like them, withdrew, and the members of the council differed with one another in their opinions.

Some, knowing the malice of those in Sparta and convinced both that the party of Adeimantus had perished because of their loyalty to the Macedonians and that the Spartans had set their minds on making common cause with the Aetolians, advised Philip to make an example of the Spartans, treating them in the same way that Alexander had treated the Thebans immediately after taking power.

friends. The king then decided the whole matter—if one ought to speak of these views as the king's: for it is not likely that a boy of seventeen could reach clear judgments about matters of such magnitude.

It is, however, proper for us who write history to attribute the opinions that decided the deliberations to those in supreme command; yet the readers themselves should bear in mind that such proposals and decisions probably came from his associates, and especially from those closest to him.

Of these, one would most fairly assign to Aratus the opinion then expressed by the king.

For Philip said that, as for the wrongs the allies committed privately against one another, it fell to him to correct and mark these only by word of mouth and by letter;

but that matters affecting the alliance as a whole were the only ones that called for joint attention and correction by all.

Since the Spartans had committed no manifest offense against the common alliance, and were promising to do everything that was just toward us, it was not right to deliberate any irrevocable measure concerning them;

for it would be absurd that his father, having conquered them when they were enemies, had done nothing harsh, while he himself, for so small a cause, should decide on some irreparable action against them.

This opinion having been ratified—that the incident should be overlooked—the king at once sent Petraeus, one of his own friends, together with the party of Omias, to urge the people to hold fast to their goodwill toward himself and the Macedonians, and at the same time to give and receive the oaths concerning the alliance.

He himself broke camp with his army and advanced once more toward Corinth, having set before the allies, in his decision regarding the Spartans, a fine example of his own policy.
