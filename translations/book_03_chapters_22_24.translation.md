# Book 3 Chapter 22: Treaties between Rome and Carthage

The first treaty between Rome and Carthage was concluded in the consulship of Lucius Junius Brutus and Marcus Horatius, the first consuls appointed after the abolition of the monarchy—the same men under whom the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol was consecrated.

This was twenty-eight years before Xerxes crossed over into Greece.

I have translated the treaty and set it down below as accurately as I possibly could. For so great is the difference between the Roman language of today and that of antiquity that even the most learned men can scarcely make out certain points, and only after careful study.

The terms of the treaty run roughly as follows: "On these conditions there shall be friendship between the Romans and the allies of the Romans, and the Carthaginians and the allies of the Carthaginians:

The Romans and their allies shall not sail beyond the Fair Promontory, unless they are forced to by storm

or by enemies. If anyone is driven beyond it by necessity, he shall not be permitted to buy or take anything except what is needed for the repair of his ship or for

[7;8]

sacrifices, and within five days he must depart. Those who come to trade shall pay no duty,

except for the fee to the herald or the clerk. Whatever is sold in their presence shall be guaranteed to the seller by the public faith—that is, whatever is sold either in Libya or in Sardinia.

If any Roman comes to the part of Sicily that the Carthaginians govern, he shall have rights equal in all respects

to the Carthaginians. The Carthaginians shall do no wrong to the people of Ardea, Antium, Laurentum, Circeii, or Tarracina, nor to any other of the Latins

who are subject to Rome. From those cities that are not subject to Rome they shall keep their hands; and if they take one,

they shall hand it back to the Romans undamaged. They shall build no fortress in Latium. If they enter the territory in arms, they shall not spend a night within that territory."

# Book 3 Chapter 23: Division of Territory

The Fair Promontory is the headland lying just in front of Carthage itself, toward the north.

The Carthaginians hold that the Romans must under no circumstances sail in warships beyond this point toward the south, because—as it seems to me—they did not want them to become acquainted either with the region around Byzacium or with that around the Lesser Syrtis, the places they call Emporia, on account of the richness of the land.

But if anyone is driven there by force of storm or of enemies and needs some of the necessities for sacrifices or for the repair of his ship, they allow him to take these and nothing beyond them, and those who have put in to anchor must of necessity depart within five days.

To Carthage, and to all the part of Libya lying on the near side of the Fair Promontory, and to Sardinia and to the part of Sicily the Carthaginians govern, the Romans are permitted to sail for trade, and the Carthaginians guarantee by the public faith that justice will be secured.

From the terms of this treaty it is clear that they speak of Sardinia and Libya as their own exclusive possessions; but in the case of Sicily they expressly stipulate the opposite, making the treaty apply only to those parts of Sicily that fall under Carthaginian dominion.

Likewise the Romans make the treaty concerning Latium alone, and make no mention of the rest of Italy, because it did not fall under their authority.

# Book 3 Chapter 24: Treaties Between Rome and Carthage

After these, they made a further treaty, in which the Carthaginians included the Tyrians and the people of Utica as well.

To the Fair Promontory they added Mastia of Tarseium, beyond which they hold that the Romans must neither raid nor found a city.

Its terms run roughly as follows: "On these conditions there shall be friendship between the Romans and the allies of the Romans, and the Carthaginians, the Tyrians, and the people of Utica and their

allies. Beyond the Fair Promontory and Mastia of Tarseium the Romans shall not raid,

nor trade, nor found a city. If the Carthaginians take some city in Latium that is not subject to the Romans, they may keep the goods and the men,

but shall give back the city. If any of the Carthaginians capture men belonging to a people with whom the Romans have a peace in writing, but who are not subject to the Romans, they shall not bring them into the harbors of the Romans; and if such a captive is brought in and a Roman lays a hand on him,

he shall be set free. The Romans shall do likewise.

If a Roman takes water or provisions from any land the Carthaginians govern, he shall not, while supplied with these provisions, do wrong to any

with whom the Carthaginians are at peace and friendship.

A Carthaginian shall do likewise. If one does otherwise, he shall not take redress on his own authority; and if anyone does so,

the wrong shall be treated as a public offense. In Sardinia and Libya no Roman shall trade or found a city—*** unless only to take on provisions

or to refit a ship. If a storm drives him in, he shall depart within five days. In the part of Sicily the Carthaginians govern, and in Carthage, a Roman may do and sell everything that is permitted

to a citizen. A Carthaginian shall do likewise in Rome."

Once again in this treaty they tighten their hold on Libya and Sardinia, claiming them as their own and shutting off every landing-place to the Romans;

but in the case of Sicily they specify the opposite, with respect to the part subject to them.

So too the Romans, in regard to Latium, hold that the Carthaginians must do no wrong to the people of Ardea, Antium, Circeii, or Tarracina. These are the cities that enclose, along the sea, the Latin territory with which the treaty is concerned.
