# Book 12 Chapter 9: Timaeus's Statement of Method

Let us now examine Timaeus's own method as well, and set side by side for comparison the statements he has made about this same colony, so that we may learn which of the two better deserves such censure. In the same book, then, he says—

no longer using the same kind of merely probable reasoning in his refutations, but claiming that he himself had genuinely gone in person to the Locrians in Greece to investigate the matters concerning the colony.

These Locrians, he says, first showed him written agreements, still preserved even today, made with the original settlers who had been sent out—agreements at the head of which the relationship was set down in these terms: "as parents to their children."

Besides these, there were decrees granting mutual rights of citizenship between the two peoples. And in general, when they heard Aristotle's account of the colony, they were astonished at the writer's effrontery.

Then, crossing over again to the Locrians in Italy, he says he found that their laws and customs likewise accorded not with the unscrupulous ways of household slaves but with a colony of free men:

for among them, certainly, penalties have been laid down against kidnappers, just as against adulterers and runaway slaves—none of which would be the case if they were aware of being themselves descended from such people.

# Book 12 Chapter 10: The Investigations of Timaeus

Now the first difficulty one might raise is this: to which of the Locrians did he go, and whom did he question, about these matters?

For if it had been the case that the Locrians in Greece, like those in Italy, all lived in a single city, then perhaps there would be no need for puzzlement, and the matter would be easy to settle.

But since there are two peoples of Locrians, to which of them did he go? And to which cities of the one or the other? And in whose keeping did he find the agreements on record? For none of this is made clear to us.

And yet that this is a specialty of Timaeus's—that it is in this above all that he has set himself in rivalry with other writers, and that this is broadly the very ground of his reputation *** —I mean his parade of precision in chronology and in records, and his care for that department of research—this, I think, we all recognize.

It is therefore worth wondering how he failed to make clear to us the name of the city in whose keeping he found the document, the place in which the agreement happens to be inscribed, or the magistrates who showed him the record and with whom he held his conversation—so that no one might have any ground for doubt at all, but that, with the place and city fixed, those who disputed the point might be able to ascertain the truth.

By passing over all this, he plainly shows that he is conscious of having lied on purpose. For that Timaeus, had he laid hold of such evidence, would have let none of it slip but would have clung to it, as the saying goes, with both hands—this is evident from the following.

For he is the man who rested his case, by name, on the authority of Echecrates, with whom he says he held his conversation about the Locrians in Italy, and from whom he says he learned these things;

and who took the further trouble, so as not to appear to have heard them from some chance informant, of adding that this man's father had earlier been thought worthy of an embassy by Dionysius.

Would such a man, then, surely, had he laid hold of a public record or an ancestrally transmitted inscribed pillar—

# Book 12 Chapter 11: Timaeus's Use of Chronological Records

—have passed it over in silence? For this is the very man who draws up his comparative tables, tracing the line of the ephors back to its origins and setting them against the kings at Lacedaemon and the archons at Athens, and matching the priestesses at Argos against the Olympic victors, and who convicts the cities of error in these records when they show a discrepancy of as little as three months—this is he.

And indeed it is Timaeus who hunted out the inscribed pillars in the rear chambers of the temples and the records of guest-friendship on the doorposts of the shrines.

He is a man whom we cannot believe either to have been ignorant of any such thing that existed, or, having found it, to have left it out; nor can pardon be granted him on any grounds if he has lied:

for, having been a harsh and relentless censurer of his neighbors, he may reasonably expect to meet with relentless accusation from his neighbors in return. Nevertheless, having been plainly convicted of falsehood in these matters,

he crosses over to the Locrians in Italy and first says that the constitution and the other humane institutions are shared by both Locrian peoples *** and that Aristotle and Theophrastus have lied against the city.

Now I am well aware that here too I shall be compelled to digress from my proper task, in laying down and affirming the truth about these matters;

yet it was for this very reason that I held over my discussion of Timaeus to a single section, so that I might not again and again be forced to neglect more pressing business.—

That Timaeus says the greatest fault in history is falsehood: hence he also advises those whom he may convict of false statements in their writings to seek some other name for their books, and to call them anything whatever rather than history.—

# Book 12 Chapter 12: Timaeus On Divination

For just as with a carpenter's rule—even if it be shorter in length or narrower in width, yet so long as it possesses the essential property of a rule, he says it must still be called a rule, but when it falls away from straightness and from kinship with the straight line, it must be called anything rather than a rule—

in just the same way, he says, with written works: however much they may go astray in their diction, or in their handling, or in any other of their particular parts, yet if they hold fast to the truth, the books may lay claim to the name of history; but when they fall away from truth, they ought no longer to be called history.

Now, that truth must be regarded as the guiding principle of such works, I myself agree—and somewhere in my own work I have said as much, putting it thus: that just as, when the eyes are removed from a living body, the whole is rendered useless, so, if you take away truth from history, what is left of it becomes a worthless tale.—

We said, however, that there are two kinds of falsehood:

one arising from ignorance, the other from deliberate intention;

and that, of these, those who stray from the truth through ignorance deserve pardon, while toward those who do so by deliberate choice one should be unforgiving.—

These points being agreed between us,

I hold that in this very matter of falsehood there is a great difference between what comes about through ignorance and what through deliberate intention—the former admitting of pardon and kindly correction, the latter justly meeting with unforgiving condemnation;

and it is to this latter kind, above all, that one would find Timaeus himself liable. That he is indeed such a man, it is now possible to consider.—
