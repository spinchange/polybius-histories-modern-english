# Book 3 Chapter 25: The Third Treaty

Furthermore, the Romans concluded yet another treaty, the last of them, at the time of Pyrrhus's crossing, before the Carthaginians had embarked on the war over Sicily.

In it they preserve all the other provisions in accordance with the existing agreements, but the following clauses are added to them.

"If they make a written alliance with Pyrrhus, both parties shall make it on such terms that they remain free to come to each other's aid in the territory of whichever is at war.

Whichever of the two has need of the assistance, the Carthaginians shall furnish the ships both for the voyage out and for the return, but each side shall provide the pay for its own men.

The Carthaginians shall also aid the Romans by sea, if there is need; but no one shall compel the crews to disembark against their will."

The oath that had to be sworn was as follows.

In the case of the first treaties, the Carthaginians swore by the gods of their ancestors and the Romans by Jupiter the Stone, in accordance with a certain ancient custom; but in the case of these later treaties, by Mars and Enyalius.

The swearing by Jupiter the Stone went like this: the man taking the oath over the treaty takes a stone in his hand, and once he has sworn in the name of the public faith, he says these words:

"If I keep my oath, may good things be mine; but if I think or do otherwise, then, while all others are kept safe in their own homelands, under their own laws, in possession of their own livelihoods, their sacred things, and their tombs, may I alone be cast out, just as this stone is now."

And having said this, he throws the stone from his hand.

# Book 3 Chapter 26: Treaties Between Rome and Carthage

Since such treaties exist, and are still preserved even now, engraved on bronze tablets beside the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus in the treasury of the aediles,

who would not reasonably feel astonishment at the historian Philinus? Not because he was ignorant of these documents—that is no wonder, since even in our own day the oldest men among both the Romans and the Carthaginians, and those reputed to take the greatest interest in public affairs, were unaware of them—

but rather, how or on what grounds did he have the boldness to write the very opposite of these facts: namely, that there existed treaties between the Romans and the Carthaginians by which the Romans were bound to keep away from the whole of Sicily

and the Carthaginians from the whole of Italy, and that the Romans violated the treaties and their oaths when they made their first crossing into Sicily—when in fact no such written document was ever drawn up or ever existed at all.

For he states this in his second book in explicit terms. I mentioned these matters in the introductory part of my own work, but I deferred treating them in detail until the present occasion, because a good many people too have been led away from the truth in this matter by trusting Philinus's account.

Nevertheless, if anyone finds fault with the Romans on this point regarding the crossing into Sicily—because they took the Mamertines into their friendship at all, and then, when these men asked for it, came to their aid, men who had broken faith not only with the city of the Messenians but also with that of the Rhegians—he might with reason appear to be displeased.

But if anyone supposes that they made the crossing in violation of their oaths and treaties, he is plainly mistaken.

# Book 3 Chapter 27: The Fourth Treaty

When the war over Sicily had been brought to an end, then, they made another treaty, in which the essential points of the written terms were these:

"The Carthaginians shall withdraw from the whole of Sicily and from all the islands

lying between Italy and Sicily. Security shall be guaranteed by each side to the allies of the other.

Neither side shall impose any orders, nor erect any public buildings, nor recruit mercenaries, nor take into alliance the allies of the other, within the other's domains.

The Carthaginians shall pay out two thousand two hundred talents within ten years, and shall hand over a thousand at once.

The Carthaginians shall return all their prisoners to the Romans without ransom."

After this, again,

when the Libyan (Mercenary) war had ended, the Romans, having declared war on the Carthaginians to the point of a formal decree, made additional terms of the following kind:

"The Carthaginians shall withdraw from Sardinia and pay an additional twelve hundred talents," as we stated above.

In addition to the foregoing, the last of the agreements were the ones concluded with Hasdrubal in Iberia, to the effect that "the Carthaginians shall not cross the river Ebro

with arms." These were the legal obligations between the Romans and the Carthaginians from the beginning down to the times of Hannibal.

# Book 3 Chapter 28: No Reasonable Pretext for Rome to Claim Sardinia

Just as, then, we find that the Romans' crossing into Sicily was not made in violation of their oaths, so too, in the case of the second war—the one in connection with which they made the treaty concerning Sardinia—one could find neither a reasonable pretext nor a just cause,

but rather it is generally agreed that the Carthaginians, contrary to all justice, were forced by their circumstances to withdraw from Sardinia and to pay out the sum of money mentioned above.

For the charge brought by the Romans on this matter—that the Carthaginians had wronged Roman seafarers during the Libyan war—had already been settled at the time when, after recovering from the Carthaginians all those who had been carried off, the Romans in return, as a favor, gave back without ransom the prisoners they held.

I have set out these matters in detail in the book before this one.

Since this is how things stand, it remains to examine and determine carefully, concerning the war of Hannibal's time, to which of the two parties the responsibility for it is to be assigned.
