# Book 4 Chapter 49: Complaints of Prusias

Prusias had grievances against the Byzantines that went back some time. In the first place, he complained that, although they had voted to set up certain statues of him, they had never actually erected them, but instead let the matter drift into neglect and oblivion.

He was also displeased with them for throwing all their energy into reconciling Achaeus and Attalus and bringing their feud and war to an end, since he reckoned that a friendship between those two would, in many respects, work against his own interests.

He was further irritated by the fact that the Byzantines, as it appeared, had sent delegates to Attalus to take part in the sacrifices at the festival of Athena, but had sent no one to him for the Soteria.

And so, with his anger quietly smoldering on account of all these things, he gladly seized upon the opening the Rhodians offered him. Coming to terms with their ambassadors, he agreed that they should wage the war by sea, while he himself reckoned he would do the enemy no less harm by land.

Such, then, was the war of the Rhodians against the Byzantines.

# Book 4 Chapter 50: The War between Rhodes and Byzantium Begins

This was how it came about and how it began. At first the Byzantines fought the war vigorously, convinced that Achaeus would come to their aid, and that they themselves, by bringing Tiboetes over from Macedonia, would in turn confront Prusias with fears and dangers.

But Prusias, prosecuting the war with the determination already described, took from them the place called Hieron at the mouth of the strait,

which the Byzantines had purchased at great expense a little while before and made their own because of the convenient position of the spot, for they wished to leave no one any base from which to operate—neither against the merchants sailing into the Pontus, nor with respect to the slave trade and the fisheries of that very sea.

He also took from them the territory in Asia, in Mysia, which the Byzantines had held for many years already.

The Rhodians, for their part, manned six ships and, together with these, took on four more from their allies, and, having appointed Xenophantus as admiral, sailed for the Hellespont with ten ships in all.

With the rest they lay at anchor off Sestos and barred those sailing into the Pontus, while the admiral, putting out with a single ship, tested the Byzantines, to see whether they had by now had a change of heart, cowed by the war.

When they paid him no attention, he sailed off, took up the remaining ships, and withdrew with the whole fleet to Rhodes.

The Byzantines meanwhile sent to Achaeus, asking him to help, and dispatched men to Tiboetes to bring him from Macedonia,

for it was thought that the rule of Bithynia belonged no less to Tiboetes than to Prusias, since he was Prusias' uncle on his father's side.

But the Rhodians, observing the firm stand of the Byzantines, set about practically achieving their aim.

# Book 4 Chapter 51: Byzantium, Rhodes, and Prusias Treaties

For seeing that the mainstay of the Byzantines' endurance in the war lay in their hopes resting on Achaeus, and observing that Achaeus' father was being held at Alexandria while Achaeus himself set the highest store by his father's safety, they resolved to send an embassy to Ptolemy and to ask for the release of Andromachus.

They had made this request before only as a side matter, but now they pressed for it in earnest, so that by rendering Achaeus this favor they might put him under such an obligation to them that he would grant whatever they asked.

When the ambassadors arrived, Ptolemy at first considered keeping Andromachus back, in the hope of using him at an opportune moment, both because his own disputes with Antiochus remained unresolved and because Achaeus, having recently proclaimed himself king, now controlled a domain of considerable importance—

for Andromachus was the father of Achaeus and the brother of Laodice, the wife of Seleucus.

Nevertheless, since Ptolemy was inclined toward the Rhodians in his whole policy and was eager to oblige them in everything, he gave his consent and handed Andromachus over to them to convey to his son.

Having accomplished this, and having added some honors for Achaeus besides, they stripped the Byzantines of their most substantial hope.

And another awkward thing also befell the Byzantines: Tiboetes, while being brought down from Macedonia, wrecked their plans by departing this life.

When this happened, the spirits of the Byzantines collapsed, while Prusias, emboldened in his hopes for the war, at once pressed it on himself energetically from the Asian side, and at the same time hired Thracians who kept the Byzantines from passing through their gates on the European side.

So the Byzantines, cheated of their own hopes and hard pressed by the war on every side, began to look about for a way out—

# Book 4 Chapter 52: Cavarus, Gallic King, Negotiates Peace

—a respectable way out of their situation. And when Cavarus, the king of the Gauls, arrived at Byzantium and showed himself eager to put an end to the war, ambitiously extending his hands in mediation, both Prusias and the Byzantines agreed to his proposals.

When the Rhodians learned of Cavarus' eagerness and of Prusias' compliance, and being themselves anxious to bring their own design to completion,

they appointed Aridices as ambassador to the Byzantines and sent off along with him Polemocles in command of three triremes,

wishing, as the saying goes, to send the Byzantines both the spear and the herald's staff at once. On their arrival the settlements were concluded, in the year when Cothon, son of Calligeiton, was Hieromnemon at Byzantium.

With the Rhodians the terms were straightforward: the Byzantines were to levy no toll on those sailing into the Pontus, and, this being done, the Rhodians and their allies were to be at peace with the Byzantines.

With Prusias the terms were roughly these: that there should be peace and friendship between Prusias and the Byzantines for all time, and that neither the Byzantines should make war on Prusias in any way nor Prusias on the Byzantines;

that Prusias should restore to the Byzantines their lands, their forts, their populations, and their prisoners of war without ransom, and, in addition to these, the vessels captured at the start of the war and the missiles seized in the strongholds, and likewise the timber, the stonework, and the roof tiles from the place called Hieron—

for Prusias, in his anxiety over the return of Tiboetes, had demolished all the forts that seemed conveniently situated for any purpose—

and that Prusias should also compel those among the Bithynians who held any property taken from the territory of Mysia that was subject to the Byzantines to give it back to the farmers.
