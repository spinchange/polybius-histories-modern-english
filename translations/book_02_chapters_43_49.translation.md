# Book 2 Chapter 43: Margos, the First Sole Strategus

For the first twenty-five years, the cities I have named governed themselves jointly, choosing in rotation a common secretary and two generals.

After this they decided instead to appoint a single man and to entrust him with authority over the whole confederacy. The first to receive this honor was Margos of Caryneia.

In the fourth year after this man held the office, Aratus of Sicyon—then only twenty years old, having freed his native city from tyranny through his own courage and daring—brought it into the Achaean union, for from the very start he had been an enthusiast for their political ideals.

Then, in the eighth year after that, having been elected general for the second time, he laid a secret plot against the Acrocorinth, which Antigonus held, and seized it. By gaining control of it he relieved the inhabitants of the Peloponnese of a great fear, and by liberating the Corinthians he brought them into the Achaean union.

During the same term of office he also won over the city of Megara and attached it to the Achaeans.

These events took place in the year before the defeat of the Carthaginians, the defeat that made them withdraw entirely from Sicily and submit, for the first time, to paying tribute to Rome.

Having advanced his design so greatly in so short a time, Aratus thereafter continued as leader of the Achaean nation, directing all his plans and actions toward a single goal:

namely, to expel the Macedonians from the Peloponnese, to abolish the autocracies, and to secure for each people the common freedom their ancestors had enjoyed.

So long as Antigonus Gonatas lived, then, Aratus persisted in opposing both that king's meddling and the rapacity of the Aetolians,

handling each matter with practical skill—even though the two of them had advanced so far in injustice and reckless daring that they made a formal compact with each other for

the dismemberment of the Achaean nation. But when Antigonus died, the Achaeans came to terms and made an alliance with the Aetolians, who joined them honorably in the war against Demetrius; and so, for the time being, the estrangement and ill will were set aside, and a sense of partnership and friendship grew up between them.

When Demetrius, after a reign of only ten years, died at about the time of the Romans' first crossing into Illyria, events flowed favorably toward the Achaeans' original design.

For the autocrats in the Peloponnese fell into despair: Demetrius, who had been, as it were, their patron and paymaster, was dead; and Aratus was pressing hard upon them, insisting that they ought to lay down their tyrannies, holding out great gifts and honors to those who complied, while threatening those who resisted with still greater terrors and dangers at the hands of the Achaeans.

So they were driven to comply—to give up their tyrannies, to set their own native cities free, and to join the Achaean union.

Now Lydiades of Megalopolis, while Demetrius was still alive, had on his own initiative—shrewdly and prudently foreseeing what was to come—laid down his tyranny and joined the national confederacy.

And Aristomachus, tyrant of Argos, Xenon, tyrant of Hermione, and Cleonymus, tyrant of Phlius, now likewise gave up their autocracies and shared in the Achaean democracy.

# Book 2 Chapter 45: The Aetolians Envy the Achaeans

But as the nation grew and prospered all the more on this account, the Aetolians, prompted by their innate injustice and greed, grew envious—and, even more, hoped to carve up the cities among themselves, just as before they had divided the cities of Acarnania with Alexander and had schemed to divide those of the Achaeans with Antigonus Gonatas.

So now, buoyed up by similar hopes, they had the audacity to make common cause and join hands at once with both Antigonus—who at that time was head of the Macedonians, acting as guardian of the boy Philip—and Cleomenes, king of the Lacedaemonians.

For they saw that Antigonus held secure control of Macedonia, and that he was an acknowledged and open enemy of the Achaeans because of their plot that had captured the Acrocorinth.

They reckoned that if they brought in the Lacedaemonians as additional partners in the scheme and pushed them first into hostility against the nation, they could easily crush the Achaeans by attacking them at the right moment and surrounding them with war on every side.

And in all likelihood they would quickly have accomplished this, had they not overlooked the most crucial point in their plan: they failed to reckon that in their designs they would have Aratus as an opponent—a man capable of meeting every emergency.

And so, when they set out to meddle and to launch unjust attacks, far from achieving any of their aims, they did the opposite: they actually strengthened Aratus, then the leader, and the nation itself, since he skillfully drew them off and wrecked their plans.

How the whole affair was managed will become clear from what I am about to relate.

# Book 2 Chapter 46: The Intrigues of the Aetolians

For Aratus observed that the Aetolians were ashamed to take up war against the Achaeans openly, since the favors the Achaeans had done them in the Demetrian War were still very fresh,

but that they were conspiring with the Lacedaemonians and envied the Achaeans so deeply that, when Cleomenes had treacherously seized from them Tegea, Mantinea, and Orchomenus—cities that were not only Aetolian allies but at that time actually members of their own league—they were not the least indignant about it, but even confirmed his takeover;

and whereas in the past, out of greed, they had treated any pretext as sufficient grounds for going to war against people who had done them no wrong, now Aratus saw them willingly letting their treaties be violated and deliberately giving up their greatest cities for one purpose only—to see Cleomenes become a formidable adversary to the Achaeans.

Seeing all this, Aratus—and likewise all the leaders of the Achaean state—decided that they should not initiate war against anyone, but should resist the designs of the Lacedaemonians.

At first, then, they held to this view; but as time went on, when they saw Cleomenes boldly building the fort called the Athenaeum in the territory of Megalopolis and openly revealing himself as their bitter enemy,

then at last they convened the Achaeans and resolved, together with the council, to take up open hostility against the Lacedaemonians.

This, then, was how the war called the Cleomenic War began, and at about these

# Book 2 Chapter 47: The Cleomenic War

times. At first the Achaeans set out to face the Lacedaemonians with their own forces, partly because they thought it most honorable not to obtain their safety through others, but to preserve their cities and territory by themselves,

and partly because they wished to maintain their friendship with Ptolemy, on account of the benefits he had bestowed earlier, and not to appear to be reaching out to others for help.

But when the war had gone on for some time, and Cleomenes had abolished his country's ancestral constitution and converted its lawful kingship into a tyranny, and was waging the war with energy and reckless daring,

Aratus, foreseeing what was to come and fearing the desperate audacity of the Aetolians, decided that he must spoil their plan well in advance.

He saw that Antigonus was a man of action and intelligence who laid claim to a reputation for trustworthiness, while he knew perfectly well that kings by nature regard no one as either an ally or an enemy, but always measure their hatreds and friendships by the calculus of self-interest.

So he resolved to open communication with that king and to join hands with him, pointing out to him the outcome that his present circumstances would bring.

To do this openly, however, he judged inexpedient for several reasons. For one thing, it would prepare Cleomenes and the Aetolians as adversaries to the scheme;

for another, it would dishearten the mass of the Achaeans, since by taking refuge with their enemies he would seem to have utterly given up his hopes in them—which was the very last impression he wanted to create.

For this reason, holding to such a purpose, he planned to manage the matter in secret.

As a result, contrary to his own real intentions, he was often forced to say and do things in public through which, by presenting the opposite impression, he meant to conceal his actual management of affairs.

For this reason he did not even record some of these things in his memoirs.

# Book 2 Chapter 48: Aratus Involves Megalopolis

Aratus knew that the people of Megalopolis were suffering badly in the war, because, lying next to Sparta, they had to bear the brunt of the fighting ahead of the others, yet were not receiving the proper support from the Achaeans, since the Achaeans too were hard-pressed and crippled by their circumstances.

He also knew clearly that they were favorably disposed toward the Macedonian royal house because of the benefits they had received in the time of Philip, son of Amyntas.

So he understood that, once hard-pressed by Cleomenes, they would quickly take refuge with Antigonus and the hopes that Macedonia offered.

Accordingly, he confided his whole plan in secret to Nicophanes and Cercidas of Megalopolis, who were his family's hereditary guest-friends and well suited to the undertaking.

Through them he easily prompted the Megalopolitans to send an embassy to the Achaeans and urge them to appeal to Antigonus for help.

So the Megalopolitans appointed Nicophanes and Cercidas themselves as envoys to the Achaeans, and from there straight on to Antigonus, should the nation agree.

The Achaeans gave the Megalopolitans permission to send the embassy.

Nicophanes and his colleagues hastened to meet the king, and concerning their own city they spoke only of the essentials, briefly and in summary; but most of what they said, following Aratus's instructions, concerned

the broader situation, in accordance with his directions. These were: to point out what the collaboration between the Aetolians and Cleomenes amounted to and where it was heading, and to make clear that the Achaeans themselves had primarily to be on their guard, but next—and even more so—Antigonus.

For one thing, it was plain to everyone that the Achaeans could not withstand a war from both sides at once; and for another, that if the Aetolians and Cleomenes prevailed over the Achaeans, they would not rest content nor stop at the present situation—and this was even easier for a sensible man to see than the first point.

For the greed of the Aetolians would never be content to be confined within the boundaries of the Peloponnese—nor even within those of Greece;

and the ambition of Cleomenes, his whole design, was for the present aimed at the rule of the Peloponnese, but once he gained that, he would at once reach for the leadership of all the Greeks.

And that leadership he could not attain without first overthrowing the Macedonian empire.

They therefore asked him to consider, looking ahead to the future, which course better served his own interests: to fight Cleomenes in the Peloponnese for the leadership of Greece alongside the Achaeans and Boeotians, or to abandon the greatest nation and stake the Macedonian empire on a battle in Thessaly against the Aetolians and Boeotians—and the Achaeans and Lacedaemonians as well.

If, then, the Aetolians—out of regard for the goodwill the Achaeans had shown them in the time of Demetrius—were to pretend to keep quiet, as they were doing even now, the Achaeans, they said, would wage war against Cleomenes on their own; and if fortune lent its support, they would have no need of anyone to help them. But if fortune turned against them,

and the Aetolians joined in the attack, they urged him to keep an eye on developments, so that he might not let the right moment slip, but might come to the aid of the Peloponnesians while they could still be saved.

As for good faith and the repayment of his kindness, they thought he need not trouble himself: once the service was rendered, they pledged that Aratus would himself find guarantees satisfactory to both parties.

Likewise, they said, he would himself indicate the right moment for the help to be given.
