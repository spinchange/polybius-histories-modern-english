# Book 3 Chapter 116: The Slaughter at Cannae

The Romans now had to carry on the fight over the bodies of the fallen. Lucius Aemilius, though he had been stationed on the right wing from the start and had shared in part of the cavalry struggle, was nevertheless still alive at this point.

Wishing to act in keeping with the words he had spoken in his address to the troops, and seeing that the crux of the whole decision of the battle lay in the infantry legions,

he rode along to the center of the entire battle line. There he both engaged personally and laid hands on the enemy, and at the same time encouraged and urged on his own soldiers.

Hannibal did the same on his side, for he too had taken up his position from the beginning at this part of his forces.

The Numidians on the right wing, charging the enemy cavalry posted on the Roman left, neither inflicted nor suffered much harm because of the peculiar manner of their fighting; but they did render the enemy ineffective by keeping them occupied and charging in upon them from every side.

But when Hasdrubal's men, having killed all but a very few of the cavalry by the river, came across from the left to support the Numidians, the Roman allied cavalry, foreseeing their attack, gave way and withdrew.

At this moment Hasdrubal appears to have done a practical and shrewd thing. Seeing that the Numidians were many in number and most effective and most fearsome against troops once they had been forced to give ground, he handed the fugitives over to the Numidians and led his own men toward the infantry battle, eager to bring help to the Libyans.

Falling upon the Roman legions from the rear and launching his squadrons against them in relays at many points at once, he both heartened the Libyans and humbled and dismayed the spirits of the Romans.

It was at this moment that Lucius Aemilius too, after suffering violent blows in the thick of hand-to-hand fighting, ended his life — a man who, if any ever did, rendered his country every duty throughout the rest of his life and at its final hour.

The Romans, as long as they could fight by wheeling about on the surface of their ranks to face those who had encircled them, held out;

but as those on the outside were continually being killed, and they were gradually pressed into a smaller and smaller compass, in the end they all fell there on the spot — among them Marcus Atilius and Gnaeus Servilius, the consuls of the previous year, men who had proved themselves brave and worthy of Rome in the moment of danger.

During this slaughter and struggle the Numidians, pursuing the fleeing cavalry, killed most of them and threw the rest down from their horses.

A few escaped to Venusia, among them Gaius Terentius, the Roman commander, a man who made his own soul a disgrace and his magistracy a loss to his country.

# Book 3 Chapter 117: Superiority in Cavalry Wins Battles

So the battle of Cannae between the Romans and the Carthaginians was fought out in this way — a battle in which the bravest of men were found among both the victors and the vanquished.

This became clear from the facts themselves. Of the six thousand cavalry, seventy escaped to Venusia with Gaius, and about three hundred of the allied horse made their way safely, scattered, into the towns;

while of the infantry, about ten thousand who were not part of the main battle were taken prisoner fighting, and from the battle itself only about three thousand perhaps escaped to the neighboring towns.

All the rest, numbering about seventy thousand, died nobly — the throng of cavalry having, both on this occasion and before it, furnished the Carthaginians their greatest asset for winning.

And it became clear to later generations that for the occasions of war it is better to have half the number of infantry but be superior in cavalry than to risk a decisive battle with all forces evenly matched against the enemy.

On Hannibal's side, about four thousand Celts fell, about fifteen hundred Iberians and Libyans, and about two hundred cavalry.

The Romans who were taken alive had been outside the main battle, and for the following reason.

Lucius had left ten thousand infantry at his own camp, so that if Hannibal, neglecting the protection of his palisade, should draw up his whole army for battle, these men might fall upon it during the fighting and gain control of the enemy's baggage;

or, if Hannibal should foresee what might happen and leave a sufficient guard, the contest over the main issue would be against fewer of the enemy.

They were taken captive in roughly the following way. When Hannibal had left an adequate guard at his palisade, the Romans, as soon as the battle began, laid siege to it according to their orders, assaulting those left behind in the Carthaginian camp. At first the defenders held out;

but when they were already under pressure, and Hannibal had by then decided the battle at every point, he came to their relief, routed the Romans, penned them up in their own camp, killed two thousand of them, and took all the rest prisoner.

In the same way the Numidians stormed those who had fled together to the strongholds in the countryside and brought them back — about two thousand of the cavalry who had been put to flight.

# Book 3 Chapter 118: The Consequences of the Battle of Cannae

The battle having been decided in the way described, the whole situation took a turn that followed from what both sides had expected.

For the Carthaginians, through this action, were at once masters of almost all the rest of the coast.

The Tarentines, indeed, immediately put themselves into their hands; the Argyrippans and some of the Capuans summoned Hannibal; and all the rest were now already looking to the Carthaginians.

These had high hopes of becoming masters of Rome itself at the first onset.

The Romans, for their part, at once gave up the rule of Italy on account of the defeat, and were in great fear and danger both for themselves and for the soil of their fatherland, expecting that at any moment Hannibal himself would arrive.

And indeed, as though Fortune were measuring out yet more and joining in the contest against them on top of what had already happened, it came about that a few days later, while fear gripped the city, the general who had been sent to Gaul fell unexpectedly into an ambush and was utterly destroyed, together with his army, by the Celts.

Nevertheless the Senate left undone none of the things in its power, but exhorted the people, secured the affairs of the city, and deliberated about the present crisis like men. This became evident from what happened afterward;

for although the Romans were on that occasion admittedly beaten and had yielded their claim to excellence in arms,

by the distinctive character of their constitution and by deliberating well they not only recovered the rule of Italy, afterward defeating the Carthaginians, but within a few years became masters of the whole inhabited world.

Accordingly, we shall close this book at these events — those of Spain and of Italy that the one-hundred-and-fortieth Olympiad embraced, having now set them forth.

When, in recounting the Greek affairs that took place during the same Olympiad, we come up to these same times, then at last we propose to compose, on its own, an account of the Roman constitution itself,

believing that an exposition of it is not only suited to the framework of our history, but also contributes greatly, for men who love learning and are concerned with practical affairs, toward the reform and construction of constitutions.
