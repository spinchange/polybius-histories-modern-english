# Book 3 Chapter 100: Hannibal In Apulia

The general Hannibal—to pick up where we left off—learned from his scouts that there was a very large supply of grain in the territory around Luceria and the place called Geronium, and that Geronium was well suited for collecting it.

He therefore decided to make his winter quarters there, and advanced, marching past Mount Liburnum toward the places just mentioned. When he reached Geronium,

which lies two hundred stades from Luceria, he at first tried through negotiation to win the inhabitants over to friendship and offered them guarantees of his promises; but when no one would listen, he set about besieging it.

He quickly took control of the town, put the inhabitants to death, but kept most of the houses and the walls intact, intending to use them as grain stores for the winter.

He stationed his army in front of the city and fortified the camp with a trench and palisade.

Once this was done, he sent two-thirds of his force out to gather grain, ordering each man to bring back a fixed measure every day for his own unit, this being the assigned quota for those put in charge of this management,

while with the remaining third he guarded the camp and watched over the foragers at various points.

Since most of the countryside was easily traversed and flat, the men gathering the grain were practically beyond counting, and the season was at its peak for the harvest, an immense quantity of grain came to be collected every day.

# Book 3 Chapter 101: Successes of Minucius

Marcus, having taken over the forces from Fabius, at first kept pace with the enemy along the mountain ridges, convinced that sooner or later he would clash with the Carthaginians somewhere at the passes.

But when he heard that Hannibal's men already held Geronium, were foraging the countryside for grain, and were encamped behind a palisade thrown up in front of the city, he turned away from the ridges and came down by the spur that runs out toward the plains.

Arriving at the height that lies in the territory of Larinum and is called Calene, he encamped near it, ready by any means whatever to engage the enemy.

When Hannibal saw the enemy approaching, he left a third of his force to forage, and taking the other two-thirds advanced sixteen stades from the city and encamped on a hill facing the enemy, intending both to intimidate his opponents and to provide security for his foragers.

After this, since there was a low hill between the two camps, conveniently situated and lying close to the enemy's position, he sent out about two thousand of his spearmen while it was still night and seized it.

When day came and Marcus caught sight of them, he led out his light-armed troops and assaulted the hill.

After a spirited skirmish, the Romans finally prevailed, and afterward they moved their whole camp to this spot.

For a while, because the camps faced each other, Hannibal kept the greater part of his force close to himself.

But as more days passed, he was forced to detach some men to pasture the livestock and others to forage for grain,

being eager—in keeping with his original plan—both to avoid spoiling his plunder and to gather as much grain as possible, so that there would be an abundance of everything for his men during the winter, and no less for the pack animals and the horses;

for he placed the greatest hopes of his army in his cavalry corps.

# Book 3 Chapter 102: The Romans Capture the Carthaginian Foragers

It was at this point that Marcus, observing that a large part of the enemy was scattered about the countryside on the tasks just described, picked the most favorable hour of the day and led out his army.

Coming up close to the Carthaginian camp, he drew up his heavy infantry in line, and dividing his cavalry and light troops into detachments, sent them against the foragers with orders to take no prisoners.

When this happened, Hannibal fell into great difficulty: he was strong enough neither to march out against the troops drawn up before him nor to relieve his men scattered about the countryside.

Of the Romans, those sent against the foragers killed many of the scattered men, while the troops drawn up in line finally reached such a pitch of contempt that they even began tearing down the palisade and all but besieging the Carthaginians.

Hannibal was in dire straits, yet even under this storm he held on, beating off those who came near and barely keeping his camp,

until Hasdrubal, gathering up the men who had fled from the countryside into the camp near Geronium—about four thousand of them—came to his aid.

Then, taking a little courage, he sallied out, drew up his line a short distance in front of the camp, and with difficulty fended off the immediate danger.

Marcus, having killed many of the enemy in the fighting around the palisade and still more out in the countryside, withdrew for the time being, with high hopes for what was to come.

The next day, when the Carthaginians abandoned the palisade, he advanced and occupied their camp.

For Hannibal, anxious that the Romans might seize by night the now-deserted camp at Geronium and become masters of his baggage and stores, decided to withdraw there himself and make his camp there once more.

From this time on, the Carthaginians went about their foraging more warily and cautiously, while the Romans, on the contrary, did so more boldly and rashly.

# Book 3 Chapter 103: Minucius Made Co-Dictator

The people in Rome, when news of what had happened reached them magnified beyond the truth, were overjoyed: first, because out of their previous despair about the whole situation some sort of change for the better seemed to be dawning for them,

and second, because they could now believe that the inaction and dismay of the armies up to this point had come about not through any cowardice in the forces, but through the caution of their commander.

And so everyone blamed and found fault with Fabius for handling his opportunities without daring, while they exalted Marcus so highly for what had occurred that something now happened which had never happened before:

they made him too a commander with absolute authority, convinced that he would soon bring the campaign to an end. Thus there came to be two dictators for the same operations—something that had never before occurred among the Romans.

When Marcus was informed of the people's goodwill and of the office granted him by the popular assembly, he was doubly spurred on to take risks and act boldly against the enemy.

Fabius too returned to the army, in no way altered by what had happened, but holding even more firmly to his original judgment.

Seeing that Marcus was puffed up, contending against him at every turn, and on the whole strongly bent on risking a battle, he offered him the following choice: either to command by turns, or to divide the forces and let each use his own legions according to his own preference.

Marcus accepted the division all too gladly, and so they split the troops and encamped apart from one another, about twelve stades apart.
