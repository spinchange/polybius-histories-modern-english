# Book 28 Chapter 5: Lycortas Advises Neutrality

When an assembly of the Acarnanians was convened at Thyrrheion, Aeschrion, Glaucus, and Chremas, who were on the Roman side, urged Gaius and his colleagues to place a garrison in Acarnania.

For, they said, there were men among them who were steering affairs toward Perseus and the Macedonians.

But Diogenes gave the opposite advice. He said that no garrison ought to be introduced into any city, since that was a measure reserved for peoples who had become enemies and had been defeated by the Romans.

The Acarnanians, having done nothing wrong, did not deserve to admit a garrison in any way at all.

As for the party of Chremas and Glaucus, he said that they wanted to build up a personal domination for themselves, and so they were slandering their political opponents and trying to bring in a garrison that would strengthen their own grasping ambitions.

After this had been said, Gaius and his colleagues saw that the crowd disliked the idea of garrisons and, wishing to keep in step with the Senate's policy, agreed with Diogenes' view, commended it, and departed for Larisa to rejoin the proconsul.

# Book 28 Chapter 6: Council of the Achaeans

The leading Greeks decided that the situation created by this embassy required close attention.

So they brought together those who agreed with them in their general political outlook: Arcesilaus and Ariston of Megalopolis, Stratius of Tritaea, Xenon of Patrae, and Apollonidas of Sicyon; and they took counsel about the crisis before them.

Lycortas remained fixed in the policy he had held from the first, judging that they should give no help either to Perseus or to the Romans, but likewise should oppose neither side.

To cooperate, he thought, would be disadvantageous for all the Greeks, since he foresaw how great the power of the victors would become.

But to oppose the Romans was dangerous, because in earlier times they had already come into conflict over public affairs with many Romans, including some of the most eminent.

Apollonidas and Stratius, on the other hand, thought that they should not deliberately set themselves against the Romans; but they said that men who tumbled headlong in their eagerness, who used public business to store up private favor with the Romans, and who acted in this way against the laws and against the common interest, ought to be checked and boldly resisted.

Archon said that they must follow the times, give their enemies no opening for slander, and not allow themselves to fall into the same condition as Nicander and his associates, who, before they had learned by experience what Roman power was, had brought themselves into the gravest troubles.

Polybius, Arcesilaus, Ariston, and Xenon shared this opinion.

Accordingly, they resolved that Archon should at once go forward for the office of strategus, and Polybius for the command of the cavalry.

# Book 28 Chapter 7: A Speech of Polybius

After these events had just taken place, and after Archon's party had already reached the conclusion that they must cooperate with the Romans and their friends, it so happened, by a fortunate turn, that Attalus made his request while they were ready to receive it.

So they readily gave him their assent and promised to support what he asked.

When Attalus had sent envoys, and these men appeared at the first assembly and addressed the Achaeans about restoring the honors paid to the king, urging them to do this as a favor to Attalus,

the crowd showed no clear opinion, and many rose to contradict the proposal for a variety of reasons.

Some, because they had originally been responsible for removing the honors, wanted to confirm the wisdom of their own policy; others, because of private grievances, thought they had found an opportunity to strike at the king; and some, from jealousy of those supporting the measure, were eager to prevent Attalus from carrying his proposal.

Archon rose to support the envoys,

for the matter called for the opinion of the strategus. But after saying only a few words he withdrew,

because he was wary of seeming to give this advice for profit, since he had spent a considerable sum of money in securing his office.

As great uncertainty prevailed, Polybius rose and made a number of arguments; but above all he won over the majority by pointing to the original decree of the Achaeans about these honors, in which it had been written that the improper and illegal honors should be removed—not, by Zeus, all of them.

He said that Sosigenes and Diopeithes, who were serving as judges at that time and were privately at odds with Eumenes, had seized on this opportunity to overturn all the honors granted to the king.

In doing so, they had acted contrary to the decree of the Achaeans, contrary to the authority given them, and, most of all, contrary to justice and what was fitting.

For the Achaeans had not resolved to abolish the honors of Eumenes because they had suffered some wrong at his hands; rather, because he had asked for rewards greater than his benefactions deserved, they had voted to remove only the excess.

Therefore, just as those judges, by putting their private hostility ahead of what was honorable for the Achaeans, had overthrown all the honors, so now the Achaeans, considering their own duty and dignity the highest authority, ought to correct the judges' error and, in general, the unreasonable treatment shown to Eumenes,

especially since the favor they were about to grant would rest not only on the king himself, but even more on his brother Attalus.

When the people approved what he said, a decree was drafted ordering the magistrates to restore all the honors of King Eumenes, except any that contained something unbecoming to the Achaean League or contrary to law.

So in this way, and at this moment, Attalus corrected the unjust treatment that had been shown in the Peloponnese regarding the honors belonging to his brother Eumenes.

# Book 28 Chapter 8: Perseus Sends Pleuratus to Genthius

Perseus sent envoys to King Genthius: Pleuratus the Illyrian, who was living with him as an exile, and Adaeus of Beroea.

He instructed them to explain what he had accomplished in the war, both against the Romans and against the Dardanians, and also what he had done for the present against the Epirotes and the Illyrians, and to invite Genthius into friendship and alliance with himself and the Macedonians.

So they made their journey beyond Mount Scardus through the region called Desert Illyria, which the Macedonians not long before had laid waste in order to make Dardanian incursions into Illyria and Macedonia more difficult.

Even so, Adaeus and his party came through those districts with great hardship to Scodra, and when they learned that Genthius was staying at Lissus they sent messengers on to him.

He quickly summoned them, and when they met him they spoke on the matters included in their instructions.

Genthius did not seem averse to friendship with Perseus, but as his excuse for not immediately agreeing to what was asked, he pleaded lack of funds and said that he could not undertake a war against Rome without money.

With this answer Adaeus and his companions set out on their return.

Perseus meanwhile arrived at Stuberra, sold the booty, and rested his forces while waiting for Pleuratus and the others.

When they arrived and he heard Genthius' reply, he at once sent Adaeus back again, together with Glaucias, one of his bodyguards, and as a third envoy the Illyrian, because he knew the Illyrian language,

giving them the same instructions as before, as though Genthius had not clearly explained what he needed and what, once provided, would allow him to comply with the request.

After they had set out, Perseus himself broke camp and marched with his army toward Uscana.
