# Book 3 Chapter 57: The Limits of History

Now that I have brought the narrative, the commanders of both sides, and the war itself into Italy, I want, before the fighting begins, to say a few words about what does and does not belong to a work of this kind.

Some readers may well ask why, after devoting so much space to the regions of Libya and Iberia, I have said nothing further about the strait at the Pillars of Heracles, or about the outer sea and the peculiar conditions found there,

nor anything about the British Isles and the working of tin, or about the silver and gold mines of Iberia itself—subjects on which the historians dispute with one another at very great length.

I passed these over not because I regard this branch of inquiry as foreign to history. My reasons were these: first, I did not want to keep breaking up the narrative at every turn, or to lead those who are eager to follow it away from the practical matter in hand;

and second, I judged it better not to deal with these topics in a scattered, incidental way, but to set aside a place and an occasion of their own for this part of the subject and there, so far as I am able, to give a true account of them.

So no one should be surprised if, further on, when I come to places of this sort, I again leave this material aside for the reasons just given.

And if there are some who insist absolutely on hearing about such things region by region and point by point, they may perhaps fail to see that they are in much the same case as greedy dinner guests.

For those guests, by sampling every dish set before them, neither truly enjoy any of the food at the moment nor afterward derive from it any useful nourishment and digestion—quite the opposite.

In the same way, readers who behave like this aim properly neither at real enjoyment in the present nor at profit for the future.

# Book 3 Chapter 58: Necessity of Refuting Past Errors

That this branch of history, if any, needs further discussion and more accurate correction is clear on many grounds, but above all on the following.

Nearly all writers, or at any rate the majority, have attempted to describe the peculiar features and locations of the regions on the outer edges of our known world, and in most of these accounts they have gone badly wrong in many particulars.

It would not do to pass this over in silence. Something must be said in answer to them—not casually and in scattered remarks, but with careful attention—and it must be said without rebuke or reproach,

praising them rather and correcting their ignorance, in the knowledge that they too, had they had the resources of our own day, would have revised and altered much of what they wrote.

For in earlier times one would find very few Greeks who undertook to investigate the remotest regions, since the attempt was impossible.

The dangers at sea in those days were many and hard to count, and those by land many times greater.

And even if someone, by necessity or by choice, did reach the ends of the inhabited world, he still did not accomplish his purpose.

It was difficult to be an eyewitness of many places at first hand, because some of the peoples had grown wholly barbarous and other regions were uninhabited; and it was harder still to learn and understand by conversation anything about what he saw, because the language was so strange.

And even if a man did gain such understanding, it was harder still than all the rest for one who had seen these things to keep to a measured account—to scorn the impulse to astonish and amaze for his own glory, to prefer the truth, and to report to us nothing beyond the facts.

# Book 3 Chapter 59: Recent Advances in Geography

Since, then, a true account of the regions I have mentioned was, in earlier times, not merely difficult but practically impossible, the writers do not deserve blame for anything they omitted or got wrong.

On the contrary, for whatever they did learn and for advancing knowledge of these matters in such an age, they deserve our praise and admiration.

But in our own day, now that the regions of Asia have been opened up by the empire of Alexander and the rest of the world by the supremacy of Rome, so that almost everywhere can be reached by sea and land,

and now that men of action, released from the rivalries of war and politics, have on that account gained many great openings for inquiry and learning about the matters just described,

it ought to be possible to know these formerly unknown things both better and more truly. This I shall myself try to do,

taking up a suitable place in my work for this part of the subject; and for those who are eager to learn I shall set out to give a fuller understanding of these matters,

since it was chiefly for this purpose that I took on the dangers [and hardships] that befell me in my travels through Libya, Iberia, and Gaul, and on the sea that borders these lands on the outside,

so that by correcting the ignorance of earlier writers I might make these parts of the inhabited world known to the Greeks as well.

But now let me return from this digression to my narrative and try to set out the pitched battles fought between the Romans and Carthaginians in Italy.

# Book 3 Chapter 60: Hannibal Attacks the Taurini

I have already stated the size of the force with which Hannibal invaded Italy.

After the invasion he pitched camp at the very foot of the Alps and at first set about restoring his troops.

For his whole army had not only been terribly worn down by the climbs and descents and by the rough ground of the passes, but was also in a wretched state from the shortage of supplies and from neglect of the men's bodies.

Many had simply given themselves up entirely, owing to the want and the unrelenting toil. For it had been impossible to bring abundant provisions for so many tens of thousands across such country, and most of what they did carry was lost along with the pack animals that perished.

So Hannibal, who had set out from the crossing of the Rhone with thirty-eight thousand infantry and more than eight thousand cavalry, lost nearly half his force in the passes, as I said above.

And even those who survived had, through the unbroken hardships just described, become almost like wild beasts in their appearance and general condition.

Hannibal therefore gave great care to looking after them and worked to restore both the spirits and the bodies of his men, and of the horses as well.

After this, when his army had now recovered, he found that the Taurini, who happen to live close to the foot of the mountains, were at odds with the Insubres and distrustful of the Carthaginians.

At first he invited them to friendship and alliance, but when they would not listen, he laid siege to their strongest city and took it by storm in three days.

By slaughtering those who had opposed him, he struck such fear into the neighboring barbarian tribes that they all came in at once and surrendered themselves to his protection.

The rest of the Celts who inhabited the plains had been eager to join forces with the Carthaginians, in keeping with their original intention,

but since the Roman legions had by now advanced past most of them and cut them off, they kept quiet; and some were even compelled to serve alongside the Romans.

Seeing this, Hannibal decided not to wait but to push forward and do something to embolden those who wished to share in the same hopes as himself.

# Book 3 Chapter 61: Approach of Scipio

Having resolved on this, and hearing that Publius had already crossed the Padus with his forces and was close at hand, Hannibal at first did not believe the reports.

He reflected that only a few days earlier he had left Publius behind near the crossing of the Rhone, and he reckoned how long and difficult the voyage from Massalia to Etruria was,

and besides this, he was aware that the march from the Tyrrhenian Sea through Italy to the Alps was a long one and hard for armies to traverse.

But as more and more messengers brought ever clearer reports, he was amazed and astonished at the whole design and achievement of the Roman general.

Publius felt much the same. At first he had not expected that Hannibal would even attempt the march through the Alps with an army of foreign races; and if he did dare it, he assumed that he would clearly be destroyed. So, while he was occupied with such thoughts,

when he learned that Hannibal had both come through safely and was already besieging certain towns in Italy, he was astounded at the man's daring and recklessness.

The same thing happened to the people at Rome over the news as it arrived.

For the last report about the Carthaginians—that they had taken Saguntum—had only just died down, and the Romans had laid their plans in view of it: they had sent off one of the consuls to Libya to besiege Carthage itself and the other to Iberia to fight Hannibal there. Then came word that Hannibal was present in Italy with an army and was already besieging some of its towns.

Since what was happening seemed beyond all expectation, they were thrown into confusion and at once dispatched a message to Tiberius at Lilybaeum, informing him of the enemy's presence and telling him that he must abandon the plans before him and hurry to the aid of their own affairs.

Tiberius at once assembled the men of the fleet and sent them off with orders to sail for home; the land forces he had the tribunes put under oath, fixing a day by which they must all be present at Ariminum to bivouac.

This is a town on the Adriatic, lying at the southern edge of the plains around the Padus.

With the alarm breaking out everywhere at once, and with these events falling upon everyone contrary to expectation, there was in every quarter no slight anxiety about what was to come.

# Book 3 Chapter 62: Gallic Prisoners

At about this time, as Hannibal and Publius were now drawing near to each other, each set out to address his own troops, putting before them what suited the present circumstances.

Hannibal undertook to deliver his exhortation in the following way.

He gathered his men together and brought forward some young prisoners whom he had captured while they were harassing his march in the difficult terrain around the Alps.

He had treated these men harshly, preparing for what he now intended: they were loaded with heavy chains, weakened by starvation, and their bodies ruined by the lash.

Setting them in the middle, he laid out suits of Gallic armor, of the kind their kings usually wear when they are about to fight in single combat; and beside these he stationed horses and brought in costly cloaks.

He then asked the young men which of them were willing to fight one another, on the terms that the winner would receive the prizes laid out, while the loser would be released from his present sufferings by death.

When they all shouted out together that they wanted to fight, he ordered them to draw lots and commanded the two on whom the lot fell to arm themselves and fight each other.

The moment the young men heard this, they raised their hands and prayed to the gods, each one eager to be among those chosen by lot.

And when the result of the drawing was announced, those who had been chosen were overjoyed, while the rest felt the opposite.

When the fight was over, the surviving prisoners counted the dead man no less fortunate than the victor, since he had been released from many great evils while they themselves still had to endure them.

The same view was taken by most of the Carthaginians as well: seeing, by the comparison set before them, the misery of those who were being led off still alive, they pitied these, while the dead man they counted altogether [blessed].

# Book 3 Chapter 63: Harangues of Hannibal and Scipio

[The Carthaginians] counted [the dead man] fortunate. Having by these means produced the frame of mind he wanted in the hearts of his troops,

Hannibal then came forward himself and said that he had brought in the captives for this very purpose: so that, seeing plainly in the misfortunes of others what was at stake, they might take better counsel about their own situation.

For Fortune had penned them into a struggle and a moment much like that of the captives, and had set before them prizes much like those just offered.

They must either conquer, or die, or fall alive into the hands of their enemies. The prize of victory was not horses and cloaks, but to become the most fortunate of all men, masters of the prosperity of Rome.

If instead they should fall in battle, fighting to their last breath for the noblest of hopes, they would end their lives by the law of combat without ever tasting any evil;

but those who were beaten, and who out of a craving for life chose to flee, or in any other way clung to living, would share in every evil and every misfortune.

For none of them, he said, was so foolish or dull-witted that—recalling the length of the road they had traveled from their homelands, recalling the multitude of enemies in between, and knowing the size of the rivers they had crossed—he could ever hope to reach home by flight.

Therefore, with every such hope cut off entirely, he urged them to take the same view of their own situation that they had just now taken of the misfortunes of others.

For just as in that case everyone counted the victor and the dead man fortunate and pitied those left alive, so, he said, they ought to judge their own case, and all of them go into battle—above all to conquer, but if that proved impossible, then to die.

The hope of living on in defeat he insisted they must in no way entertain.

For if they adopted this reasoning and this resolve, both victory and survival would plainly follow for them.

For all who, whether by choice or by necessity, have held to such a resolve have never been disappointed of overcoming those drawn up against them.

And when it happens, moreover, that the enemy hold the opposite hope—as is now the case with the Romans, whose native land is at hand, so that most of them clearly have safety in flight—then plainly the daring of men who have given up all hope becomes irresistible.

When most of the men had embraced both the example and the words, and had taken up just the drive and resolve that the speaker had aimed to inspire, Hannibal praised them and dismissed them for the time being, and gave orders to break camp at first light the next day.
