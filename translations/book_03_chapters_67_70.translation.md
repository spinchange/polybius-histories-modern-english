# Book 3 Chapter 67: The Gauls Join Hannibal

The Celts who were serving alongside the Romans, seeing that the Carthaginians' prospects were the more promising of the two, made an agreement among themselves and watched for the right moment to strike, each remaining meanwhile in his own tent.

After the men in the camp had eaten their supper and gone to sleep, the Celts let most of the night pass, and then, around the time of the morning watch, they armed themselves and fell upon the Romans encamped nearest to them.

They killed many of them and wounded a good number more; and in the end, after cutting off the heads of the dead, they withdrew to the Carthaginians, numbering about two thousand infantry and a little under two hundred cavalry.

Hannibal welcomed their arrival warmly. He at once encouraged them, promised each of them suitable rewards, and sent them off to their own cities to report to their fellow citizens what they had done and to urge them to join his alliance.

For he knew that, once they learned of the act of treachery committed against the Romans by their own countrymen, they would all be compelled to throw in their lot with him.

At the same time the Boii also arrived and handed over to him the three men whom the Romans had sent out to distribute the land—men whom they had seized at the very start of the war by breaking the truce, as I have already described above.

Hannibal welcomed their goodwill and gave pledges of friendship and alliance to those who had come; but he returned the men to them, instructing them to keep them safe, so that through them they might recover their own hostages, in keeping with their original purpose.

Publius, indignant at the treachery that had occurred, reasoned that since the Celts had long been hostile toward the Romans, the defection of these men would cause all the surrounding Gauls to go over to the Carthaginians; and so he decided that he must guard against what was coming.

For this reason, when night fell, he broke camp toward dawn and set out toward the river Trebia and the hills adjoining it, trusting in the strength of the terrain

As it was, however, most of them managed to cross the river Trebia first; of those left behind in the rearguard, some were killed and others taken alive by the Carthaginians.

Publius, then, having crossed the river just mentioned, made camp on the first line of hills,

and after surrounding his position with a ditch and a palisade, he waited for Tiberius and the forces with him, while attending carefully to his own recovery, eager to be able to take part in the coming engagement.

Hannibal pitched his camp about forty stades away from the enemy.

The mass of the Celts who inhabited the plains, having committed themselves to the Carthaginians' hopes, supplied the army abundantly with provisions and were ready to share in every undertaking and danger alongside Hannibal.

The people in Rome, when news of the cavalry engagement reached them, were taken aback that the outcome had run contrary to their expectations; yet they were not at a loss for excuses to convince themselves that what had happened was not a defeat.

Some blamed the rashness of the general, others the deliberate treachery of the Celts, which they inferred from the latter's recent defection.

In general, since their infantry legions remained intact, they reckoned their hopes for the overall outcome were likewise intact.

And so, when Tiberius and the legions with him arrived and were marching through Rome, the people imagined that the mere sight of them would decide the battle.

When the soldiers had gathered at Ariminum in accordance with their oath, the general took them up and led them forward, hurrying to join Publius's forces.

Having effected the junction and pitched camp beside them with his own troops, he let his men recover their strength—for they had marched on foot continuously for forty days from Lilybaeum to Ariminum—while he made every preparation as if for battle,

and he himself conferred carefully with Publius, inquiring into what had already taken place and deliberating together about the present situation.

# Book 3 Chapter 69: A Skirmish Near the Trebia

Around this same time Hannibal, by an act of treachery, gained control of the town of Clastidium, which was surrendered to him by the man from Brundisium to whom the Romans had entrusted it.

Having become master of the garrison and the stores of grain, he used the grain for his immediate needs, but took the men he had captured along with him unharmed,

wishing to give a demonstration of his own policy, so that those caught up by the circumstances need not be afraid or despair of obtaining safety from him.

He honored the traitor lavishly, eager to entice those holding positions of authority over toward the Carthaginians' prospects.

After this, observing that certain Celts who lived between the Padus and the river Trebia had made a friendship with him while at the same time sending messages to the Romans—convinced that in this way they would secure their safety from both sides—

he sent out two thousand infantry and about a thousand cavalry, both Celts and Numidians, with orders to overrun their territory.

When these men had carried out their orders and gathered a great deal of plunder, the Celts immediately came to the Roman camp begging for help.

Tiberius, who had long been looking for an opportunity to act, now seized on this as a pretext and sent out the greater part of his cavalry, together with about a thousand javelin-throwers from his infantry.

When these troops quickly came up with the enemy across the Trebia and fought them for the plunder, the Celts and Numidians were put to flight and withdrew toward their own camp.

But the troops on guard in front of the Carthaginian camp quickly grasped what was happening and brought up reserves from there to help the hard-pressed men; whereupon the Romans in turn were routed and withdrew back to their own camp.

Tiberius, seeing what was happening, sent out all his cavalry and javelin-throwers. When this happened, the Celts again gave way and retreated to the safety of their own position.

The Carthaginian general, being unprepared to risk a decisive engagement and believing that one should never bring on a full-scale battle without a settled plan or on the strength of any chance opening—which, it must be said, is the mark of a good commander—

checked his own men as they drew near the camp and forced them to halt and turn about, but prevented them from pursuing and engaging the enemy, recalling them through his aides and trumpeters. The Romans, after waiting a short time, withdrew,

having lost only a few of their own men while destroying a greater number of the Carthaginians.

# Book 3 Chapter 70: Tiberius Sempronius Plans to Attack

Tiberius, elated and overjoyed at this success, was keen to decide the whole issue as quickly as possible.

Now it lay within his power to act on his own judgment in the present circumstances, since Publius was ill; nevertheless, wishing to secure his colleague's opinion as well, he discussed the matter with him.

Publius, however, held the opposite view about the present situation.

For he reckoned that their legions would become better after wintering under arms; that the faithlessness of the Celts would not remain loyal once the Carthaginians were inactive and forced to keep quiet, but would instead make some fresh move against them again;

and besides, that once he himself had recovered from his wound, he hoped to be able to render real service to their common cause.

Reasoning along these lines, he urged Tiberius to remain with things as they stood.

Tiberius knew that each of these points was being stated truly and properly; but driven by ambition and an unreasonable confidence in the situation, he was eager to decide the whole matter himself—before Publius could be present at the battle and before the incoming consuls could take over the command; for the time for that was now at hand.

For this reason, choosing not the moment that the circumstances called for but his own, he was plainly bound to fall short of what was needed.

Hannibal, holding much the same view as Publius about the present situation, was for the opposite reason eager to engage the enemy: first, he wished to make use of the Celts' fighting spirit while it was still fresh;

second, to engage the Roman legions while they were still untrained and newly recruited; third, to fight while Publius was still unable to take the field; and, most important of all, to be doing something and not let the time slip away to no purpose.

For when a general has brought his army into foreign territory and is attempting bold undertakings, this is the one means of safety: continually to keep the hopes of his allies fresh.
