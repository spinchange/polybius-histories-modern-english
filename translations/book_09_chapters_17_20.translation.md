# Book 9 Chapter 17: Failures Arising From Ignorance

When Aratus, the Achaean general, planned to seize the city of Cynaetha by treachery, he agreed with his collaborators inside the city on a day when Aratus was to come by night to the river flowing from Cynaetha and wait there quietly with his force.

The men inside, about midday when they got the chance, were to send one of their number out through the gate quietly, wrapped in a cloak, and tell him to stand before the city on the tomb that had been agreed upon as the signal.

The rest were to lay hands on the officials who, around midday, when they were asleep, were accustomed to guard the gate.

Once that was done, the Achaeans were to hurry out from their ambush and make straight for the gate.

When all had been arranged and the time came, Aratus arrived and remained hidden by the river, waiting for the signal.

But about the fifth hour a man who kept fine sheep near the city happened to need to ask a shepherd some ordinary question. He came out through the gate wearing a cloak and stood on the very same tomb, looking around for the shepherd.

Aratus and his men thought the agreed signal had been given and all rushed toward the city in haste.

But the gate was quickly shut by the guards, since the men inside had not yet made any preparations. So Aratus not only failed in the attempt, but brought the gravest misfortunes upon those within who were helping him: for once they were exposed, they were immediately seized and put to death.

What, then, was the cause of this disaster? It was that the general had made the signal too simple, being still young and inexperienced in the precision provided by double signals and counter-signals.

So narrow is the margin by which military operations incline toward success or failure.

# Book 9 Chapter 18: Cleomenes Tries to Take Megalopolis

Likewise Cleomenes the Spartan, intending to capture Megalopolis by stratagem, arranged with the men guarding the stretch of wall near the place called Pholeus that he would come by night with his force during the third watch; for at that hour his accomplices happened to be on guard at the wall.

But because he failed to take into account that around the rising of the Pleiades the nights are already very short, he set his army in motion from Sparta at sunset.

As a result he could not get there in time, and when daylight overtook him he made a rash and senseless attempt, failed disgracefully, lost many men, and put everything at risk.

Yet if he had struck the agreed time correctly and had brought in his troops while his supporters controlled the entrance, he would not have failed in the attempt.

Again, King Philip too, as I said earlier, made two mistakes in his intrigue against the city of the Melitaeans: he brought ladders shorter than the need required, and he also failed in timing.

For though he had arranged to arrive around midnight, when everyone would already be asleep, he started too early from Larisa and reached the territory of the Melitaeans before the proper moment.

He could neither remain where he was, for fear that his arrival would be reported to those inside, nor withdraw again and still escape notice.

So, compelled to continue forward, he reached the city while the people were still awake. Consequently he could neither force an entry by ladders, because they were out of proportion, nor get in through the gate, because the men inside could not cooperate at that hour.

In the end he merely stirred up the city, lost many of his own men, and returned in disgrace without accomplishing anything, having done nothing except warn everyone else to distrust him and stay on their guard.

# Book 9 Chapter 19: Example: Why Nicias Failed at Syracuse

Again Nicias, the Athenian general, was in a position to save the army at Syracuse. He had chosen the proper moment of the night to escape the notice of the enemy and withdraw to safety, but when an eclipse of the moon occurred he became superstitious, as though it portended some grave danger, and halted the departure.

Because of this, when he set the army in motion on the following night, the enemy had already sensed what was happening, and both the camp and the commanders fell into the hands of the Syracusans.

And yet, if he had only asked those who had experience in such matters, he could not only have avoided missing his proper opportunity for so foolish a reason, but could even have used the phenomenon to his own advantage because of the enemy's ignorance.

For the ignorance of one's opponents is a great advantage to those who understand.

So much, then, for the need to study astronomy up to the point already described. As for the proper proportion of ladders, the method of calculating it is as follows.

If the height of the wall is supplied by one of the collaborators inside, the proper size of the ladders is obvious: if the wall's height is, say, ten units, the ladders ought to be twelve of the same units, with some room to spare.

And the distance of the foot of the ladder from the wall should be half the ladder's length, so that the ladders may neither stand too far off and break easily under the weight of those climbing, nor lean too upright and become dangerously insecure for the men mounting them.

But if it is impossible either to measure the wall or even to get near it, then one must estimate from a distance the height of any object that stands upright at right angles to a level surface.

The method of making this estimate is both practicable and easy for those willing to study what mathematicians teach.

# Book 9 Chapter 20: Need of Some Knowledge of Mathematics

Therefore it is again clear that those who wish to succeed in military enterprises and operations must have studied geometry—not with full professional mastery, but enough to understand proportion and the theory of similarity.

For this knowledge is necessary not only in the cases already mentioned, but also in rearranging the shapes of camps, so that one may sometimes alter the whole form while preserving the same proportions among all the enclosed parts,

and at other times keep the same shapes while increasing or reducing the ground occupied by the camp, always in proportion to the numbers added to or removed from it.

I have explained these matters more exactly in my notes on tactical formations.

And I do not think anyone can reasonably blame me for adding many requirements to the art of generalship by insisting that those who aim at it must study astronomy and geometry.

For while I strongly reject what is superfluous in such disciplines—what is dragged in merely for display and empty talk—and likewise reject carrying them further than practical usefulness demands, I am very earnest and serious about what is necessary.

It would be absurd if those who aspire to dancing or flute-playing willingly accept preliminary training in rhythm and music,

and likewise the corresponding preparation required for athletics, because the final achievement in each field seems to need the help of such studies,

while those who claim to pursue generalship should feel annoyed if they must take up outside disciplines to some limited extent. That would mean that practitioners of vulgar crafts are more diligent and more ambitious than men who choose to distinguish themselves in the noblest and most dignified of pursuits.

No sensible person would admit such a thing. Let this be enough on these matters.
