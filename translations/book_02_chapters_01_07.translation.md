# Book 2 Chapter 1: Recapitulation of Book 1

In the book before this one I set out clearly when the Romans, having consolidated their position in Italy, began to take a hand in affairs beyond it; and after that, how they crossed over into Sicily, and for what reasons they undertook the war against the Carthaginians over that island.

After that I told when they first began to assemble naval forces, and what happened to each side over the course of the war down to its conclusion—the outcome of which was that the Carthaginians withdrew from the whole of Sicily, while the Romans gained control of the entire island except for the districts under Hiero's command.

Following on from this, I set about describing how the mercenaries, in revolt against the Carthaginians, kindled what is called the Libyan War; how far the atrocities committed during it went; and what course these extraordinary events ran until their end and the triumph of Carthage.

Now I shall try to make plain what came next, touching on each matter in summary fashion, in keeping with the plan I laid out from the start.

For as soon as the Carthaginians had settled affairs in Libya, they at once raised an army and dispatched Hamilcar to the regions of Iberia.

He took over the troops, together with his son Hannibal—then nine years old—and crossing by the Pillars of Heracles, set about recovering Carthaginian fortunes in Iberia.

He spent about nine years in those parts, and after subjecting many of the Iberians to Carthage, some by war and some by persuasion, he ended his life in a way worthy of his earlier achievements.

For while drawn up in battle against the most warlike peoples, those with the greatest power, and while exposing himself with daring and reckless courage at the critical moment of danger, he met a valiant death.

The Carthaginians handed over the command to Hasdrubal, who was his son-in-law and the commander of his fleet.

# Book 2 Chapter 2: Situation in Illyria

It was at this same period that the Romans set about making their first crossing, with an army, into Illyria and these parts of Europe. This is not to be looked at as a minor matter,

but must be studied with close attention by anyone who genuinely wishes to grasp both the purpose of my work and the growth and consolidation of Roman power.

They decided to make the crossing for reasons of the following kind.

Agron, king of the Illyrians, was the son of Pleuratus, and he possessed the most powerful land and naval forces of any king who had reigned in Illyria before him.

Bribed with money by Demetrius, the father of Philip, he had promised to help the Medionians, who were being besieged by the Aetolians.

For the Aetolians, being quite unable to persuade the Medionians to join their own league, had resolved to take them by force.

So they marched out with their whole army, encamped around the city, and laid siege to it without a break, bringing every kind of force and siege engine against it.

But when the time for the annual elections drew near, and a new general had to be chosen, and the besieged were already in a bad way and seemed likely to surrender any day, the general then in office made a proposal to the Aetolians. He argued

that, since it was he who had taken on the hardships and dangers of the siege, it was only fair that, once they captured the place, he should be allowed both the management of the spoils and the privilege of inscribing his name on the dedicated arms.

But some objected to what he said—above all those who were standing for the office—and urged the assembly not to settle the matter in advance, but to leave it open, so that fortune might bestow this crown on whomever it chose.

In the end the Aetolians decided that whoever was appointed general and captured the city should share with his predecessor both the management

of the spoils and the inscribing of the arms. This had been resolved, and on the very next day the election and the transfer of office were due to take place, as is the custom among the Aetolians, when during the night a hundred boats sailed up to the territory of Medion, to the places nearest the city, carrying five thousand Illyrians.

They put in to shore, and when day came they made their landing swiftly and unobserved, and forming up in the order customary among them, advanced in companies against the Aetolian camp.

When the Aetolians realized what was happening, they were taken aback by the unexpectedness and the boldness of the Illyrians; yet, having long been full of self-importance and confident in their own strength, they remained reasonably steady.

So they drew up the bulk of their hoplites and cavalry on the level ground in front of their camp, while with a portion of their cavalry and their light troops they moved to seize in advance the high ground in front of the palisade, which was conveniently placed for defense.

But the Illyrians, charging the light troops at the first rush, drove them back by the sheer mass and weight of their formation, and forced the cavalry stationed with them to fall back on the heavy infantry.

Then, charging down from the high ground against the troops drawn up on the plain, they quickly routed them, especially as the Medionians joined the attack on the Aetolians by sallying out from the city at the same time.

They killed many of them and took even more prisoners, and they captured all their arms and baggage.

So the Illyrians, having carried out their king's orders and brought the baggage and the rest of the plunder down to their boats, at once put to sea, setting

their course for home. The Medionians, having won an unexpected deliverance, gathered in assembly and deliberated on their affairs in general and, in particular, on the inscribing of the arms.

They resolved to make the inscription a joint one, naming both the man who had held the office of Aetolian general and those who were standing for it for the coming year, in accordance with the Aetolians' own decree—

as though Fortune were deliberately demonstrating her power to the rest of mankind through what had happened to these men.

For the very things the Medionians had expected to suffer at the hands of their enemies any moment now, she gave them the power to inflict on those same enemies, all in a very short space of time.

And the Aetolians, by the unexpected disaster they met with, taught everyone never to treat the future as though it had already happened, nor to count confidently on outcomes that may still turn out otherwise, but to allow a share to the unexpected—being human in every situation, but most of all in matters of war.

As for King Agron: when the boats had sailed back and he had heard from his officers the details of the engagement, he was overjoyed at the thought of having beaten the Aetolians, those men of the highest spirit. He gave himself over to bouts of drinking and other such revelry, and fell ill with pleurisy, from which within a few days he died.

His wife Teuta took over the kingdom and conducted the detailed administration of affairs through trusted friends.

But she relied on a woman's reasoning, looking only at the recent stroke of good fortune and taking no thought for anything beyond it. First, she gave permission to those sailing on their own account to plunder any ships they fell in with;

second, she gathered a fleet and a force no smaller than the previous one, and sent it out, declaring every coast fair game to its commanders.

# Book 2 Chapter 5: Queen Teuta's Pirates

Those who were sent out made their first attack on Elis and Messenia, for the Illyrians were always raiding these regions.

Because of the length of their coastline, and because the cities that held power in them lay inland, relief against the Illyrians' landings was always very slow and a long time in coming. As a result, the Illyrians overran and pillaged these regions whenever they liked, without fear.

On this occasion, however, when they reached Phoenice in Epirus, they put in for supplies.

There they fell in with some Gauls—about eight hundred in number—who were serving as mercenaries with the Epirotes and were stationed in Phoenice. They discussed the betrayal of the city with them, and when these Gauls agreed to the plan, they disembarked and at the first assault made themselves masters of the city and everyone in it, with the Gauls inside working with them.

When the Epirotes learned what had happened, they hurried to the rescue with a full levy. On reaching Phoenice, they encamped with the river that flows past the city in front of them, and for safety's sake tore up the planks of the bridge over it.

But when word reached them that Scerdilaidas was approaching by land with five thousand Illyrians through the pass near Antigoneia, they detached part of their force and sent it off to guard Antigoneia; meanwhile they passed the rest of their time carelessly, indulging without restraint in the produce of the countryside, and grew slack about their guard duty and outposts.

The Illyrians, perceiving both the division of their forces and their general carelessness, marched out by night, laid planks on the bridge, crossed the river safely, took up a strong position, and stayed there for the rest of the night.

When day came, and both sides drew up in front of the city, the Epirotes were beaten: many of them fell, still more were captured, and the rest escaped in the direction of the Atintanes.

# Book 2 Chapter 6: The Aetolians and Achaeans Support the Epirotes

The Epirotes, then, having met with such misfortunes and lost all the hopes they had placed in themselves, sent envoys to the Aetolians and to the Achaean League, begging with suppliant pleas for help.

These took pity on their plight and consented, and afterwards came to their aid, arriving at Helicranum.

The men who had seized Phoenice, having first joined up with Scerdilaidas and advanced to the place, encamped opposite the relief force, wishing to give battle.

But they found themselves hampered by the difficult terrain; and at the same time a letter arrived from Teuta, in which she said they ought to return home as quickly as possible, because some of the Illyrians had defected to the Dardani.

So, after ravaging Epirus, they made a truce with the Epirotes.

Under its terms they restored to them the free persons and the city for a ransom, but loaded the slaves and the rest of the spoils onto their boats; then some of them sailed away, while the troops under Scerdilaidas withdrew again by land through the pass near Antigoneia.

In doing so they instilled no small or ordinary alarm and fear in the Greeks who lived along the coasts.

For each of these peoples, seeing the most strongly fortified and most powerful city in Epirus so unexpectedly enslaved, no longer felt anxiety, as in earlier times, merely for their property in the countryside, but now for themselves and their cities.

The Epirotes, however, having been saved beyond all expectation, were so far from trying to take revenge on those who had wronged them, or to show gratitude to those who had helped them, that on the contrary they sent envoys to Teuta and made an alliance with the Illyrians, together with the Acarnanians,

under which they cooperated with the Illyrians in the period that followed, while working against the Achaeans and the Aetolians.

By these actions they showed themselves to have behaved senselessly toward their benefactors at that time, and to have planned foolishly from the start in regard to their own affairs.

# Book 2 Chapter 7: A Band of Gallic Mercenaries

For that men, being human, should fall unexpectedly into some calamity is the fault not of the sufferers but of Fortune and of those who do the wrong;

but that they should plunge themselves into the gravest disasters thoughtlessly and with their eyes open is, by common admission, the fault of the victims themselves.

That is why those who stumble through Fortune are met with pity, forgiveness, and assistance, whereas those who fail through their own bad judgment are followed by reproach and censure from sensible people.

And this is just what the Epirotes would rightly have met with from the Greeks at that time.

For, in the first place, who—mindful of the general reputation of the Gauls—would not have been wary of entrusting to them a prosperous city that offered so many openings for treachery?

And second, who would not have been on his guard against the known character of that particular band? For in the beginning they had been driven out of their own country, when their fellow countrymen banded together against them because they had betrayed their own friends and kinsmen;

then, having been taken in by the Carthaginians because the latter were hard pressed by war, they were brought into Agrigentum to garrison it—being at that time more than three thousand strong—and, when a dispute arose between the soldiers and their generals over pay, they at once set about plundering the city of the Agrigentines.

After that, when the Carthaginians once more brought them into Eryx for the same purpose, while the Romans were besieging the city, they attempted to betray both the city and those besieged in it along with them;

and when this attempt failed, they deserted to the enemy, by whom they were again trusted—and then plundered the temple of Aphrodite of Eryx.

For this reason the Romans, having clearly recognized their impiety, made it their first priority, as soon as they had ended the war with the Carthaginians, to disarm them, put them aboard ships, and banish them from the whole of Italy.

These were the men the Epirotes made guardians of their democracy and their laws, and into whose hands they entrusted their most prosperous city. How, then, could they not reasonably be seen as the authors of their own misfortunes?

So much, then, for the folly of the Epirotes, and for the principle that sensible people should never bring in a garrison stronger than themselves—especially one of barbarians.
