# Book 31 Chapter 13: Ariarathes of Cappadocia

Once everything was ready on the shipmaster's side, and Demetrius only had to complete his own arrangements, he sent his foster-father ahead to Syria to gather intelligence and observe what was happening there among the populace.

His companion Apollonius had shared in the plan from the beginning; and, since there were two brothers, Meleager and Menestheus, he disclosed the undertaking to them, but to no one else among his followers, though they were more numerous.

These men were Apollonius' own sons. Their father had enjoyed great influence with Seleucus, but when Antiochus took over the kingdom he had withdrawn to Miletus.

As the day agreed upon with the sailors drew near, there had to be a farewell entertainment at the house of one of his friends before the departure.

For it was impossible to hold the dinner at his own quarters, since he was accustomed to invite everyone in his circle with great care.

Those who knew of the plan were to dine at home and then come to the ship, each bringing one servant-boy with him; the rest they had sent on to Anagnia, intending to follow themselves on the next day.

At this time Polybius happened to be ill and confined to bed, but he knew everything that was being done, because Menyllus kept continually informing him of each new development.

So Polybius, anxious that habit might get the better of Demetrius — since he was naturally fond of drinking parties and still altogether young — and that drunkenness might create some difficulty at the moment of departure,

wrote a short note, sealed it, and at dusk sent one of his own servants with instructions to call out Demetrius' cupbearer, hand him the note without saying who he was or who had sent it, and tell him to give it to Demetrius at once for immediate reading.

Everything was done as arranged, and Demetrius took the note and read it.

The note contained these thoughts:

The man who acts with the future in mind goes forward prepared.

Night gives equal cover to all, but to the daring it gives more.

Dare something,

risk it, act, fail,
or succeed — anything rather than betray yourself.

Stay sober, and remember to mistrust: these are the sinews of good sense.

# Book 31 Chapter 14: Ambassadors from Ariarathes to Rome

After reading this and understanding the situation, and who the men were and who had sent them, Demetrius at once pretended that he was going aboard for the voyage and slipped away, his friends escorting him part of the way.

When he reached his lodging, he sent off the unnecessary servants to Anagnia, ordering them to take the nets and the dogs and meet him at Circeii.

For that was where he regularly liked to hunt wild boar with special zeal; indeed, this had been the original beginning of his friendship with Polybius.

After this he explained the plan to Nicanor and his companions and urged them to share the same hopes.

Since they all accepted the proposal eagerly, he ordered them to go back at once to their lodgings and instruct their servants to go on ahead before dawn to Anagnia and, together with the hunters, meet them at Circeii,

while they themselves were to take traveling clothes and return to him, telling their servants that on the next day they would join Demetrius at the place already mentioned.

When everything had been done as described, they set out by night for Ostia, to the mouth of the Tiber.

Menyllus went on ahead and spoke with the sailors, saying that a message had come to him from the king, and that for this reason he himself had to remain in Rome for the present, but should send to the king the most trustworthy of the young men, from whom he would learn everything concerning his brother.

So he said that he himself would not embark, but that the young men who were to sail would arrive about midnight.

The shipmasters were indifferent, because the freight agreed on had been paid to them from the beginning and they had long had everything ready for the voyage.

The party with Demetrius arrived as the third watch was ending: there were eight of them, with five servant-boys and three younger attendants.

Menyllus then talked matters over with them, showed them the monthly provisions, and further introduced them fully to the shipmaster and the passengers; after that they went aboard.

At first light the captain weighed anchor and put to sea, having absolutely no suspicion of what was really going on, but thinking that he was simply carrying some soldiers from Menyllus to Ptolemy.

# Book 31 Chapter 15: Rhodes Asks Rome for Calynda

On the next day in Rome no one was likely to go looking for Demetrius or for the companions who had left with him.

Those who stayed behind with him thought that he had gone off to Circeii, while those at Anagnia were coming to meet him at the same place, expecting that he would be there.

So the escape remained completely unnoticed until one of the servant-boys, after being flogged at Anagnia, ran off to Circeii, intending to join Demetrius there.

Not finding him there either, he ran back to Rome, expecting to meet him on the road.

When he failed to encounter him anywhere, he revealed the matter to Demetrius' friends in Rome and to those who had been left behind at the house.

So when Demetrius was looked for on the fourth day after his departure, what had happened was suspected.

On the fifth day the Senate at once met to consider the matter; by then Demetrius was already beyond the strait near Sicily.

They therefore gave up the idea of pursuit, both because they thought he had already gained too great a lead on the voyage — for he had a strong following wind — and because they foresaw that even if they wished to stop him, they would be unable to do so.

After some days they appointed as envoys Tiberius Gracchus, Lucius Lentulus, and Servilius Glaucia,

who were first to inspect affairs among the Greeks, then to cross over into Asia and keep watch on the situation concerning Demetrius, examine the intentions of the other kings, and clear up the disputed issues involving the Galatians that were mentioned earlier.

This was why they appointed Tiberius, because he had been an eyewitness of the whole situation.

And this was the state of affairs in Italy.

Demetrius, meanwhile, was intent on the arrival of the man who was to be sent to him.
