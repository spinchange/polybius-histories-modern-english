# Book 4 Chapter 25: War Declared With the Aetolians

When he reached Corinth, he found that the representatives from the allied cities had already arrived. He held a council with them and deliberated about what should be done and how they ought to deal with the Aetolians.

The Boeotians charged that the Aetolians had plundered the temple of Athena Itonia while a state of peace prevailed; the Phocians, that they had marched against Ambrysus and Daulium and tried to seize those cities;

the Epirotes, that they had ravaged their territory; and the Acarnanians demonstrated in detail how the Aetolians had organized a plot against Thyrium and had even dared to assault the city while it was still night.

In addition to these, the Achaeans set out their case: that the Aetolians had seized Clarium in the territory of Megalopolis; that they had ravaged the lands of Patrae and Pharae as they passed through them; that they had thoroughly sacked Cynaetha; that they had plundered the temple of Artemis at Lusi; that they had besieged the people of Cleitor; and that they had made designs against Pylus by sea, and by land against the recently founded city of the Megalopolitans, striving together with the Illyrians to lay it utterly waste.

When the councillors of the allies had heard all this, they all with one accord resolved to declare war on the Aetolians.

Having set out the aforementioned grievances in the preamble of their resolution, they appended a decree, making the further declaration that they would help the allies recover any territory or city of theirs that the Aetolians had been holding since the time when Demetrius, the natural father of Philip, died;

and likewise, as for those who had been compelled by circumstances to take part unwillingly in the Aetolian confederacy, that they would restore all of these to their ancestral forms of government, in possession of their own land and cities, free of garrisons, free of tribute, and independent, living under their own constitutions and ancestral laws.

They also wrote that they would help the Amphictyons recover their laws and their authority over the temple, which the Aetolians had now taken away from them, since the Aetolians wished to control the affairs of the temple themselves.

# Book 4 Chapter 26: Opening of the Social War

When this resolution had been ratified, in the first year of the 140th Olympiad, the war called the Social War had taken its beginning—a beginning that was just and fitting in view of the wrongs that had been committed.

The councillors at once sent envoys to the allies, so that, once the resolution had been confirmed among each people by a vote of the citizens at large, all might join in declaring war on the Aetolians from their respective territories.

Philip also sent a letter to the Aetolians, making clear that, if they had anything just to say in their defense regarding the charges against them, they should even now come together and settle the matter through discussion;

but that if they supposed they could, without any common declaration of war, plunder and ravage everyone, while those they wronged would not defend themselves—and that, if these did defend themselves, they would be judged to have started the war—then they were the most simple-minded of all people.

When the magistrates of the Aetolians received this letter, at first they expected that Philip would not come, and so they fixed an appointed day on which they would meet him at Rhium.

But when they learned that he was actually on his way, they sent a courier explaining that they were not able, before the assembly of the Aetolians met, to settle anything on their own authority concerning matters of general policy.

The Achaeans, having gathered for their regular assembly, all ratified the resolution and proclaimed the right of reprisal against the Aetolians.

And when the king too came before the council at Aegium and spoke at some length, they received what he said with goodwill and renewed with Philip himself the friendly relations that had existed between their ancestors.

# Book 4 Chapter 27: Disingenuous Policy of the Spartans

At about the same time the Aetolians, the season of their elections having arrived, chose as their Strategus Scopas, the very man who had been responsible for all the wrongful acts just described.

About these matters I scarcely know how one ought to speak. For to pass a public decree against making war, and yet to march out in full force and plunder and carry off their neighbors' property; and to punish none of those responsible, but on the contrary to elect as generals and to honor the leaders in such deeds—this seems to me to be brimming with utter villainy.

For what else could one call such wickedness? My meaning will be made clear from the following examples.

When Phoebidas treacherously seized the Cadmeia in violation of the treaty, the Lacedaemonians penalized the man responsible, but did not withdraw the garrison—as though the wrong were undone by the punishment of the man who committed it, when in fact the opposite course was open to them; for it was the garrison that mattered to the Thebans.

Again, they proclaimed that they were leaving the cities free and self-governing in accordance with the peace concluded under Antalcidas, yet they did not withdraw their governors from the cities.

And when they had driven the Mantineans from their homes—men who were their friends and allies—they claimed they were doing no wrong, on the grounds that they had merely resettled them from one city into several;

acting with a folly that was joined to wickedness, plainly because they imagined that, if a man shuts his own eyes, his neighbors too cannot see.

For both peoples, then, this devotion to such a policy became the cause of the greatest disasters; and it is one that should in no way and on no account be emulated, either by individuals or by states, if they deliberate rightly.

King Philip, having concluded his business with the Achaeans, set out with his army for Macedonia, hastening to make his preparations for the war,

and through the aforementioned decree he held out fair hopes not only to the allies but to all the Greeks, of his mildness and royal magnanimity.

# Book 4 Chapter 28: The Wars in Italy, Greece, and Asia Become Interlaced

These events were taking place at the same time as when Hannibal, who had by now become master of all the territory south of the Iber river, was launching his attack on the city of Saguntum.

Now, if it had happened that Hannibal's first undertakings were from the very outset interwoven with affairs in Greece, it would clearly have been my duty to have given an account of them in the previous book, alternating and setting them side by side with the affairs in Iberia, following the order of the dates.

But since the wars in Italy, in Greece, and in Asia had their beginnings separately, while their conclusions were shared, I have decided to give my account of them separately as well, until we arrive at the point at which the aforementioned events became interwoven with one another and began to tend toward a single end—

for in this way both the account of the several beginnings will be clear and the interweaving evident (the very interweaving I indicated at the start, showing when, how, and for what causes it came about)—and from then on it remains to make my history of them all a single, common one.

The interweaving of events occurred near the conclusion of the war, in the third year of the 140th Olympiad. Therefore, from that point onward I shall give my account following the dates in a common narrative, but I shall treat the events before it separately,

as I have said, merely calling to mind those events of the same period that have been related in the previous book, so that my narrative may become not only easy to follow but also striking to those who attend to it.
