# Book 5 Chapter 57: Achaeus Attempts Treason

When Antiochus had returned home and distributed his troops into winter quarters, he kept sending messages to Achaeus,

reproaching him first for having dared to put on the diadem and act as king, and second for warning him that his dealings with Ptolemy and, in general, his excessive agitation had not escaped notice.

For while the king was campaigning against Artabazanes, Achaeus had convinced himself that Antiochus might suffer some disaster; and even if he did not, Achaeus hoped that, because of the great distance,

he would be able to forestall him by invading Syria and, using the Cyrrhestians who had revolted from the king as his allies, quickly gain control of the kingdom's affairs. With this in mind he set out from Lydia with his whole army.

When he reached Laodicea in Phrygia, he put on the diadem and for the first time dared to conduct himself and write to the cities as king, being urged on above all in this direction by the exile Garsyeris.

But as he continued his advance and was already near Lycaonia, the troops mutinied, displeased at the thought that the campaign was being directed against the man who was by nature and from the beginning their lawful king.

So Achaeus, recognizing the change in their feelings, gave up the enterprise he had planned; and wishing to persuade the troops that he had never intended from the beginning to march into Syria, he wheeled about and ravaged Pisidia.

By thereby providing abundant profit for the camp, he returned home with all his soldiers favorable to him and fully trusting him.

# Book 5 Chapter 58: Seleucia

The king understood all these matters clearly; so while he kept sending repeated warnings to Achaeus, as I said above, he threw his whole energy into the preparations against Ptolemy.

Therefore, when spring approached and he had gathered his forces at Apamea, he invited his friends to deliberate on how they ought to conduct the invasion of Coele-Syria.

Many things were said on this question, about the terrain, the necessary preparations, and the cooperation to be expected from the naval force; but Apollophanes, whom I mentioned before, a native of Seleucia, cut short all the proposals just described.

He said it was foolish to desire Coele-Syria and march against it while allowing Seleucia to remain in Ptolemy's hands, when that city was the founding center and, so to speak, the hearth of their own dominion.

Quite apart from the disgrace involved in seeing it garrisoned by the kings of Egypt, it also offered, in practical terms, the greatest and finest bases for action.

So long as it was held by the enemy, it was the greatest hindrance to all their plans.

For wherever they might decide to advance, they would need to exercise no less care and guarding over their own territory, because of the danger that enemy operations launched from that city posed.

But if they took it, he said, they would not only secure their own country firmly, but would also gain powerful support for their other undertakings and designs, both by land and by sea, because of the site's strategic advantages.

Everyone was persuaded by what he said, and it was decided to make the capture of this city their first step.

For Seleucia was still at that time held by garrisons of the Egyptian kings, dating from the time of Ptolemy called Euergetes,

when, because of what had happened to Berenice and in anger on her behalf, he campaigned in Syria and gained possession of this city.

# Book 5 Chapter 59: Description of Seleucia

Once this had been decided, Antiochus ordered Diognetus the admiral to sail against Seleucia, while he himself set out from Apamea with the army and, after coming to within about five stades of the city, encamped near the hippodrome.

He also sent Theodotus Hemiolius with the proper force toward Coele-Syria, to seize the passes and at the same time secure the way for his own operations.

The position of Seleucia and the peculiar nature of the surrounding country are as follows.

The city lies on the sea between Cilicia and Phoenicia, and above it towers a very great mountain called Coryphaeus.

Its western side is washed by the stretch of sea that lies between Cyprus and Phoenicia, while its eastern parts rise above the territory of Antioch and Seleucia.

On its southern slopes lies Seleucia, separated by a deep and hard-to-cross ravine, descending toward the sea and enclosed for the most part by cliffs and sheer rocks.

On the side sloping toward the sea, on the level ground below, lie the trading quarter and the suburb, strongly fortified.

Likewise the whole body of the city is secured by expensive walls and adorned in splendid fashion with temples and impressive buildings.

There is only one approach on the sea side, an artificial stair-like ascent broken up by frequent turns and awkward steps.

Not far from the city the river called the Orontes empties into the sea. It takes its source in the region of Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon and, after traversing the plain called Amyce, reaches Antioch.

Flowing through that city and carrying off all its human refuse by the force of its current, it finally discharges into the sea just mentioned not far from Seleucia.

# Book 5 Chapter 60: Capture of Seleucia

Antiochus first sent messages to the officials of the city, offering money and many inducements if they would hand over Seleucia without danger.

When he could not persuade the men in chief authority, he corrupted some of the subordinate commanders; trusting in them, he prepared his forces so that the assault from the sea side would be made by the fleet, and that from the landward side by the troops from the camp.

So he divided the army into three parts, encouraged them with words suited to the moment, and publicly proclaimed large rewards and crowns for bravery, both for common soldiers and for officers.

He assigned to Zeuxis and his men the positions by the gate leading toward Antioch; to Hermogenes the sector near the Dioscurium; and to Ardys and Diognetus he entrusted the assaults on the dockyards and the suburb.

For he had made such agreements with certain men inside that, if he took the suburb by force, the city itself would then be handed over to him.

When the signal was given, all of them together and from every side pressed the attack with energy and violence; but the men with Ardys and Diognetus attacked with the greatest daring.

For at the other points the ground did not really admit an assault by scaling ladders at all, unless men somehow clung and scrambled upward on all fours; but the dockyards and the suburb did allow ladders to be brought up, planted, and leaned securely against them.

So, while the men from the fleet fixed their ladders against the dockyards and Ardys' men against the suburb and pressed the attack hard, and while those inside the city could not bring help to these places because danger surrounded them everywhere at once, the suburb quickly fell into the hands of Ardys and his men.

As soon as it was taken, the corrupted subordinate officers ran to Leontius, the chief commander, and urged him to send envoys and come to terms with Antiochus before the city was stormed outright.

Leontius, unaware that the commanders had been corrupted but alarmed by their panic, sent men to offer guarantees and arrange for the safety of everyone in the city.
