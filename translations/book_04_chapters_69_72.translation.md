# Book 4 Chapter 69: Euripidas Deserts the Eleans

...were gathering under arms. As the vanguard of the Macedonians reached the top of the pass near the place called Apelaurus—which lies about ten stades in front of the city of Stymphalus—it happened that at that very moment the vanguard of the Eleans also came up onto the same pass.

Euripidas, realizing from the reports brought to him what had occurred, took some of the cavalry with him, slipped away from the immediate crisis, and made his retreat to Psophis by the trackless cross-country routes.

The rest of the Elean host, abandoned by their commander and thrown into panic by what had happened, halted on the march, at a loss what to do and which way to turn.

At first their officers supposed that the troops they saw were some of the Achaeans who had come up to lend support; and what deceived them most of all were the bronze-shielded men.

They imagined these to be Megalopolitans, because the soldiers of that city had carried such arms in the battle near Sellasia against Cleomenes, when King Antigonus had equipped them for the occasion.

For this reason they withdrew in good order toward some higher ground that commanded the position, not yet despairing of getting away safely. But as soon as the advancing Macedonians drew close to them and they grasped the truth of the matter, they all threw down their arms and rushed into flight.

About twelve hundred of them were taken alive, while the rest of the host was destroyed—some by the Macedonians, others over the cliffs—and no more than a hundred escaped.

Philip sent the spoils and the prisoners off to Corinth and pressed on with the business in hand.

To all the Peloponnesians what had happened seemed beyond belief, for they learned of the king's arrival and of his victory in one and the same moment.

# Book 4 Chapter 70: Philip In Arcadia

Marching through Arcadia and enduring heavy snow and great hardship in the passes around Mount Olygyrtus, he came down on the third day, by night, to Caphyae.

After resting his force there for two days and taking on Aratus the younger together with the Achaeans he had gathered—so that the whole force came to about ten thousand men—he advanced through the territory of Clitor toward Psophis, collecting missiles and scaling ladders from the towns he passed through.

Psophis is, by common agreement, an ancient foundation of the Arcadians, belonging to the district of Azanis. With respect to the Peloponnese as a whole it lies in the interior, but with respect to Arcadia itself it stands on the western frontier, bordering on those who dwell at the farthest edges of the western Achaeans.

It is also well placed to overlook the territory of the Eleans, with whom at that time it happened to share a political alliance.

Reaching it on the third day from Caphyae, Philip encamped on the hills that rise opposite the city, from which he could safely survey both the whole town and the country round about it.

But when the king observed the strength of Psophis, he was at a loss what to do.

Along its western side rushes a violent winter torrent, which for the greater part of the winter is impassable, and which in any case makes the city secure and hard to approach because of the size of the ravine that its waters have gradually carved out over time as they sweep down from the higher ground.

Along its eastern side it has the Erymanthus, a great and violent river about which much has been said by many writers.

Since the torrent flows into the Erymanthus on the southern side of the city, the result is that three of its faces are enclosed by the rivers and protected in the manner described.

On the remaining side, that toward the north, there rises a strong fortified hill, which serves as a well-placed and serviceable citadel; and the city also has walls remarkable for their size and construction.

On top of all this, a relieving force from the Eleans had come into the town, and Euripidas, having survived his flight, was now inside it.

# Book 4 Chapter 71: Philip Captures Psophis

Taking all of this in and weighing it up, Philip was in part dissuaded by his calculations from forcing and besieging the city, yet in part eager for it, seeing the strategic advantage of the place.

For in proportion as it then bore down upon the Achaeans and Arcadians and served the Eleans as a secure base of war, so, if it were taken in turn, it would shield the Arcadians and become a well-placed jumping-off point for the allies against the Eleans.

Having therefore set his mind on this course, he ordered all the Macedonians to take their breakfast at first light and to stand ready in full equipment.

After this he crossed the bridge over the Erymanthus, and since no one stood in his way—so unexpected was the move—he came right up to the city itself with energy and to terrifying effect.

Euripidas and all those in the city were thrown into confusion by what was happening, for they had been convinced that the enemy would neither dare to attack and storm so strong a city in a single onset, nor be able to mount a prolonged siege, given the difficulty of the season.

And as they reasoned thus, they grew distrustful of one another, fearing that Philip had arranged some treachery against the city through men within it.

But when they saw nothing of the kind arising among themselves, the greater part rushed to the walls to defend them, while the Elean mercenaries went out by a gate on the higher ground, meaning to fall upon the enemy.

The king arranged the men who were to bring the ladders against the wall at three points, distributed the rest of the Macedonians likewise among them, and then, giving each party the signal by trumpet, launched the assault on the walls from every side at once.

At first the defenders of the city resisted gallantly and hurled many men down from the ladders.

But when the supply of missiles and of the other things needed ran short—as was bound to happen, since the preparations had been made on the spur of the moment—and the Macedonians, far from being daunted by what was happening, kept replacing each man thrown from the ladders, the next coming up without hesitation into his place,

at last the men of the city broke and all fled toward the citadel. Of the king's troops, the Macedonians mounted the wall, while the Cretans closed with the mercenaries who had sallied out by the upper gate and forced them to throw down their arms and flee in utter disorder.

Pressing upon them and laying hands on them, the Cretans burst in through the gate along with them; and so it came about that the city was taken from every side at once.

The Psophidians, then, withdrew with their children and wives into the citadel, and with them Euripidas and his men, and likewise the rest of the survivors.

# Book 4 Chapter 72: The People of Psophis Surrender

The Macedonians, pouring in, at once plundered all the household goods from the houses, and after that quartered themselves in the dwellings and held the city.

Those who had taken refuge in the citadel, having no provisions of any kind, foresaw what was coming and decided to surrender themselves to Philip.

So they sent a herald to the king, and having obtained leave to send an embassy, they dispatched their magistrates and Euripidas along with them; these men made a truce and secured safety for all who had taken refuge there, foreigners and citizens alike.

The envoys then returned to the place from which they had set out, carrying instructions to stay where they were until the army should march off, so that none of the soldiers might disobey and plunder them.

The king, however, was forced by a fall of snow to remain on the spot for some days. During this time he gathered the Achaeans who were present and first pointed out to them the strength and strategic value of the city for the present war.

He then recounted the goodwill and devotion he felt toward their nation, and ended by declaring that he now ceded and gave the city to the Achaeans; for it was his settled aim to grant them what favors he could and to fall short in nothing of his zeal for them.

When Aratus and his men, along with the rest of the gathering, thanked him for this, Philip dismissed the assembly, set his army in motion, and marched toward Lasion.

The Psophidians came down from the citadel and recovered their city, each man his own dwelling, while Euripidas and his men departed to Corinth and from there to Aetolia.

Of the Achaean magistrates who were present, they set Prolaus of Sicyon over the citadel with an adequate garrison, and Pythias of Pallene over the city.

Such was the conclusion of the affair at Psophis.
