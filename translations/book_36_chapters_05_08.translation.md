# Book 36 Chapter 5: Speech of Mago Brettius

At this moment, they say that Mago Brettius spoke with manliness and sound judgment.

He said that, as it seemed, the Carthaginians had two occasions on which to deliberate about themselves and their country, and that they had let one of them pass by.

For now, by God, there was no point in wondering what the consuls would order them to do, or why the Senate had made no mention of the city itself, but had spoken only when they were surrendering themselves.

Once they had done that, they must clearly understand that they would have to accept every order, unless it turned out to be utterly outrageous and beyond expectation.

If that was not the course they chose, then they must consider once more whether they would rather await war in their own land and suffer whatever horrors it might bring, or, in fear of the enemy's approach, submit willingly to whatever commands were imposed.

Since everyone, because of the war at hand and the uncertainty of the future, was inclined to obey what was ordered, it was decided to send the hostages to Lilybaeum.

Accordingly they immediately selected three hundred of the young men and sent them off with loud lamentation and tears, every one being accompanied by his nearest relatives and friends, and the women especially setting the whole scene ablaze with grief.

When they reached Lilybaeum, the consuls handed them over to Quintus Fabius Maximus, who happened to be the commander in Sicily at that time.

He conveyed them safely to Rome and had them all confined together in the dockyard of the six-banked ships.

# Book 36 Chapter 6: The Roman Army In Africa

Once the hostages had been disposed of there, the generals brought their fleet to the citadel of Utica.

When this reached Carthage, the city was in a state of utter tension and fear because no one knew what to expect next.

Nevertheless, they decided to send envoys to learn from the consuls what they should do, and to make clear that everyone was ready to obey whatever was ordered.

When the envoys reached the Roman camp and the council had been summoned, they entered and spoke according to their instructions.

The senior consul praised their purpose and willingness to comply and ordered them to hand over all their weapons and missiles without trickery or deceit.

The envoys said they would do what had been ordered, but asked them to consider what would happen if they gave up their weapons and the Romans then took them and sailed away from the country. Nevertheless, they surrendered them.

Thus it became clear how vast the city's resources were: they handed over to the Romans more than two hundred thousand weapons and two thousand catapults.

# Book 36 Chapter 7: Panic at the Envoys' Report

In short, they had no idea at all what was about to be demanded of them.

But from the very look on the envoys' faces they suspected the worst and immediately fell into every kind of lamentation and crying.

All the senators, crying out in horror, were left as though paralyzed by the shock.

Then, as the report quickly spread through the people, there was no more restraint: some rushed at the envoys, as though they were the guilty cause of the city's disasters,

others turned their anger on the Italians who had been seized in the city, and still others hurried toward the gates of the city.

# Book 36 Chapter 8: Character of Hamilcar Phameas

Hamilcar, also called Phameas, the Carthaginian general, was in the prime of life, strong in body, and, most importantly for war, an excellent and daring horseman.

Those who were jealous of Scipio tried to disparage his actions.

Phameas, however, seeing the Roman pickets, was not without courage; still, he avoided close fighting with Scipio. On one occasion he drew near the reserves, took up position behind the brow of a hill, and remained there for a considerable time.

The Roman maniples withdrew to a hill; and when everyone had given his opinion, Scipio said:

"When men are deliberating from scratch, they should take even greater care to avoid suffering harm than to do harm to their enemies."

It should not be surprising that I give a more detailed account of Scipio's actions and report everything he said with precision.

For when Marcus Porcius Cato heard in Rome of Scipio's achievements, he said, in effect: "What have you heard? He alone has the breath of wisdom in him; the rest are but shadows."
