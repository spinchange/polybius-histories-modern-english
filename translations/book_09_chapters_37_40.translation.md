# Book 9 Chapter 37: A Mightly Cloud is Coming From the West

Let what I have said be granted for the moment, even if stricter judges decide it lies outside the immediate issue. I will return to the central point, as these men define it.

Their point was this: if circumstances now are the same as when you made your alliance with them, then your own policy ought to remain as it is now; for that was their starting principle.

But if the situation has been completely altered, then it is just that you should now deliberate afresh about what is being urged upon you.

So I ask you, Cleonicus and Chlaeneas, what allies did you have when you first invited these men into joint action? Was it not all the Greeks?

And with whom do you now share your hopes, or into what alliance are you now calling them?

Is it not into an alliance with barbarians? You seem to think the present circumstances are exactly like the former ones, though in fact they are the opposite.

Then you were contending for leadership and prestige against the Achaeans and Macedonians, men of your own race, and against Philip their leader. But now war confronts the Greeks over the issue of slavery, against men of another race.

You think you are drawing these people in against Philip, but you do not realize that you have drawn them in against yourselves and against all Greece.

For just as men in wartime, by bringing into their cities garrisons stronger than their own forces for the sake of safety, both ward off the fear that comes from enemies and at the same time make themselves subject to the power of their friends, so the Aetolians are acting now.

Desiring to prevail over Philip and humble the Macedonians, they have failed to notice that they have drawn in from the west so great a cloud that, for the present, it may first cast its shadow over Macedonia, but in the end will become the cause of great evils for all Greece.

# Book 9 Chapter 38: Contrast of the Aetolian Policy

All Greeks, then, ought to foresee the crisis that is coming, but the Lacedaemonians above all.

For why do you suppose, men of Lacedaemon, that when Xerxes sent an envoy to you demanding earth and water, your ancestors threw him into a well and, tossing earth on top of him, told him to report back to Xerxes that from the Lacedaemonians he had received exactly what he demanded—earth and water?

And why again did Leonidas and his men set out willingly and openly to die?

Was it not so that they might seem to risk themselves in advance not only for their own freedom, but also for that of the rest of the Greeks?

It would indeed be worthy of the descendants of such men, and now in your present condition, to make an alliance with barbarians, march with them, and make war on Epirotes, Achaeans, Acarnanians, Boeotians, Thessalians—in short, on nearly all the Greeks except the Aetolians.

For the Aetolians it is their custom to do such things and to regard nothing as shameful so long as there is some profit in it. But that is not your way.

And what are we to expect these men to do now that they have taken the Romans into alliance?

Why, when they gained strength and support from the Illyrians, they at once set about piratical violence at sea and seized Pylus in violation of treaty, while on land they besieged the city of Cleitor and enslaved the people of Cynaetha.

Earlier too, as I said above, these same men made agreements with Antigonus against the Achaean and Acarnanian peoples; and now they have made an agreement with the Romans against all Greece.

# Book 9 Chapter 39: Sparta Must Be On Guard Against Attack from Rome

Who, on hearing these facts, would not both be suspicious of the Roman advance and hate the recklessness of the Aetolians for daring to make such treaties?

Already they have stripped the Acarnanians of Oeniadae and Nesus; and quite recently they seized the city of the unfortunate Anticyreans, enslaving it together with the Romans.

The Romans carry off the children and women, who will plainly suffer the usual fate of those who fall under the power of foreigners; while the Aetolians inherit the lands of the unfortunate victims.

A fine alliance indeed this is to join by deliberate choice—especially for Lacedaemonians,

who once decreed that the Thebans, because alone among the Greeks they had resolved to remain quiet during the Persian invasion, should pay a tithe to the gods after the barbarians had been defeated in war.

The honorable and fitting course for you, men of Lacedaemon, is to remember your ancestors, to beware the Roman advance, to suspect the wicked enterprise of the Aetolians, and above all to remember what Antigonus did for you; then even now show your hatred of baseness, turn away from friendship with the Aetolians, and share the same hopes as the Achaeans and Macedonians.

But if some of the more powerful men among you oppose this, then at least incline toward neutrality and do not take part in the wrongdoing of these Aetolians.

# Book 9 Chapter 40: Philip in Thessaly

For the Athenian city always wishes to preserve that kind of character.

For zeal shown by friends at the right moment gives great help, but if it drags behind and comes too late it makes assistance altogether useless.

At least if they wish to keep their alliance not merely in writing, but in deeds as well...

The Acarnanians, when they learned of the Aetolian attack against them, partly despairing in their expectations and partly fighting with their anger, came to a terrible resolve.

If any man, left alive, should fail to die but instead flee the danger, he was not to be admitted into any city or given fire to kindle.

And they uttered curses against all alike, but most of all against the Epirotes, so that no one who fled should be received in their land.
