Conditions in Aetolia were in good order, since civil strife there had been extinguished after the death of Lyciscus.

And when Mnasippus of Coroneia also died, the situation in Boeotia improved; the same happened in Acarnania when Chremas was removed from the scene.

For it was almost as though some purification of Greece had taken place, when its accursed men passed out of the world of the living.

In that same year Charops of Epirus also happened to die at Brundisium.

But affairs in Epirus were still unsettled and troubled, as they had been in the preceding years, because of the brutality and lawlessness of Charops, from the time when the war with Perseus was brought to an end.

For after Lucius Anicius and Lucius Aemilius had condemned some of the leading men to death and carried others off to Rome, all those who had come under even the slightest suspicion,

Charops then obtained full power to do whatever he wished, and there was no outrage he did not commit,

some by his own hand and others through his friends, since he himself was still very young, and the worst and most reckless men had gathered around him because they could enrich themselves from other people's property.

And what served as a reserve force and gave weight to the belief that he was acting with some justification and with Roman approval was his previous association with the men already mentioned, and besides them with Myrton, an elderly man, and his son Nicanor,

men otherwise moderate in character and thought to be friends of Rome, who for a long time had kept themselves far from every sort of injustice, but somehow at that moment gave themselves over to strengthening and sharing in the lawless acts of Charops.

After this man had openly murdered some in the marketplace, killed others in their own houses, and had still others assassinated in the countryside and on the roads by sending agents after them, and after he had seized the estates of all the dead, he introduced another scheme.

He posted up as exiles those whose property made them worth targeting, not only men but women as well.

By keeping this fear alive, he continually extorted money, dealing with the men himself and the women through his mother Philotis.

She too proved highly suited to the role, and was able to assist in acts of violence with more force than one would expect from a woman.

After they had squeezed as much money as possible out of everyone, men and women alike, they nevertheless brought all those who had been proscribed before the people.

And most of the people in Phoenice, partly out of fear and partly because they had been won over by Charops and his circle, condemned all those who had been denounced, not to exile but to death, on the charge that their sympathies were alien to Rome.

So all these people fled, while Charops set out for Rome, taking money with him and bringing along Myrton and his associates, because he wanted to have his lawlessness sealed with the senate's approval.

At that moment there appeared both a splendid example of Roman policy and a splendid sight for all the Greeks who were staying there, especially for those who had been summoned.

For Marcus, who was chief priest and was enrolled first in the senate, and Lucius, the conqueror of Perseus, who possessed the greatest credit and influence, when they learned what Charops had done in Epirus, forbade Charops to enter their houses.

When this became widely known, all the visiting Greeks were overjoyed, applauding the Romans' hatred of wickedness.

After this, when Charops entered the senate, it did not agree to what he was asking and did not wish to give a definite reply,

but said that it would give instructions to the envoys who were being sent out to examine what had happened.

But Charops, after withdrawing, concealed that answer, and instead wrote home a report adapted to his own interests, saying that the Romans approved what he was doing.

# Book 32 Chapter 7: Isocrates the Grammarian

Olympiad 155, year 2. Affairs in Italy.

Envoys came from the Athenians, and from the Achaeans came Thearidas and Stephanus on behalf of the Delians.

For after the Romans had given the Delians the answer that, once Delos had been granted to the Athenians, they themselves must withdraw from the island,

though they might carry away their possessions, the Delians moved to Achaea, obtained citizenship there, and wished to obtain justice from the Athenians under the agreement between the Athenians and the Achaeans.

But since the Athenians claimed that this jurisdiction did not apply to them, the Delians asked the Achaeans for the right of reprisal against the Athenians.

It was on this matter that they had come on embassy at that time, and they received the answer that the arrangements lawfully made by the Achaeans concerning the Delians were to remain valid.

# Book 32 Chapter 8: Legacy of L. Aemilius Paulus

II. Affairs at Pergamum.

King Eumenes was crippled in bodily strength, but by the brilliance of his spirit he still held out,

a man who in most respects was second to none of the kings of his own age, and in the greatest and noblest matters was greater and more distinguished than they were.

For in the first place, when he inherited from his father a kingdom narrowed down to a few small and meager towns, he made his own power a match for the greatest dynasties of his age,

relying not chiefly on fortune or on sudden reversals, but on intelligence, hard work, and his own action.

Second, he showed the greatest eagerness for honor, and benefited more Greek cities than any of the kings of his time, while also attaching more private individuals personally to himself.

Third, although he had three brothers close to him in age and active in affairs, he kept all of them obedient to him, serving as his supporters and preserving the dignity of the kingship.

Such a thing one would rarely find to have happened.
