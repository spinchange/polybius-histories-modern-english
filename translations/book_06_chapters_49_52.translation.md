# Book 6 Chapter 49: The Defect in the Spartan Constitution

Who does not know that the Spartans were among the first Greeks to cast covetous eyes on their neighbors' land, and for sheer greed undertook a war to enslave the Messenians?

And who has not read that, out of stubborn rivalry, they swore not to raise the siege until they had taken Messene by force?

It is equally well known to everyone that, out of their passion for supremacy in Greece, they later submitted to take orders from the very people whom they had once defeated in battle.

For when the Persians invaded, they fought and won in defense of Greek freedom.

But after the invaders had gone back and fled, the Spartans handed over the Greek cities to them in the Peace of Antalcidas, in order to get money for their domination over Greece.

It was then that the defect in their legislation became visible.

As long as they aimed only at ruling their immediate neighbors, and later the Peloponnesians themselves, the resources and supplies of Laconia were enough for them. They had provisions ready at hand and could make rapid returns home and easy transport of supplies.

But once they undertook to send fleets overseas and to campaign with armies outside the Peloponnese, it became obvious that neither their iron currency nor the exchange of yearly produce, in accordance with Lycurgus' laws, could supply what they lacked.

Such enterprises required a currency accepted everywhere and provisions from abroad.

So they were compelled to stand at Persian doors, impose tribute on the islanders, and levy silver from all the Greeks, having learned that under Lycurgus' legislation it was impossible not only to claim the leadership of Greece but, in fact, to compete for great affairs at all.

# Book 6 Chapter 50: Sparta Compared with Rome

Why, then, have I gone into this digression? So that the facts themselves may make clear that Lycurgus' legislation is sufficient for the secure preservation of a people's own territory and for maintaining its freedom.

And for those who regard that as the proper end of a constitution, it must be granted that no arrangement has ever existed, or does now exist, more choiceworthy than the Spartan order and system.

But if someone aims at greater things, and thinks it finer and nobler to lead many peoples, to prevail over them and rule them, and to have all men look toward him and depend on him,

then he must admit that the Spartan constitution is deficient in this respect, while the Roman constitution is superior and possesses a stronger organization for power.

This too has been made plain by events themselves. When the Spartans set out to win the leadership of Greece, they quickly put even their own freedom at risk.

The Romans, by contrast, once they had secured dominion over Italy, in a short time brought the whole inhabited world under their power, and a great part of their success came from the abundance and ready availability of their supplies.

# Book 6 Chapter 51: Carthage Compared with Rome

The Carthaginian constitution, in its original form, seems to me to have been well framed in its main divisions.

They had kings, their council of elders possessed aristocratic authority, and the people held power in the matters that belonged to them. In general, the fitting together of the whole closely resembled that of Rome and Sparta.

But at the time when Carthage entered the war with Hannibal, the Carthaginian constitution was already worse, while the Roman was better.

For every body, constitution, and undertaking has a natural growth, then a peak, and after that decline; and everything is strongest at its peak. That is exactly why these constitutions differed at that moment.

Carthage had become strong and prosperous earlier than Rome; and by just so much, at that time Carthage was already past its prime, while Rome, so far as its constitution was concerned, was then at its very height.

Accordingly, in Carthage the people had already gained the greater share of power in deliberations, while at Rome the senate was still in its prime.

So where policy was being debated by the many in the one state, and by the best men in the other, Roman deliberation proved stronger in public affairs.

That is why, even after suffering grave disasters, they finally overcame the Carthaginians in the war through the soundness of their counsels.

# Book 6 Chapter 52: The Causes of Roman Superiority

If we look at particular matters, such as the provisions for war, the Carthaginians are naturally better practiced and better prepared for naval service, since from ancient times that skill has been hereditary among them and they are more devoted to the sea than any other people.

But in land warfare the Romans train themselves far more effectively than the Carthaginians.

The Romans devote their whole effort to this; the Carthaginians neglect their infantry altogether and take only slight thought even for their cavalry.

The reason is that the Carthaginians use foreign and mercenary forces, whereas the Romans use native troops and citizens.

In this respect the Roman constitution deserves the preference over the Carthaginian. The one rests its hopes of freedom on the courage of mercenaries; the Roman state rests them on the valor of its own citizens and the support of its allies.

That is why, even if they suffer an initial defeat, the Romans recover and continue the war, whereas the Carthaginians do the opposite.

For the Romans fight for their homeland and their children, and they can never let their anger slacken, but persist with all their spirit until they overcome their enemies.

So although the Romans, as I said before, are far inferior in naval experience, they nevertheless prevail overall because of the courage of their men.

For even though seamanship contributes a great deal in fighting at sea, the bravery of the marines weighs most heavily in deciding victory.

Indeed, all the peoples of Italy are by nature superior to Phoenicians and Libyans alike in bodily strength and in daring; and beyond that, their training powerfully encourages the young toward such conduct.

One example will be enough to show how seriously the Roman constitution is concerned with producing men willing to endure anything in order to win the reputation for courage in their own country.
