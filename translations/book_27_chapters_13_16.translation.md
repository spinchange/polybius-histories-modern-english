# Book 27 Chapter 13: Indecisive Result of the First Campaign

II. Affairs in Egypt.

Ptolemy, the commander in Cyprus, was not Egyptian in his ways at all, but a sensible and capable man.

For when he took over the island, while the king was still an infant, he applied himself diligently to raising money, but he gave absolutely nothing to anyone, though the royal administrators often asked him for funds and bitterly criticized him for never releasing anything.

But when the king came of age, he gathered together a considerable sum of money and sent it off.

As a result, King Ptolemy himself and the courtiers approved of his former frugality and of his refusal to part with any money.

# Book 27 Chapter 14: Dispute at Rhodes

Olympiad 152, year 2. The war with Perseus.

At the time when Perseus broke off operations in the war against the Romans, Antenor arrived from Perseus to negotiate the ransom of the prisoners who had sailed with Diophanes, and a serious division arose among the Rhodian statesmen over what ought to be done.

The party of Philophron and Theaetetus was completely opposed to entering into such an entanglement, while the party of Deinon and Polyaratus favored it.

In the end they came to an arrangement with Perseus for the redemption of the prisoners.

# Book 27 Chapter 15: Charops of Epirus

Cephalus came from Epirus. He had previously had ties with the Macedonian royal house, but was then compelled by circumstances to choose the side of Perseus. The cause of this was as follows.

There was an Epirote named Charops, a man of fine character and a friend of the Romans, who, when Philip had occupied the passes into Epirus, was responsible for driving Philip out of Epirus and for enabling Titus to gain control of both Epirus and the Macedonians.

This man had a son, Machatas, whose own son was Charops.

When the father died while this boy was still under age, the elder Charops sent him to Rome with the protection appropriate to his station, so that he might learn the Roman language and letters.

After some time, this young man returned home, having become acquainted with many people there.

The elder Charops then died. The young man, unstable by nature and full of every kind of wickedness, became puffed up and attached himself to the prominent men.

At first no one paid him any attention, but the men who held the leading place both in age and in reputation, the party around Antinous, managed public affairs according to their own judgment. But when the war with Perseus broke out,

this young man at once began to slander the men just mentioned before the Romans, using as his pretext their earlier connection with the Macedonian royal house,

while in present circumstances he watched everything closely and interpreted every word and action of theirs in the worst possible way, suppressing some facts and adding others, and so made his accusations against them convincing.

Cephalus, however, was in every other respect a prudent and steady man, and at this crisis too he held to the soundest judgment.

At the outset he prayed to the gods that the war might not break out and that matters might not be decided by force.

But once the war was actually under way, he wished to fulfill Rome's claims under the alliance fairly, while beyond that he did not want to run to them in a servile way or render any service beyond what was proper.

When Charops actively employed his slanders against him and treated everything done contrary to Roman wishes as deliberate malice, the men already mentioned at first ignored him, since they were conscious of no plans hostile to Rome.

But when they saw the Aetolians Hippolochus, Nicander, and Lochagus being carried off to Rome without cause after the cavalry action, and when they saw that the accusations brought against them by the party of Lyciscus had been believed, men who in Aetolia represented the same political tendency that Charops did in Epirus, then, foreseeing what was coming, they began to take counsel for their own safety.

They therefore resolved to try every possible means to prevent themselves from being carried off to Rome without a hearing because of Charops' slanders.

Thus Cephalus and his friends were forced, contrary to their own preference, to choose the side of Perseus.

# Book 27 Chapter 16: Plot to Kidnap a Roman Consul

The party of Theodotus and Philostratus committed an act that was openly impious and a clear breach of faith.

For when they learned that the Roman consul Aulus Hostilius was coming, on his way to the camp in Thessaly, and thought that, if they delivered Aulus to Perseus, they would both give the strongest proof of loyalty and do the Romans the greatest harm at that moment, they kept writing to Perseus and urging him to hurry.

The king wanted to advance at once and join them, but since the Molossians had occupied the bridge over the river Aous, he was prevented from making the attempt and was first forced to fight them.

It happened that when Aulus arrived among the Phanoteis he lodged with Nestor of Cropia, and thus gave his enemies just the opportunity they needed against him; and if some turn of fortune had not decided matters for the better, I do not think he would have escaped.

But as it was, Nestor somehow, with almost divine foresight, perceived what was coming and forced him to move on from there to Gitana during the night.

So, abandoning the overland route through Epirus, he put to sea and sailed to Anticyra, and from there made his way into Thessaly.
