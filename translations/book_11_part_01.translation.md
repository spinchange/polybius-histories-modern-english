# Book 11 Chapter 1: Death of Hasdrubal

Hasdrubal's arrival in Italy proved much easier and quicker than had been expected.

So Rome was never at any other time more tense or more terrified, as it waited to see what would happen.

None of this pleased Hasdrubal; but since circumstances no longer allowed any withdrawal, because he saw the enemy already drawn up and advancing, he was forced to form his Iberians and the Gauls who were with him in battle order.

He placed the ten elephants in front, deepened his lines, compressed his whole force into a narrow space, and stationed himself in the center of the formation under the protection of the elephants, advancing against the enemy's left with the settled resolve that in the present danger he must either win or die.

Livius advanced against the enemy in earnest and fought bravely once the armies had joined.

Claudius, who had been posted on the right wing, could neither move forward nor outflank the opposition because of the difficult ground in front, and it was in reliance on this ground that Hasdrubal had made his attack on the Roman left.

Unable to act and troubled by his inaction, he learned from the situation itself what he ought to do.

So he took the troops from his own right wing, marched them behind the battle line, passed beyond the Roman left, and attacked the Carthaginians near the elephants on the flank.

Up to this point victory hung in the balance. Both sides fought with equal desperation, because the Romans knew that if they failed they had no hope of safety, while the Iberians and Carthaginians were in the same case; and the elephants served both sides alike in the battle.

Caught in the middle and pelted with missiles, they threw both the Roman and the Iberian lines into confusion.

But as soon as Claudius' men fell on the enemy from the rear, the battle was no longer equal, since the Iberians were being pressed from the front by some and from behind by others.

As a result, most of the Iberians were cut down where they stood in the very crisis of the fight.

Six of the elephants fell together with their riders, and the other four, after breaking through the ranks, were later captured by themselves, stripped of their Indian drivers.

# Book 11 Chapter 1a: Chapter 1a

Some may perhaps ask why in this book I have not prefaced the narrative with summaries, as earlier writers did, but have instead set out an introduction to the events for each Olympiad.

In my judgment the use of summaries is indeed valuable. They help readers understand, attract and encourage those who come upon the work, and make it easy to find whatever one is looking for.

But since I see that this kind of prefatory summary is often neglected and loses its force for many reasons, I was led to adopt this other method.

For the introductory survey is not merely as useful as a summary but can do even more, and it also has a safer place because it is woven into the history itself.

Therefore I decided to use this method throughout the whole work, except in the first six books. In those I used summaries, because that sort of introduction did not fit them very well.

# Book 11 Chapter 2: Hasdrubal's Conduct in His Last Battle

Hasdrubal showed himself a brave man both before this and in his final hour, and he ended his life fighting hand to hand. That deserves notice and should not be passed over in silence.

That he was Hannibal's own brother, and that when Hannibal went to Italy he entrusted the affairs in Iberia to him, I have already explained earlier.

I have also shown how he fought many struggles against the Romans and wrestled with many varied difficulties because of the generals sent out from Carthage to Iberia, and how through all these reverses and setbacks he continued to bear himself nobly and with courage worthy of the son of Barca. But now I will speak of his last contests,

for in these above all he seems to me to deserve close attention and even imitation.

For one may observe that most generals and kings, when they undertake decisive struggles, keep before their eyes only the glory and advantage to be won by success, and often pause to consider how they will use each success if events turn out as they wish.

But they do not set before themselves the consequences of failure, nor do they consider what each must do in a reversal. Yet the first is easy, while the second requires much foresight.

Therefore most men, through weakness of spirit and poor judgment in such moments, make their defeats shameful, even when their soldiers have often fought bravely; they disgrace what they had accomplished before and leave behind a life marked by reproach.

Since many leaders fail precisely in this point, and since the difference between one man and another here is very great, anyone who wishes can learn it readily; for past history has provided many examples.

Hasdrubal, by contrast, so long as any reasonable hope remained that he could do something worthy of his earlier achievements, took no greater thought in danger than for his own preservation.

But when Fortune had shut him in at the last extremity and stripped away every hope for the future, then, though he omitted nothing either in preparation or in battle that might secure victory, he took just as much thought for how, if he failed, he might confront the event in a way worthy of all that he had done before and endure nothing unworthy of his former life.

Let these remarks stand for those engaged in action, so that they may neither, by reckless courting of danger, ruin the hopes of those who trusted them, nor, by clinging to life beyond what is honorable, make their disasters shameful.

# Book 11 Chapter 3: The Romans Celebrate a Victory

After winning the battle, the Romans at once plundered the enemy camp, and they slaughtered many of the Celts in their sleeping-places, drunk and helpless like sacrificial victims.

They also gathered the rest of the captured booty, from which more than three hundred talents were brought into the public treasury.

Of the Carthaginians and Celts together, not fewer than ten thousand fell in the battle, while about two thousand Romans died. Some distinguished Carthaginians were taken alive, but the rest were destroyed.

When the report reached Rome, at first they could not believe it, so strongly did they want it to be true.

But when more messengers arrived, making clear not only that the event had happened but the details of it as well, then the city was filled with overwhelming joy, every sacred precinct was adorned, and every temple was crowded with cakes and sacrificial victims.

In general they were brought to such confidence and courage that all thought Hannibal, whom they had feared more than anything before, could no longer even remain in Italy.

Yet another declared that such words had only the appearance of truth, while the reality was otherwise—indeed the opposite.
