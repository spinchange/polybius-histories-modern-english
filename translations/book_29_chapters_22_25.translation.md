# Book 29 Chapter 22: Further Problems for Eumenes

After the battle between Perseus and the Romans had been brought to an end, King Eumenes fell into what seemed, to most people, a strange and unexpected situation; though in truth, if one looks at the course human affairs usually take, it was nothing out of the ordinary.

For Fortune is quite able to upset reasoned plans, and even when she has helped a man and added her own weight to his side, she can turn around again, as if repenting, and immediately spoil what has been achieved.

That is exactly what happened then in Eumenes' case.

For just when he believed that his own rule was now secure and that the coming years would be easy, since Perseus and the whole Macedonian kingdom had been utterly overthrown, he suddenly ran into the gravest danger, because the Galatians in Asia unexpectedly took advantage of the moment and rose up.

# Book 29 Chapter 23: The Ptolemies Ask Help From Achaia

In the Peloponnese, during the winter, ambassadors arrived from both kings — Ptolemy and Ptolemy — asking for help, and this led to repeated and highly spirited debates.

The party around Callicrates, Diophanes, and Hyperbatus did not like the idea of granting aid.

But Archon, Lycortas, and Polybius thought it right to give the kings help in accordance with the existing alliance.

For by then it had happened that the younger Ptolemy had been proclaimed king by the crowd because of the crisis, while the elder had come back from Memphis and was ruling jointly with his brother.

Since they were asking for every kind of support, they sent Eumenes and Dionysodorus to the Achaeans, requesting one thousand foot soldiers and two hundred cavalry, with Lycortas to command the infantry and Polybius the cavalry.

They also sent word to Theodoridas of Sicyon, asking him to raise a body of one thousand mercenaries.

This happened because the kings had previously formed a close acquaintance with the men we have mentioned, as a result of the events already described.

When the envoys arrived, the Achaean congress was meeting at Corinth. They accordingly came forward and, after recalling the many proofs of goodwill the Achaeans had shown toward the Egyptian kingdom and setting out the danger the kings were then in, they asked them for help.

The majority of the Achaeans were ready to go to the kings' assistance — both because the kings at that time wore the diadem and exercised royal authority — and not merely with a detachment, but with their full levy; for the party around Callicrates objected,

saying that in general they should not meddle in such matters, and certainly not at that time, but should keep themselves entirely available for Roman needs.

For at that very moment there was especially expected to be a crisis over the whole situation, since Quintus Philippus was wintering in Macedonia.

# Book 29 Chapter 24: The Achaeans Agree to Help the Ptolemies

When many of the people became uncertain and feared that they might seem to fall short of Roman expectations, Lycortas and Polybius took up the argument again and taught them,

among other things, that in the previous year the Achaeans had voted to march with their full levy alongside the Romans and had sent Polybius as envoy, but Quintus, after accepting their goodwill, had declined the help, saying that he had no need of it because he had already secured the invasion route into Macedonia.

From this it was clear that their supposed concern for Roman needs was only a pretext for preventing aid from being sent.

So they urged the Achaeans not to overlook the present moment, seeing how great the danger then surrounding the kingdom was, but to keep faith with the agreements and benefactions, and above all with the oaths, by carrying out the treaty.

But when the majority again became inclined toward sending help, the party around Callicrates blocked the motion, intimidating the magistrates by claiming that under the laws it was not lawful to debate aid in the public assembly.

After some time, however, when the congress had been summoned to the city of the Sicyonians, where not only the council but all men over thirty were present, and after several speeches had been made,

Polybius in particular argued first that the Romans did not need help — and he seemed to be speaking not without good reason, since he had spent the previous summer in Macedonia in the camp of Philippus —

and second, that even if the Romans did need the alliance, the Achaeans would still not be unable to provide help because of the two hundred cavalry and one thousand infantry who were to be sent to Alexandria; for if they were acting wisely, they could field thirty or forty thousand fighting men.

The majority were won over by what they heard and leaned toward sending the allied force.

So on the second of the two days, on which by law those who wished to do so were required to present motions, Lycortas proposed that the aid should be sent, while Callicrates proposed that ambassadors should be dispatched to reconcile the two kings with Antiochus.

When these motions were put forward again, a lively contest followed, but the men around Lycortas clearly had the advantage.

For the comparison between the two kingdoms was very striking.

It was hard to find any act of friendship at all between Syria and the Greeks in earlier times, although the present king's generosity was well known among them;

whereas from Egypt there had been so many and such great acts of kindness toward the Achaeans in earlier times that no one could ask for more.

By presenting this contrast, Lycortas made a strong impression, since the difference between the two kingdoms on this point was so great.

For just as it was not easy to count the benefactions of the Alexandrian kings, so it was impossible to find any act of goodwill done by the dynasty of Antiochus for the Achaeans in the course of affairs.

# Book 29 Chapter 25: Antiochus Forced To Leave Egypt

For a time Andronidas and Callicrates kept using arguments in favor of making peace, but since no one was listening to them, they resorted to a trick.

A letter-carrier happened to come from the road into the theater, bringing a letter from Quintus Marcius, in which he urged the Achaeans, in line with Roman policy, to try to reconcile the kings.

This was also in fact what the Senate had done, sending envoys with Numisius to bring about a settlement between the kings.

But this actually told against their case: for the men with Titus had failed to achieve reconciliation and had returned to Rome completely unsuccessful.

Still, Polybius and his faction did not want to argue against the letter, out of consideration for Marcius, and withdrew from the debate. Thus the proposal to help the kings came to nothing.

The Achaeans then decided to send ambassadors to make the reconciliation, and Archon of Aegira, Arcesilaus, and Ariston of Megalopolis were appointed.

The envoys from Ptolemy, having failed to secure the alliance, then handed over to the magistrates the letter from the kings, which asked the Achaeans to send Lycortas and Polybius to take part in the present war.
