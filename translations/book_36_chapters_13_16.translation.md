# Book 36 Chapter 13: Chapter 13

Through an unusual coincidence, the images of Callicrates were being brought in under cover of darkness while those of Lycortas were on the same day being carried out into the light, exactly as the original arrangement had intended.

This compelled everyone to remark that one must never use prosperity insolently against one's neighbors, knowing that this is especially the habit of Fortune: to overturn, by one’s own devices and laws, the very men who established them.

The natural love of novelty in human beings is itself enough to bring about every kind of change.

# Book 36 Chapter 14: Chapter 14

The Romans sent envoys to restrain Nicomedes from his rashness and to prevent Attalus from making war on Prusias, and they appointed Marcus Licinius,

a man crippled by gout and completely unable to use his feet, then Aulus Maninus, who had such large scars across his head from a roof tile falling on him that it was astonishing he survived, and Lucius Malloelus, who was thought by all Romans to be the dullest of men.

Since the business required speed and boldness, those appointed seemed utterly unfitted for the task.

For this reason, it is said that Marcus Porcius Cato remarked in the Senate that Prusias would arrive not only already dead, but that Nicomedes too would grow old in his kingdom before the embassy could ever get there.

For how could they possibly move quickly, or what could their embassy accomplish if it had neither feet nor head nor heart?

# Book 36 Chapter 15: Chapter 15

King Prusias, though repellent in appearance and, by reasoned judgment, not without some better qualities, was in outward form only half a man and in military matters ignoble and womanish.

For he was not merely cowardly, but also a stranger to hardship and altogether unmanly in both body and soul throughout his whole life.

This is the last thing one wishes to find in kings, and especially in the nation of the Bithynians.

He was also accompanied by a great deal of wantonness in bodily appetites.

He was wholly untrained in education, philosophy, and the questions studied in those fields.

In short, he had no conception at all of what the good is, and lived a barbarous life like Sardanapalus, both by day and by night.

Accordingly, once the people under his rule caught a little hope, they immediately formed an unalterable resolve not only to think differently from the king, but also to seek punishment from him.

# Book 36 Chapter 16: Chapter 16

Masinissa, king of the Numidians in Libya, was the best and happiest of the kings of our own time.

He reigned for more than sixty years and was exceptionally healthy and long-lived, having reached ninety years of age.

He was also the strongest man of his time in bodily vigor: when he had to stand, he would stand on the same spot for a whole day, and when he sat down, he would not rise again.

He endured the hardships of cavalry service continuously, day and night, and suffered nothing from them.

A proof of his bodily strength: when he was ninety and about to die, he left behind a four-year-old son named Stembanus, whom Micypsa later adopted, along with nine other sons.

Because of the goodwill they had for one another, he preserved his whole life and left his kingdom free from every plot and every domestic stain.

But the greatest and most godlike thing about him was this: Numidia had previously been useless and was thought by nature incapable of producing cultivated crops,

yet he was the first and only one to show that it could yield all kinds of cultivated produce just as well, having set out, for each of his sons, extensive and fully fruitful estates in separate allotments.

One might fairly and rightly say this upon his death.

Scipio arrived at Cirta on the third day after the king's death and settled everything properly.

Polybius records that Masinissa died at ninety, leaving behind a four-year-old child who had been born to him.
