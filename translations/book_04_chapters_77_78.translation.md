# Book 4 Chapter 77: Phillidas and the Aetolian Troops Arrive

Philip, then, was winning a high reputation—both for his conduct in his dealings with the men who shared the open-field life of the camp, and for his action and daring in matters of war—not only among the soldiers serving with him, but among all the rest of the Peloponnesians as well.

For it would not be easy to find a king more abundantly furnished by nature with the resources needed for acquiring power.

He possessed, indeed, a keen intelligence, a strong memory, and an exceptional charm, and on top of these a kingly bearing and authority—and, most important of all, capacity and boldness in war.

But what it was that in the end overcame all these qualities, and turned a king of fine natural gifts into a savage tyrant, is not easy to explain in a few words. For that reason it will be more fitting to examine and work through these questions at some other occasion than the present one.

Philip, having broken camp at Olympia, took the road toward Pharae and came to Telphusa, and from there to Heraea. There he sold off the plunder as spoils, and set about repairing the bridge over the Alpheus, intending to make his invasion of Triphylia by that route.

At about this same time Dorimachus, the general of the Aetolians, in response to the appeal of the Eleans for help against the ravaging of their land, sent them six hundred Aetolians under the general Phillidas.

When Phillidas arrived in Elis, he took over the Elean mercenaries, who numbered about five hundred, and a thousand citizen soldiers, and along with these the Tarentine cavalry, and came to the relief of Triphylia.

This district has received its name from Triphylus, one of the sons of Arcas. It lies in the Peloponnese along the sea, between the territories of Elis and Messenia, faces toward the Libyan Sea, and forms the farthest edge of Arcadia in the direction of the winter sunset (the southwest).

It contains the following cities: Samicum, Lepreum, Hypana, Typaneae, Pyrgos, Aepium, Bolax, Stylangium, and Phrixa.

A short time before this the Eleans had gained control of these and had also annexed the city of the Aliphereans, which had originally belonged to Arcadia [and Megalopolis], after Lydiadas the Megalopolitan, during his tyranny, had handed it over to the Eleans in an exchange to serve certain private purposes of his own.

# Book 4 Chapter 78: Continued Success of Philip

Phillidas, however, sent the Eleans off to Lepreum and the mercenaries to Aliphera, while he himself, keeping the Aetolians with him at Typaneae, waited to see what would come of it.

The king, meanwhile, having set aside his baggage train and crossed the river Alpheus by the bridge—the river that flows right past the city of the Heraeans—

came to Aliphera. This town lies on a hill that is sheer on every side, with an ascent of more than ten stades, and it has a citadel on the very summit of the whole hill, together with a bronze statue of Athena, outstanding in beauty and size.

As for this statue—from what intention or with what funding the work was first undertaken—it happens to be a matter of dispute even among the local people, for it cannot be clearly established either from where or by whom it was dedicated.

The finished work of art, however, is acknowledged by everyone to be among the most magnificent and skillfully executed of works, made by Hypatodorus and Sostratus.

Be that as it may, when a clear and bright day had dawned, the king at first light drew up at several points the men carrying the scaling ladders, with the supporting detachments of mercenaries stationed in front of them,

and, posting the Macedonians divided up behind each of these forces, gave orders to all, the moment the sun came up, to advance against the hill.

As the Macedonians carried out the order with spirit and to terrifying effect, it came about that the Aliphereans kept rushing and running together against them to whatever spots they saw the Macedonians most closely approaching.

At this moment the king himself, with the most suitable men, climbed up unnoticed by way of certain cliffs to the suburb of the citadel.

When the signal had been given, all at once set their ladders against the walls and made their attempt on the city.

The king was the first to seize the suburb of the citadel, finding it deserted. As this was being set ablaze, the men defending the walls, foreseeing what was about to happen and terrified that, if the citadel were seized first, they would be robbed of their last hope as well, abandoned the walls and rushed to flee toward the acropolis.

When this happened, the Macedonians at once gained control of both the walls and the city.

After this the men on the citadel sent envoys to Philip, and he, granting them safe conduct, took possession of it too by agreement.
