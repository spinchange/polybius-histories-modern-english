# Book 28 Chapter 17: Antiochus Invades Egypt

Agepolis and his colleagues came to Quintus and found him encamped in Macedonia near Heracleum, where they delivered the instructions they had been given.

After hearing them out, he said that he himself paid no attention to these slanders, and he urged them as well not to listen to anyone who dared to speak against Rome. He added many other expressions of goodwill besides.

He also wrote the same sentiments in a letter to the people of Rhodes.

Agepolis was greatly encouraged by the reception as a whole; and Quintus, taking him aside privately, said that he was amazed the Rhodians were not trying to bring the present war to an end, especially since that course suited their interests so well.

Whether he did this because he suspected Antiochus, fearing that if he gained possession of Alexandria he might become a dangerous additional enemy to the Romans while the war against Perseus was dragging on,

for by this time the war over Coele-Syria had already become entangled,

or whether, seeing that the issue with Perseus would soon be decided now that the Roman armies had entered Macedonia,

and holding high hopes about the outcome, he wanted to prod the Rhodians into presenting themselves as mediators and, once they had done so, to give the Romans plausible grounds for deciding to deal with them however they saw fit,

it is not easy to say with certainty. But I think the latter explanation is more likely, judging from what happened to the Rhodians shortly afterward.

In any case, Agepolis and his colleagues then went on from there to Gaius, and after receiving from him even more exceptional marks of goodwill than from Marcius, they quickly returned to Rhodes.

When the embassy reported back, and both generals seemed to have rivaled one another in friendliness, both in their conversation and in their formal replies, all the Rhodians were lifted up in spirit and full of confidence, though not all in the same way.

Those of sound judgment were delighted by the goodwill of the Romans, but the restless and unhealthy elements reasoned among themselves that such excessive kindness was a sign that the Romans were afraid of the crisis and that events were not turning out as they had expected.

And when Agepolis happened to let slip to some of his friends that he had received private instructions from Marcius to mention before the council the question of ending the war,

then Deinon and his circle became completely convinced that the Romans were in grave difficulties.

So they sent envoys to Alexandria as well, to put an end to the war then being fought between Antiochus and Ptolemy.

# Book 28 Chapter 18: Antiochus IV. Epiphanes

King Antiochus was energetic in action, bold in his designs, and worthy of the royal office, except in his operations around Pelusium.

# Book 28 Chapter 19: Envoys Sent to Antiochus

After Antiochus took control of affairs in Egypt, Comanus and Cineas, having met in council with the king, decided to convene an advisory assembly made up of the most distinguished leaders to deliberate about the crisis at hand.

The first decision of the council was to send to Antiochus, to negotiate a settlement, the envoys from Greece who happened to be staying there.

At that time there were two embassies from the Achaean League: one to renew friendly relations, represented by Alcithus of Aegium, son of Xenophon, and Pasiadas; and another concerning the festival of the Antigoneia.

There was also an embassy from the Athenians concerning a gift, headed by Demaratus, and two sacred delegations: one for the Panathenaea, led by Callias the pancratiast, and the other concerning the Mysteries, in which Cleostratus conducted the business and delivered the speeches.

There were also present Eudemus and Hicesius from Miletus, and Apollonides and Apollonius from Clazomenae.

King Ptolemy also sent Tlepolemus and Ptolemy the rhetorician as envoys.

These men accordingly sailed up the river to meet him.

# Book 28 Chapter 20: Antiochus Epiphanes In Egypt

At the time when Antiochus occupied Egypt, the envoys sent to arrange a settlement joined the envoys from Greece. He received the men graciously and made their first reception notably splendid.

On the following day he granted them an audience and told them to speak on the matters for which they had been instructed.

The Achaean envoys spoke first, then Demaratus on behalf of the Athenians, and after him Eudemus of Miletus.

Since they were all speaking at the same moment of the same issue, the substance of their individual speeches was naturally much alike.

All of them laid the blame for what had happened on Eulaeus, and by appealing to Ptolemy's kinship with Antiochus and to the king's youth, they begged Antiochus to put aside his anger.

Antiochus agreed with all this and even strengthened their case further, but then began to speak in defense of his original rights, by which he tried to establish that the regions of Coele-Syria properly belonged to the kings of Syria,

supporting his position by referring to the dominion of Antigonus, the first ruler who held the kingdom in Syria, and citing the concessions granted to Seleucus by the kings of Macedonia after Antigonus's death.

Next, building on these points, he appealed to the final acquisition made by his father Antiochus in war,

and, above all, he denied the agreement which the men in Alexandria said had been made between the recently deceased Ptolemy and Antiochus's father: namely, that Coele-Syria was to be given to him as a dowry when he married Cleopatra, the mother of the present king.

After speaking on this case, and convincing not only himself but also those who had come to meet him that his claims were just, he then sailed to Naucratis.

There too he treated the people kindly, gave a gold coin to each of the Greeks living there, and then advanced against Alexandria.

To the envoys he promised that he would give his answer when Aristides and Theris returned to him.

For, he said, he had sent them to Ptolemy, and he wanted the envoys from Greece all to be fully informed and to serve as witnesses.
