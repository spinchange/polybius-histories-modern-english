# Book 3 Chapter 1: Summary of the Work

In the first book of my whole composition—which is the third counting backward from the present one—I made clear that I would take as the starting points of my work the Social War, the Hannibalic War, and, alongside these, the war over Coele-Syria.

In that same book I also explained the reasons why, going back in time to the period before these events, I composed the books that precede this one.

Now, after a few preliminary remarks about my own undertaking, I shall attempt to give a demonstrated account of the wars mentioned above, of the causes from which they arose, and of the reasons why they grew to such proportions.

Since the whole subject I have set my hand to write about is in fact a single action and a single spectacle—namely, how, when, and why all the known parts of the inhabited world fell under the dominion of Rome—

and since this event has a recognizable beginning, a fixed span of time, and an acknowledged conclusion, I think it useful to give in summary form a preliminary account of the greatest of its parts, all those that lie between the beginning and the end.

For I believe that in this way I shall best furnish to those eager to learn an adequate grasp of the whole design.

The mind takes in much when it moves from the whole toward knowledge of the particulars, and much again when it moves from the particulars toward understanding of the whole; and since I judge that the best comprehension and view comes from combining both, I shall make my preliminary outline of my own work follow accordingly.

I have already set out the general impression of my subject and its overall scope.

As for the particular events that occurred within it, the starting points are the wars mentioned above; the culmination and conclusion is the overthrow of the kingdom in Macedonia; and the time between the beginning and the end is fifty-three years.

Within this span are contained deeds so many and so great that no previous age has embraced their equal in an equal interval of time.

Beginning my account of these from the hundred and fortieth Olympiad, I shall make my approach to the exposition in the following manner.

# Book 3 Chapter 2: Plan: Causes of Wars

I shall make my approach to the narrative as follows. First, having pointed out the causes from which the previously mentioned war between the Carthaginians and the Romans—the one called the Hannibalic War—arose,

I shall tell how the Carthaginians invaded Italy, broke up the dominion of Rome there, and brought the Romans into great fear for themselves and for the very soil of their homeland, while they themselves conceived great and unexpected hopes, expecting even to take Rome itself by assault.

Next, I shall try to make clear how, during the same period, Philip of Macedon—after fighting his war with the Aetolians to its end and afterward settling affairs among the Greeks—set out to share these same hopes with the Carthaginians,

how Antiochus and Ptolemy Philopator first disputed and finally went to war with each other over Coele-Syria,

and how the Rhodians and Prusias took up war against the Byzantines and forced them to stop levying tolls on those sailing into the Pontus.

Pausing my account at this point, I shall put together a discussion of the Roman constitution, in which I shall show in due sequence that the distinctive character of their political system contributed enormously to their success—not only in winning back their dominion over the Italians and Sicilians, and in further acquiring the rule over the Iberians and the Celts, but finally also, after they had defeated the Carthaginians in war, in coming to conceive the idea of universal empire.

Together with this, by way of digression, I shall describe the fall of the dynasty of Hiero of Syracuse.

To these I shall append the disturbances in Egypt: how, after King Ptolemy departed this life, Antiochus and Philip agreed together to divide up the realm of the child he had left behind, and began their treacherous dealings and laid their hands on it—Philip on the regions around the Aegean, Caria, and Samos, and Antiochus on Coele-Syria and Phoenicia.

# Book 3 Chapter 3: Plan: Events in Greece

After this, having summarized the operations of the Romans and Carthaginians in Iberia, Libya, and Sicily, I shall, along with the shift in events, move my narrative entirely to the regions of Greece.

Having recounted the naval battles of Attalus and the Rhodians against Philip, and also the war between Rome and Philip—how it was conducted, by whom, and what end it had—

I shall join to this in unbroken sequence the account of the resentment of the Aetolians, in the course of which they drew in Antiochus from Asia and kindled the war against the Achaeans and the Romans.

After explaining its causes and the crossing of Antiochus into Europe, I shall make clear, first, how he was driven out of Greece; second, how, after being defeated, he withdrew from all the territory on this side of the Taurus;

and third, how the Romans, after crushing the insolence of the Galatians, secured for themselves undisputed mastery over Asia and freed those dwelling on this side of the Taurus from their fear of the barbarians and from the lawlessness of the Galatians.

After this, having set before the reader's eyes the misfortunes of the Aetolians and the Cephallenians, I shall take up the wars that Eumenes waged against Prusias and the Galatians, and likewise the one waged together with Ariarathes against Pharnaces.

Following these, after recalling the concord and resettlement of the Peloponnesians, and also the growth of the Rhodian state, I shall draw together my whole narrative and the events within it,

and finally relate the campaign of Antiochus, called Epiphanes, against Egypt, the Persian War, and the overthrow of the kingdom in Macedonia.

Through all of this it will at the same time be observed how the Romans, by their handling of each situation, made the whole inhabited world subject to themselves.

# Book 3 Chapter 4: Extension of the First Plan of the Work

Now, if it were possible to form an adequate judgment about which men and political systems are blameworthy and which, on the contrary, praiseworthy simply on the basis of their successes or their failures, then I ought to stop here and bring both my narrative and my work to a close with the last events I have mentioned, in keeping with my original intention.

For the fifty-three years came to their end at this point, and the growth and advance of Roman power had reached completion;

and besides, it seemed an accepted fact, forced upon everyone, that from then on all that remained was to listen to the Romans and to obey their commands.

But since judgments based purely on the contests themselves are not conclusive, whether about the victors or about the defeated,

because to many what seemed the greatest of successes has, when not used as it should be, brought the greatest disasters, while not a few who have met the most shattering reverses, when they bore them nobly, have often turned them to their advantage—

I ought therefore to add to the events already described an account of the policy of the victors: what it was afterward, and how they presided over the whole; and also the reactions and judgments of the others—how many and what kind there were concerning the leading power; and besides these I must set out the impulses and ambitions that prevailed and held sway among each people, both in their private lives and in their public affairs.

For it is clear that from these things it will become evident to people now living whether the rule of Rome is to be avoided or, on the contrary, to be welcomed, and to later generations whether their empire is to be considered praiseworthy and enviable or blameworthy.

It is precisely in this part that the usefulness of my history, both for the present and for the future, will chiefly lie.

For it must not be supposed that the end of a course of action—either for those who direct affairs or for those who pronounce judgment on them—is simply to conquer and bring everyone under one's own power.

No one with any sense makes war on his neighbors merely for the sake of defeating those who oppose him, nor sails the seas merely to make the crossing, nor takes up skills and crafts merely for the sake of the knowledge itself.

Everyone does everything for the sake of the resulting pleasure, honor, or advantage that comes from his actions.

Therefore the crowning achievement of this work will be to learn the condition of each people: what it was after everything had been subdued and had fallen under Roman authority, down to the disturbance and upheaval that arose again afterward.

Because of the magnitude of the events involved, the unexpected character of what happened, and—most of all—because I was not only an eyewitness to most of it but in some cases a participant and in others the very director, I was led to begin, as it were, a new work.

# Book 3 Chapter 5: The True Theory of Historical Causes

—as if making this the beginning of a fresh narrative to write. The upheaval I have spoken of was that in which the Romans waged war against the Celtiberians and the Vaccaei, and the Carthaginians against Massinissa, king of the Libyans;

while in Asia Attalus and Prusias made war on each other, and Ariarathes, king of the Cappadocians, having been driven from his throne by Orophernes through the agency of King Demetrius, recovered his ancestral kingdom again with the help of Attalus.

Demetrius, son of Seleucus, after holding the throne of Syria for twelve years, was robbed at once of his life and his rule when the other kings combined against him.

The Romans, too, restored to their own country those Greeks who had been accused in connection with the Persian War, having acquitted them of the charge brought against them.

Not long afterward these same Romans laid their hands on the Carthaginians, intending first to compel them to relocate, and then, for reasons to be stated in what follows, to root them out altogether.

Corresponding to these events—as the Macedonians broke away from their friendship with Rome and the Lacedaemonians from their membership in the Achaean confederation—the common calamity of all Greece had at once its beginning and its end.

Such, then, is the design of my work.

But it still has need of Fortune, that the circumstances of my life may run together to bring my purpose to completion.

For I am persuaded that, even if some human fate should befall me, this theme will not be left idle, nor will it lack men worthy of it; rather, because of its excellence, many will pledge themselves to it and be eager to bring it to completion.

Now that I have gone through the most notable events in summary—wishing to give my readers a conception of the whole history both in general and in its parts—it is time, keeping my purpose in mind, to lead the account back to the beginning of my subject.

# Book 3 Chapter 6: Causes of the Second Punic War

Some of those who have written accounts of the deeds of Hannibal, wishing to point out to us the causes from which the aforementioned war between the Romans and the Carthaginians arose, name as the first the siege of Saguntum by the Carthaginians,

and as the second their crossing, in violation of the treaties, of the river called by the local inhabitants the Iber.

For my part, I would say that these were the beginnings of the war, but I would by no means concede that they were its causes. Far from it—

unless one is also going to say that Alexander's crossing into Asia was the cause of the war against the Persians, and that Antiochus' landing at Demetrias was the cause of his war with the Romans: neither of which is either probable or true.

For who could suppose these to be causes, when Alexander earlier, and Philip while still alive, had done and prepared a great deal for the war against the Persians, and likewise the Aetolians had done much before the arrival of Antiochus for the war against the Romans?

But such notions belong to men who have not grasped how a beginning differs from a cause and a pretext, and how far apart they stand—that causes and pretexts come first of all, while the beginning is the last of the things mentioned.

I, however, declare that the beginnings of anything are the first attempts and actions to carry out what has already been decided, while the causes are the things that precede the decisions and judgments. By this I mean the conceptions, the dispositions, and the reasonings about these matters, and the considerations through which we come to decide on something and to set it as our purpose.

What I have said is made clear by what follows.

For what the true causes were, and from what source the war against the Persians sprang, is easy even for an ordinary person to discern.

The first was the return march of the Greeks under Xenophon from the upper satrapies, in the course of which, as they passed through the whole of Asia—hostile though it was—no one dared to stand and face them in the open;

the second was the crossing of Agesilaus, king of the Lacedaemonians, into Asia, in which he found nothing worthy or capable of opposing his designs, and was forced to return midway, without accomplishing his aim, because of the disturbances in Greece.

From these things Philip perceived and reckoned up the cowardice and laziness of the Persians, as well as his own excellence and that of the Macedonians in matters of war; and setting before his eyes, too, the magnitude and splendor of the prizes that would come from the war,

while at the same time securing the acknowledged goodwill of the Greeks, he at once made use of the pretext that he was eager to avenge the lawless treatment the Greeks had suffered from the Persians, conceived an eagerness for the enterprise, resolved to make war, and began preparing everything toward this end.

Accordingly, the first set of circumstances mentioned should be regarded as the causes of the war against the Persians, the second as the pretext, and the beginning as—

# Book 3 Chapter 7: Fabius Pictor on the Cause of the Punic War

—Alexander's crossing into Asia. And so too, in the case of the war between Antiochus and the Romans, it is clear that the cause must be considered the resentment of the Aetolians.

For they, believing that they had been slighted by the Romans in many respects regarding the outcome of the war with Philip—as I said above—not only drew in Antiochus, but undertook to do and suffer anything because of the anger that had arisen in them out of the events of that time.

The pretext should be considered the liberation of the Greeks, which they went about proclaiming from city to city in company with Antiochus, without reason and falsely; and the beginning of the war was the landing of Antiochus at Demetrias.

I have made this rather extended distinction about these matters not for the sake of censuring the historians in question, but for the sake of correcting the understanding of those eager to learn.

For what use is a physician to the sick who does not know the causes of the body's disorders? And what use is a man of affairs who cannot reason out how and why and from what source each of these matters took its origin?

For it is no more likely that the one will ever properly arrange treatments for the body than that the man of affairs will be able to handle any situation that arises as he should, without knowledge of the things just mentioned.

Therefore there is nothing we ought to be more on the watch for and to seek out than the causes of each event that occurs, since the greatest matters often grow out of trifling ones, and it is easiest of all to remedy anything in its first impulses and conceptions.
