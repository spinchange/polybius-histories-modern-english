# Book 4 Chapter 1: Review of Achaean History

In the book before this one I set out the causes of the second war that broke out between the Romans and the Carthaginians, and I gave an account of Hannibal's invasion of Italy.

In addition to this, I described the battles the two sides fought against one another, down to the engagement that took place by the river Aufidus and the city of Cannae.

Now I shall go through the affairs of Greece that were carried out during the same period as the events already related, beginning from the 140th Olympiad.

But first I shall briefly remind those who take up this work of the preliminary account I gave in the second book concerning Greek affairs, and especially concerning the Achaean nation—since this political body too achieved a remarkable growth, both in the times before our own and in our own day.

I began with Tisamenus, one of the sons of Orestes, and stated that from his time the Achaeans were ruled by kings in unbroken succession down to Ogygus; that afterwards they adopted, by an excellent choice, a democratic constitution; and that at first they were broken apart, city by city and village by village, by the kings from Macedonia.

Following this, I set out to tell how they began once more to unite in common purpose, and when and which cities were the first to band together.

Next in order I explained by what method and with what policy they went about winning over the cities and undertook to bring all the Peloponnesians under the same name and constitution.

After giving a general account of the plan just described, I then touched continuously on the particular actions, until I came to the downfall of Cleomenes, king of the Lacedaemonians.

Having summed up the events of this preliminary survey down to the deaths of Antigonus, Seleucus, and Ptolemy—since all of these passed away at about the same time—I announced, as what remained, that I would make the beginning of my own work the events that follow upon those already described.

# Book 4 Chapter 2: Why Begin With This Period of History

I judged this to be the best starting point, first because it is here that the history of Aratus ends; and by joining my narrative to his I have chosen to give the account of Greek affairs that follows directly upon it.

Second, because the periods that follow, and those that fall within my own history, run together in such a way that some belong to our own time and some to the time of our fathers; from which it comes about that I was personally present at some of the events, while others I have heard from those who saw them.

To reach further back in time, and to write down a report of a report, did not seem to me a safe basis either for forming judgments or for making assertions.

But above all I began from these times because it was then that fortune, so to speak, made everything in the inhabited world new.

For Philip, the natural son of Demetrius, while still a boy, had just taken over the rule of the Macedonians;

Achaeus, who held power over the region on this side of the Taurus, possessed not merely the outward standing of a king but real power as well;

and Antiochus, surnamed the Great, a little earlier, on the death of his brother Seleucus, had succeeded while still quite young to the kingdom of Syria.

At the same time as these, Ariarathes took over the rule of Cappadocia, and Ptolemy Philopator had in the same period become master of affairs in Egypt.

Not long afterward Lycurgus was established as king of the Lacedaemonians, and the Carthaginians had recently chosen Hannibal as their general for the operations already mentioned.

With such a renewal taking place in all the dynasties, there was bound to be the beginning of new affairs; for this is the natural course of things and tends to happen so—and that is just what came about at this time.

For the Romans and the Carthaginians entered upon the war already described; Antiochus and Ptolemy, at the same time as these, the war over Coele-Syria; and the Achaeans and Philip the war against the Aetolians and the Lacedaemonians. The causes of this last came about as follows.

# Book 4 Chapter 3: Dorimachus In the Peloponnese

The Aetolians had long chafed at peace and at having to meet expenses from their own resources, since they were accustomed to live off their neighbors and needed lavish means to support their ingrained arrogance. Enslaved to it, they always live a grasping, beast-like life, regarding nothing as belonging to others and everything as theirs to plunder.

Nevertheless, in the time before this, so long as Antigonus was alive, fear of the Macedonians kept them quiet.

But when he departed this life, leaving behind the boy Philip, they grew contemptuous and began looking for openings and pretexts for meddling in the Peloponnese—drawn by their old habit of plundering that country, and at the same time reckoning that they were a match for the Achaeans themselves in war.

While they were set on this purpose, chance gave them a little assistance, and they got the following openings for their design.

Dorimachus of Trichonium was the son of that Nicostratus who broke the truce at the festival of the Pamboeotia. Being young and full of Aetolian impetuosity and greed, he was sent out by public authority to the city of the Phigaleans,

which lies in the Peloponnese, on the borders of the Messenians, and which at that time happened to be in political union with the Aetolians—

ostensibly to guard the territory and city of the Phigaleans, but in fact holding the post of a spy upon affairs in the Peloponnese.

When a band of pirates gathered and came to him at Phigalea, he had no way of providing them with plunder by lawful means, since the general peace among the Greeks that had been concluded under Antigonus was still in force.

At last, at a loss what to do, he allowed the pirates to carry off the livestock of the Messenians, although they were friends and allies.

At first they did their wrong to the flocks on the outlying lands, but afterward, as their recklessness grew, they set about breaking into the farmhouses as well, appearing without warning by night.

The Messenians were indignant at this and sent embassies to Dorimachus; but at first he turned a deaf ear, wishing partly to benefit the men under his command and partly to profit himself by taking a share of the plunder.

When the embassies kept coming in greater numbers because of the continual wrongdoing, he said that he would come in person to Messene to argue the case against those who were bringing charges against the Aetolians.

But when he arrived, and the injured parties approached him, he ridiculed some with mockery, browbeat others, and frightened still others with abuse.

# Book 4 Chapter 4: Dorimachus Leaves Messene

While he was still staying in Messene, the pirates approached the city by night, set scaling-ladders against the walls, and broke into the farmstead called Chiron's villa; those who resisted they cut down, and the rest of the household slaves they bound and led away, together with the cattle.

The Messenian ephors, who had long been grieved both at what was happening and at Dorimachus's presence among them, and who now felt themselves insulted on top of it, summoned him before the assembled magistrates.

On this occasion Sciron, who was at that time an ephor of the Messenians and was also highly esteemed by his fellow citizens for the rest of his conduct, advised that they should not let Dorimachus leave the city unless he restored to the Messenians everything that had been lost and handed over the wrongdoers to stand trial for those who had been killed.

When everyone signified that Sciron was speaking justly, Dorimachus flew into a rage and declared that they were complete fools if they imagined they were now insulting Dorimachus and not the common body of the Aetolians; in general he regarded what was happening as outrageous, and said that they would incur a collective reckoning, and would suffer it deservedly.

Now there was at that time in Messene a depraved fellow named Babyrtas, a man debased in every possible way, upon whom, if one put Dorimachus's sun-hat and cloak, it was impossible to tell them apart,

so closely did he resemble the said Dorimachus in his voice and in the other features of his body—and this did not escape Dorimachus's notice.

So while he was speaking to the Messenians in this threatening and exceedingly arrogant manner, Sciron, in a burst of anger, said: "Do you suppose we care a whit for you or your bluster, Babyrtas?" When this was said,

Dorimachus for the moment yielded to the circumstances and agreed to make redress to the Messenians for all the wrongs that had been done;

but on returning to Aetolia he took what had been said so bitterly and so hard that, having no other reasonable pretext, for this reason alone he kindled the war against the Messenians.
