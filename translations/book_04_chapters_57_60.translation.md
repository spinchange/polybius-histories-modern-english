# Book 4 Chapter 57: Philip Starts for Aetolia

So the affairs at Sinope stood as I have described. Meanwhile King Philip, having set out from Macedonia with his army — for it was at this stage of his undertakings that I broke off my account of the Social War — marched toward Thessaly and Epirus, eager to make his invasion of Aetolia by that route.

At about this same time Alexander and Dorimachus, having an intrigue under way against the city of the Aegiratans, gathered around twelve hundred Aetolians at Oeanthe in Aetolia, which lies directly opposite the aforesaid town, and, having made ready ferry boats for them, were watching for a chance to sail across in fulfillment of their plan.

For one of the deserters from Aetolia, who had spent a considerable time among the Aegiratans and had observed that the men guarding the gate facing Aegium got drunk and carried out their watch carelessly,

had crossed over again and again to Dorimachus and his party and invited them to attempt the venture, since they were thoroughly at home in enterprises of this kind.

The city of the Aegiratans is built on the Peloponnesian shore of the Corinthian Gulf, between the cities of Aegium and Sicyon. It sits upon rugged and hard-to-climb hills, faces by its position toward Parnassus and that part of the coast opposite, and lies about seven stades back from the sea.

When a chance to sail offered itself to Dorimachus and his men, they put out and came to anchor, while it was still night, at the mouth of the river that flows down past the city.

Alexander and Dorimachus, then, and with them Archidamus the son of Pantaleon, having the main body of the Aetolians about them, began advancing toward the city along the road that comes from Aegium.

But the deserter, taking the twenty fittest men, made his way over the cliffs by the trackless routes faster than the rest thanks to his familiarity with the ground, and slipping in through a certain water channel, caught the men at the gate still asleep.

Having cut them down while they were still in their beds, and having hacked through the bars with their axes, he opened the gates to the Aetolians.

These rushed in, but then handled the situation with conspicuous lack of forethought — which proved to be a contributing cause of the Aegiratans' salvation and of the Aetolians' destruction.

For supposing that getting inside the gates was the whole point of taking an enemy's city, in this manner

they handled their affairs. And so, after staying together only a very short time around the marketplace, they then, in their passion for plunder, drifted apart, broke into the houses, and began carrying off the people's property — and by now it was broad daylight.

The Aegiratans, the affair having befallen them utterly unhoped-for and unexpected, were thrown into panic and terror: those upon whose houses the enemy had descended all turned to flight out of the city, supposing that it was already firmly in the enemy's hands.

But all those who, hearing the shouting, came out to help from houses that had not yet been touched, ran together to the citadel.

These kept growing more numerous and more confident, while the body of Aetolians, on the contrary, grew smaller and more disordered, for the reasons already given.

Nevertheless Dorimachus and his men, seeing the danger now closing around them, rallied together and charged the men holding the citadel, supposing that by their boldness and daring they would strike panic into those who had assembled for the defense and put them to flight.

But the Aegiratans, cheering one another on, fought back and grappled with the Aetolians nobly.

Since the citadel was unwalled, and the fighting was hand to hand and man against man, the contest was at first what one would expect, the one side fighting for fatherland and children, the other for their own survival. In the end, however, the Aetolians who had broken in were routed.

The Aegiratans, seizing the opening of their enemies' wavering, pressed upon them vigorously and terrifyingly. As a result it came about that most of the Aetolians, in their panic, fleeing of their own accord, were trampled together at the gates.

Alexander fell in hand-to-hand fighting in the thick of the action, while Archidamus perished in the crush and suffocation at the gates.

Of the rest of the Aetolian host, part was trampled underfoot, and part, fleeing over the cliffs by the trackless ways, broke their necks.

What part of them did get through safely to the ships threw away their arms in utter disgrace and made their voyage back, as unexpectedly as they had come.

Thus the Aegiratans, having lost their fatherland through negligence, recovered it again, against all expectation, through their courage and gallantry.

# Book 4 Chapter 59: Euripidas

At about the same time Euripidas, who had been sent out by the Aetolians as general for the Eleans, overran the territory of Dyme, Pharae, and also Tritaea, and after rounding up a considerable quantity of plunder, began withdrawing toward Elis.

But Miccus of Dyme, who happened at that time to be deputy general of the Achaeans, came out to the rescue with the full levy of the Dymaeans and Pharaeans, and with the Tritaeans as well, and fell upon the enemy as they were retiring.

Pressing too eagerly upon the retreating force, however, he ran into an ambush and was checked, and lost many of his men: forty were killed, and about two hundred of the infantry taken prisoner.

Euripidas, then, having won this advantage and being elated at what had happened, marched out again a few days later and seized, near the Araxus, a well-placed fort of the Dymaeans called the Wall —

which the myths say Heracles built long ago, when he was at war with the Eleans, wishing to use it as a base of operations against them.

The peoples of Dyme, Pharae, and Tritaea, having come off the worse in their attempt at relief and fearing what was to come from the seizure of the fort, at first sent messengers to the general of the Achaeans, reporting what had happened and asking him to help them; afterwards they dispatched envoys to make the same request.

But Aratus was unable to assemble the mercenary force, because during the Cleomenic War the Achaeans had fallen behind in some of the pay owed to the hired soldiers; and in general he conducted his undertakings — in a word, the whole business of the war — without daring and sluggishly.

For this reason Lycurgus took the Athenaeum of the Megalopolitans, and Euripidas, following up the successes already mentioned, took Gortyna in the territory of Telphusa.

And the people of Dyme, Pharae, and Tritaea, having lost hope in any help from the general, agreed among themselves not to pay the common contributions to the Achaeans,

but to raise mercenaries of their own — three hundred infantry and fifty cavalry — and through these to secure their territory.

In doing this they were thought to have deliberated reasonably enough regarding their own affairs, but the opposite regarding the common interest; for they seemed to have become the originators and leaders of a wicked approach and pretext for those who wished to break up the confederacy.

Yet the greatest share of the blame for this action one might justly lay upon the general, who was negligent, kept procrastinating, and continually abandoned those who needed him.

For every man who is in danger, so long as he can hold on to some hope from his kinsmen and allies, is inclined to rely on them; but when, in his straits, he gives up that hope, then at last he is forced to help himself as best he can.

Therefore the Tritaeans, Pharaeans, and Dymaeans are not to be blamed for raising mercenaries of their own when the leader of the Achaeans was dragging his feet; but for renouncing the contributions to the common treasury they deserve reproach.

For they ought not to have neglected their own need — being indeed in a good position and able to act — yet they should at the same time have kept up their just obligations toward the common commonwealth, all the more since recovery of such payment was guaranteed without fail under the common laws, and, above all, since they had been founders of the Achaean confederacy.
