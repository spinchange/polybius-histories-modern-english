# Translator's Preface

*Pragmatike Historia* presents Polybius's *Histories* — his account of how, in the fifty-three years from 220 to 167 BC, Rome came to rule the Mediterranean world — in a modern English translation of the Greek that survives. The title borrows Polybius's own name for his method: pragmatic history, the study of causes and consequences by someone who has seen events at first hand or questioned those who did.

## The text translated
The translation is made from the Greek text of Büttner-Wobst (Teubner, 1889–1905), as made available through the Perseus Digital Library. It was produced by Claude, Anthropic's family of AI models, working chapter by chapter from the Greek. Each chapter was then checked against the Greek by a second pass, and chapters where the first English departed from the Greek were retranslated. An older public-domain English version of 1889 served as a crib for chapter numbering and for hard passages; it was not used as the wording of this edition.

## What is and is not here
- Books 1–39 are translated in full as far as the Greek survives. Books 1–5 are complete; the later books are in large part fragments preserved by later writers.
- **Book 17** does not survive; a note says so.
- **Book 40**, the index to the *Histories*, and the unplaced fragments beyond Book 39 are not included. The closing summary at 39.8 is.
- Chapters preserved in quotation by other authors are marked with the quoting author where the source names them.

## Conventions
- **Gaps.** A gap in the Greek is marked `[...]`. Nothing is supplied to fill it.
- **Peoples.** Peoples of Italy and Europe are called Gauls; those who settled in Asia Minor are Galatians. "Celts" appears only in an explicitly ethnographic remark. Spartans appear in narrative; "Lacedaemonians" is kept for treaties, embassies and official language.
- **Names and dates.** Rivers take their modern names (Ebro, Po, Danube, Rhone); cities and regions keep their ancient names. Years are written `215 BC`, ranges `210–206 BC`.
- **Measures.** Distances are in stades, as in the Greek; the camp chapters of Book 6 keep Polybius's feet.
- **Chapters.** Chapter numbers follow the standard divisions. Where a division breaks a sentence, the unfinished sentence is moved under the next heading.

## Limits
This is a translation by a machine, and it makes the errors of one: a lacuna read straight over, a sense inverted, a name mistaken. Many were found and corrected in the checking described above; some will remain. Corrections are welcome.
