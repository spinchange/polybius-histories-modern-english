# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXVIII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 28 Chapter 1: Antiochus and Ptolemy Appeal to Rome

Olympiad 152, year 3. Affairs in Italy.

Since the war over Coele-Syria had already begun between Kings Antiochus and Ptolemy, envoys came to Rome: from Antiochus, Meleager, Sosiphanes, and Heracleides; from Ptolemy, Timotheus and Damon.

At that time Antiochus held the advantage in the affairs of Coele-Syria and Phoenicia.

For ever since Antiochus, the father of the king now reigning, defeated Ptolemy's generals in the battle near Panium, all the regions just mentioned had been subject to the kings in Syria.

So Antiochus, thinking that possession won in war was the strongest and noblest title, pursued the matter as though he were defending property that was his own.

Ptolemy, on the other hand, believed that the former Antiochus had unjustly taken advantage of his father's orphaned condition and stripped them of the cities of Coele-Syria; so he could not bring himself to yield those places to him. That was why Meleager and his party came,

with instructions to call the Senate to witness that Ptolemy, against every rule of justice, had been the first to lay hands on territory that was not his.

Timotheus and his colleagues came both to renew friendly relations and to speak about ending the war with Perseus, but above all to observe the interviews of Meleager's party.

They did not dare speak about ending the war, however, because Marcus Aemilius advised them against it. So, after renewing the friendship and receiving answers in line with what they had requested, they returned to Alexandria.

To Meleager and his colleagues the Senate replied that it would entrust Quintus Marcius to write to Ptolemy on these matters in whatever way he judged advantageous and consistent with his own good faith. This, then, was how the matter was handled for the present.

## Book 28 Chapter 2: Embassy from Rhodes

About this same time, and now with summer already drawing to a close, envoys also arrived from Rhodes: Agesilochus, Nicagoras, and Nicander.

They came to renew the friendship, to obtain permission to ship out grain, and at the same time to answer the slanders being spoken against their city.

For faction at Rhodes seemed to be quite open: Agathagetus, Philophron, Rhodophon, and Theaetetus rested all their hopes on the Romans, while Deinon and Polyaratus looked to Perseus and the Macedonians.

As a result, when objections were repeatedly raised in the course of their public business and deliberations were pulled in opposite directions, those who wanted to invent stories against the city found plenty of opportunities.

Nevertheless, on that occasion the Senate took no notice of any of this, though it knew clearly what had happened among them; and it granted them permission to export one hundred thousand medimni of grain from Sicily.

That was the business the Senate handled privately with the Rhodian envoys.

After that it gave a similar answer to all the other envoys who had arrived from Greece and were pursuing the same policy.

Such was the state of affairs in Italy.

## Book 28 Chapter 3: Pressure Put On Achaia and Aetolia

II. The war with Perseus.

At this time Aulus, serving as proconsul and wintering in Thessaly with the army, sent envoys to the regions of Greece, Gaius Popilius and Gnaeus Octavius.

They first came to Thebes, where they praised the Thebans and urged them to preserve their goodwill toward the Romans.

Then, as they went on through the cities of the Peloponnese, they tried to show the people the Senate's mildness and humanity by producing the decrees that had been issued a little earlier.

At the same time, through their remarks, they let it be understood that they knew the men in each city who were hanging back beyond what was proper, and likewise those who were pressing forward.

And it was plain to everyone that they were displeased with the laggards no less than with those who openly opposed them.

By this they drove many people into uncertainty and bewilderment about what words or actions would best meet the demands of the present moment.

Gaius and his colleague, when the Achaean assembly had been convened, were said to have intended to accuse Lycortas, Archon, and Polybius,

and to show that these men were out of sympathy with Roman policy and, though quiet for the moment, were not naturally so disposed, but were watching events and waiting for their opportunity.

They did not dare to do this, however, because they had no reasonable pretext against the men just named.

So when the council was assembled for them at Aegium, they addressed the Achaeans in a speech at once cordial and exhortatory, and then sailed away to Aetolia.

## Book 28 Chapter 4: Discussion of the Aetolian Congress

And again, when an assembly had been convened for them at Thermon, they came before the people and delivered speeches full of exhortation and professions of goodwill.

But the real object for which they had the assembly in hand, they said, was that the Aetolians must give them hostages.

After they had stepped down, Proandrus rose and wanted to set forth certain services he had rendered that were useful to the Romans, and to accuse those who were slandering him. Thereupon Gaius rose again,

and although he knew perfectly well that Proandrus was no friend of Rome, he nevertheless praised him and confirmed everything he had said.

After him Lyciscus came forward and, though he brought no accusation against anyone by name, cast suspicion on many.

For he said that the Romans had acted rightly in dealing with the ringleaders by taking them off to Rome—meaning Eupolemus and Nicander—

but that their associates and supporters were still left in Aetolia, and that all of them ought to meet with the same correction, unless they surrendered their children to the Romans as hostages.

His hints were aimed especially at Archedamus and Pantaleon.

When he yielded the floor, Pantaleon rose and briefly denounced Lyciscus, saying that he shamelessly and basely flattered those who held the upper hand.

Then he turned against Thoas, thinking that he was the man who gave the accusations against them their greatest credibility, since no quarrel between the two of them was thought to exist.

Reminding him partly of what had happened in the time of Antiochus, and partly reproaching him for ingratitude because, after he had been surrendered to the Romans, he had unexpectedly been saved through the embassy of Pantaleon and Nicander,

he quickly worked the crowd up so far that they were ready not only to shout Thoas down whenever he tried to speak, but even to stone him with one accord.

After this had happened, Gaius briefly reproached the Aetolians for stoning Thoas, and at once departed with his fellow-envoy for Acarnania, saying no more about the hostages. Meanwhile affairs in Aetolia were full of mutual suspicion and complete disorder.

## Book 28 Chapter 5: Lycortas Advises Neutrality

When an assembly of the Acarnanians was convened at Thyrrheion, Aeschrion, Glaucus, and Chremas, who were on the Roman side, urged Gaius and his colleagues to place a garrison in Acarnania.

For, they said, there were men among them who were steering affairs toward Perseus and the Macedonians.

But Diogenes gave the opposite advice. He said that no garrison ought to be introduced into any city, since that was a measure reserved for peoples who had become enemies and had been defeated by the Romans.

The Acarnanians, having done nothing wrong, did not deserve to admit a garrison in any way at all.

As for the party of Chremas and Glaucus, he said that they wanted to build up a personal domination for themselves, and so they were slandering their political opponents and trying to bring in a garrison that would strengthen their own grasping ambitions.

After this had been said, Gaius and his colleagues saw that the crowd disliked the idea of garrisons and, wishing to keep in step with the Senate's policy, agreed with Diogenes' view, commended it, and departed for Larisa to rejoin the proconsul.

## Book 28 Chapter 6: Council of the Achaeans

The leading Greeks decided that the situation created by this embassy required close attention.

So they brought together those who agreed with them in their general political outlook: Arcesilaus and Ariston of Megalopolis, Stratius of Tritaea, Xenon of Patrae, and Apollonidas of Sicyon; and they took counsel about the crisis before them.

Lycortas remained fixed in the policy he had held from the first, judging that they should give no help either to Perseus or to the Romans, but likewise should oppose neither side.

To cooperate, he thought, would be disadvantageous for all the Greeks, since he foresaw how great the power of the victors would become.

But to oppose the Romans was dangerous, because in earlier times they had already come into conflict over public affairs with many Romans, including some of the most eminent.

Apollonidas and Stratius, on the other hand, thought that they should not deliberately set themselves against the Romans; but they said that men who tumbled headlong in their eagerness, who used public business to store up private favor with the Romans, and who acted in this way against the laws and against the common interest, ought to be checked and boldly resisted.

Archon said that they must follow the times, give their enemies no opening for slander, and not allow themselves to fall into the same condition as Nicander and his associates, who, before they had learned by experience what Roman power was, had brought themselves into the gravest troubles.

Polybius, Arcesilaus, Ariston, and Xenon shared this opinion.

Accordingly, they resolved that Archon should at once go forward for the office of strategus, and Polybius for the command of the cavalry.

## Book 28 Chapter 7: A Speech of Polybius

After these events had just taken place, and after Archon's party had already reached the conclusion that they must cooperate with the Romans and their friends, it so happened, by a fortunate turn, that Attalus made his request while they were ready to receive it.

So they readily gave him their assent and promised to support what he asked.

When Attalus had sent envoys, and these men appeared at the first assembly and addressed the Achaeans about restoring the honors paid to the king, urging them to do this as a favor to Attalus,

the crowd showed no clear opinion, and many rose to contradict the proposal for a variety of reasons.

Some, because they had originally been responsible for removing the honors, wanted to confirm the wisdom of their own policy; others, because of private grievances, thought they had found an opportunity to strike at the king; and some, from jealousy of those supporting the measure, were eager to prevent Attalus from carrying his proposal.

Archon rose to support the envoys,

for the matter called for the opinion of the strategus. But after saying only a few words he withdrew,

because he was wary of seeming to give this advice for profit, since he had spent a considerable sum of money in securing his office.

As great uncertainty prevailed, Polybius rose and made a number of arguments; but above all he won over the majority by pointing to the original decree of the Achaeans about these honors, in which it had been written that the improper and illegal honors should be removed—not, by Zeus, all of them.

He said that Sosigenes and Diopeithes, who were serving as judges at that time and were privately at odds with Eumenes, had seized on this opportunity to overturn all the honors granted to the king.

In doing so, they had acted contrary to the decree of the Achaeans, contrary to the authority given them, and, most of all, contrary to justice and what was fitting.

For the Achaeans had not resolved to abolish the honors of Eumenes because they had suffered some wrong at his hands; rather, because he had asked for rewards greater than his benefactions deserved, they had voted to remove only the excess.

Therefore, just as those judges, by putting their private hostility ahead of what was honorable for the Achaeans, had overthrown all the honors, so now the Achaeans, considering their own duty and dignity the highest authority, ought to correct the judges' error and, in general, the unreasonable treatment shown to Eumenes,

especially since the favor they were about to grant would rest not only on the king himself, but even more on his brother Attalus.

When the people approved what he said, a decree was drafted ordering the magistrates to restore all the honors of King Eumenes, except any that contained something unbecoming to the Achaean League or contrary to law.

So in this way, and at this moment, Attalus corrected the unjust treatment that had been shown in the Peloponnese regarding the honors belonging to his brother Eumenes.

## Book 28 Chapter 8: Perseus Sends Pleuratus to Genthius

Perseus sent envoys to King Genthius: Pleuratus the Illyrian, who was living with him as an exile, and Adaeus of Beroea.

He instructed them to explain what he had accomplished in the war, both against the Romans and against the Dardanians, and also what he had done for the present against the Epirotes and the Illyrians, and to invite Genthius into friendship and alliance with himself and the Macedonians.

So they made their journey beyond Mount Scardus through the region called Desert Illyria, which the Macedonians not long before had laid waste in order to make Dardanian incursions into Illyria and Macedonia more difficult.

Even so, Adaeus and his party came through those districts with great hardship to Scodra, and when they learned that Genthius was staying at Lissus they sent messengers on to him.

He quickly summoned them, and when they met him they spoke on the matters included in their instructions.

Genthius did not seem averse to friendship with Perseus, but as his excuse for not immediately agreeing to what was asked, he pleaded lack of funds and said that he could not undertake a war against Rome without money.

With this answer Adaeus and his companions set out on their return.

Perseus meanwhile arrived at Stuberra, sold the booty, and rested his forces while waiting for Pleuratus and the others.

When they arrived and he heard Genthius' reply, he at once sent Adaeus back again, together with Glaucias, one of his bodyguards, and as a third envoy the Illyrian, because he knew the Illyrian language,

giving them the same instructions as before, as though Genthius had not clearly explained what he needed and what, once provided, would allow him to comply with the request.

After they had set out, Perseus himself broke camp and marched with his army toward Uscana.

## Book 28 Chapter 9: Unwise Parsimony of Perseus

At this time the envoys who had been sent to Genthius returned, having accomplished nothing more than the earlier embassies and bringing no new result,

because Genthius remained of the same mind, ready enough to join Perseus in the same enterprise, but saying that he needed money.

Perseus ignored this and again sent Hippias and his party to confirm the agreement, leaving out the central point and saying that he would somehow make Genthius well disposed.

So one is at a loss whether such conduct should be called mere irrationality or a kind of heaven-sent delusion. For my part, I think it is rather such a delusion, when men set their hearts on bold enterprises and risk their lives, yet neglect the very thing that holds their plans together, though they see it and have the power to do it.

For if Perseus at that time had been willing to spend money, both publicly on states and privately on kings and political leaders—not lavishly, as his resources allowed, but only in moderation—it would have turned out that all the Greeks and all the kings,

or at least most of them, would have been won over. I do not think any sensible person will dispute this.

But as things stood, by good fortune he did not take that path, by which, if he had succeeded, he would have gained an overbearing mastery of everything, or, if he had failed, would have made many others share the same fate as himself.

Instead he took the opposite course, by which only a very few Greeks were driven into utter folly at the crisis of the action.

## Book 28 Chapter 10: Perseus Blames Hippias for the Failure

Perseus, after suffering a general failure when the Romans entered Macedonia, laid the blame on Hippias.

But it seems to me easy enough to criticize other people and to detect the mistakes of one's neighbors; the hardest thing of all is to do what one can oneself and to understand one's own position. That, too, is what happened in the case of Perseus.

## Book 28 Chapter 11: Heracleum Captured by the Testudo

Heracleum was taken in a rather unusual way. On one side of the city the wall was low for a short stretch, and the Romans selected three detachments for the assault.

The first locked their shields together above their heads, so that by the close-set mass of the arms they looked like a sloping tiled roof. After this came two other detachments in succession ... This "tiled-roof" formation was a tactical arrangement,

which the Romans even practiced as part of their mock exercises.

## Book 28 Chapter 12: Achaean Aid To Rome Declined

III. Greek Affairs

Because Perseus intended to come into Thessaly with an army and decide the whole issue by force of arms, Archon and his colleagues thought it best to answer the suspicions and slanders against them once again by their actions themselves.

So they introduced before the Achaeans a decree that they should make a general levy and march into Thessaly, taking their full part in the struggle alongside the Romans.

When this decree had been ratified, the Achaeans decided that Archon should take charge of assembling the army and making preparations for the expedition, and that envoys should be sent to the consul in Thessaly to explain the Achaean decision and ask when and where they should join their army to his.

They immediately appointed Polybius and others as envoys, and gave Polybius special instructions: if the general approved the arrival of the army, his fellow envoys were to send word back at once explaining this, so that they might not miss the right moment,

while he himself was to see to it that the whole force found markets in the cities through which it marched and that the soldiers lacked none of their necessities.

With these instructions the men just mentioned set out.

They also appointed Telocritus and his colleagues as envoys to Attalus, carrying the decree concerning the restoration of the honors of Eumenes.

At the same time King Ptolemy had applied to the Achaeans because the customary rites performed for kings when they come of age had now taken place in his case.

Judging that it was proper for them to mark the occasion, they voted to send envoys to renew the goodwill already existing between the League and the kingdom, and they at once appointed Alcithon and Pasiadas.

## Book 28 Chapter 13: Marcius Declines Assistance from the Achaeans

Polybius and his colleagues found that the Romans had moved on from Thessaly and were encamped in Perrhaebia, between Azorus and Doliche.

So, because of the critical circumstances, they postponed their audience, but shared in the dangers involved in the advance into Macedonia.

But when the army had marched down into the region around Heracleum, it seemed that the time had come for an interview, since the general appeared to have accomplished the greatest part of what lay before him.

Taking this opportunity, they presented the decree to Marcius and made clear the purpose of the Achaeans: they had resolved, as a whole people, to share with him in the same struggles and dangers. Besides this, they pointed out that every request made of the Achaeans by the Romans during the present war, whether written or delivered by message, had been accepted without objection.

Marcius warmly approved the zeal of the Achaeans, but released them from the hardship and expense, since the situation no longer required assistance from allied forces. So the other envoys returned to Achaea,

but Polybius remained there and took part in the business at hand,

until Marcius, on hearing that Appius Cento was asking the Achaeans for five thousand soldiers to serve in Epirus, sent the aforesaid Polybius off, urging him to see that the troops were not granted and that the Achaeans did not incur so great an expense to no purpose; for Appius, he said, had no sound reason for asking for the soldiers.

Whether he did this out of concern for the Achaeans or because he wanted Appius to achieve nothing is hard to say.

At any rate, when Polybius withdrew to the Peloponnese, letters from Epirus had already arrived, and not long afterward the Achaeans had assembled at Sicyon, so he found himself in a very great difficulty.

When the deliberation was opened on the soldiers Cento was requesting, Polybius decided that he could not possibly disclose the private instructions Marcius had given him; yet to oppose the aid openly, when no clear reason appeared for doing so, was altogether dangerous.

Since the situation was awkward and full of complications, he used, for the immediate crisis, the senate's decree ordering that no one should comply with written demands from generals unless they acted in accordance with a decree of the senate.

And no such authority was attached to the letters.

Thus he carried the point, and the question was referred to the consul, and through this means the nation was relieved of an expense amounting to more than a hundred and twenty talents.

Yet he gave those who wished to slander him before Appius an opening, because he had cut short Appius's attempt to obtain the reinforcement.

## Book 28 Chapter 14: Treachery of the Cydonians

At this time the Cydonians committed a deed universally acknowledged to be dreadful and a flagrant violation of sworn faith.

For although many acts of that sort had taken place in Crete, what happened then seemed to surpass the ordinary measure even of such crimes.

They were bound to the Apolloniates not only by friendship, but by political union, and in general by partnership in all the rights that human beings regard as sacred; and concerning these obligations there stood a sworn treaty in the name of Zeus of Ida.

Yet in breach of that sworn agreement they seized the city of the Apolloniates, slaughtered the men, plundered their property, and, after dividing among themselves the women, children, city, and territory, kept possession of it.

## Book 28 Chapter 15: The Cydonians Ask Help from Eumenes

In Crete, the Cydonians were afraid of the Gortynians, because in the previous year they had narrowly escaped losing their city when Nothocrates and his party attempted to seize it. So they sent envoys to Eumenes, asking for help under the terms of their alliance.

The king selected Leon and sent him off in haste with three hundred soldiers.

When they arrived, the Cydonians handed over the keys of the gates to Leon and entrusted the city as a whole to him.

## Book 28 Chapter 16: Dissensions In Crete and Rhodes

At Rhodes the strife between rival parties kept growing more and more intense.

For when the senate's decree reached them, explaining that they should no longer pay attention to the commands of military leaders, but to the senate's own decrees, and when the majority approved the senate's foresight,

Philophron and Theaetetus and their associates seized on this opening and pressed the matter forward, saying that envoys ought to be sent to the senate, to Quintus Marcius the consul, and to Gaius, the commander of the fleet.

By that time it was already known to everyone which Roman magistrates were going to arrive in Greece.

So, though opposition was voiced, the proposal carried, and at the beginning of summer Agesilochus son of Hegesias was sent to Rome,

along with Nicagoras son of Nicander; while to the consul and the admiral they sent Agepolis,

Ariston, and Pasicrates, with instructions to renew their friendly relations with the Romans and to answer the accusations that some people were making against their city,

while Agesilochus and his colleagues were at the same time to speak about the export of grain.

What these men said to the senate, what answers the senate gave them, and how they returned after receiving every mark of goodwill, I have already recounted in the section on Italian affairs.

But on this point it is useful to remind the reader repeatedly, as I also try to do, because we are often compelled to report the audiences and negotiations of embassies before describing the circumstances of their appointment and dispatch.

For since in each year, while writing the events that properly belong to it, I try to bring together in one place a summary of the actions of each people, it is clear that this must necessarily happen in the course of the narrative.

## Book 28 Chapter 17: Antiochus Invades Egypt

Agepolis and his colleagues came to Quintus and found him encamped in Macedonia near Heracleum, where they delivered the instructions they had been given.

After hearing them out, he said that he himself paid no attention to these slanders, and he urged them as well not to listen to anyone who dared to speak against Rome. He added many other expressions of goodwill besides.

He also wrote the same sentiments in a letter to the people of Rhodes.

Agepolis was greatly encouraged by the reception as a whole; and Quintus, taking him aside privately, said that he was amazed the Rhodians were not trying to bring the present war to an end, especially since that course suited their interests so well.

Whether he did this because he suspected Antiochus, fearing that if he gained possession of Alexandria he might become a dangerous additional enemy to the Romans while the war against Perseus was dragging on,

for by this time the war over Coele-Syria had already become entangled,

or whether, seeing that the issue with Perseus would soon be decided now that the Roman armies had entered Macedonia,

and holding high hopes about the outcome, he wanted to prod the Rhodians into presenting themselves as mediators and, once they had done so, to give the Romans plausible grounds for deciding to deal with them however they saw fit,

it is not easy to say with certainty. But I think the latter explanation is more likely, judging from what happened to the Rhodians shortly afterward.

In any case, Agepolis and his colleagues then went on from there to Gaius, and after receiving from him even more exceptional marks of goodwill than from Marcius, they quickly returned to Rhodes.

When the embassy reported back, and both generals seemed to have rivaled one another in friendliness, both in their conversation and in their formal replies, all the Rhodians were lifted up in spirit and full of confidence, though not all in the same way.

Those of sound judgment were delighted by the goodwill of the Romans, but the restless and unhealthy elements reasoned among themselves that such excessive kindness was a sign that the Romans were afraid of the crisis and that events were not turning out as they had expected.

And when Agepolis happened to let slip to some of his friends that he had received private instructions from Marcius to mention before the council the question of ending the war,

then Deinon and his circle became completely convinced that the Romans were in grave difficulties.

So they sent envoys to Alexandria as well, to put an end to the war then being fought between Antiochus and Ptolemy.

## Book 28 Chapter 18: Antiochus IV. Epiphanes

King Antiochus was energetic in action, bold in his designs, and worthy of the royal office, except in his operations around Pelusium.

## Book 28 Chapter 19: Envoys Sent to Antiochus

After Antiochus took control of affairs in Egypt, Comanus and Cineas, having met in council with the king, decided to convene an advisory assembly made up of the most distinguished leaders to deliberate about the crisis at hand.

The first decision of the council was to send to Antiochus, to negotiate a settlement, the envoys from Greece who happened to be staying there.

At that time there were two embassies from the Achaean League: one to renew friendly relations, represented by Alcithus of Aegium, son of Xenophon, and Pasiadas; and another concerning the festival of the Antigoneia.

There was also an embassy from the Athenians concerning a gift, headed by Demaratus, and two sacred delegations: one for the Panathenaea, led by Callias the pancratiast, and the other concerning the Mysteries, in which Cleostratus conducted the business and delivered the speeches.

There were also present Eudemus and Hicesius from Miletus, and Apollonides and Apollonius from Clazomenae.

King Ptolemy also sent Tlepolemus and Ptolemy the rhetorician as envoys.

These men accordingly sailed up the river to meet him.

## Book 28 Chapter 20: Antiochus Epiphanes In Egypt

At the time when Antiochus occupied Egypt, the envoys sent to arrange a settlement joined the envoys from Greece. He received the men graciously and made their first reception notably splendid.

On the following day he granted them an audience and told them to speak on the matters for which they had been instructed.

The Achaean envoys spoke first, then Demaratus on behalf of the Athenians, and after him Eudemus of Miletus.

Since they were all speaking at the same moment of the same issue, the substance of their individual speeches was naturally much alike.

All of them laid the blame for what had happened on Eulaeus, and by appealing to Ptolemy's kinship with Antiochus and to the king's youth, they begged Antiochus to put aside his anger.

Antiochus agreed with all this and even strengthened their case further, but then began to speak in defense of his original rights, by which he tried to establish that the regions of Coele-Syria properly belonged to the kings of Syria,

supporting his position by referring to the dominion of Antigonus, the first ruler who held the kingdom in Syria, and citing the concessions granted to Seleucus by the kings of Macedonia after Antigonus's death.

Next, building on these points, he appealed to the final acquisition made by his father Antiochus in war,

and, above all, he denied the agreement which the men in Alexandria said had been made between the recently deceased Ptolemy and Antiochus's father: namely, that Coele-Syria was to be given to him as a dowry when he married Cleopatra, the mother of the present king.

After speaking on this case, and convincing not only himself but also those who had come to meet him that his claims were just, he then sailed to Naucratis.

There too he treated the people kindly, gave a gold coin to each of the Greeks living there, and then advanced against Alexandria.

To the envoys he promised that he would give his answer when Aristides and Theris returned to him.

For, he said, he had sent them to Ptolemy, and he wanted the envoys from Greece all to be fully informed and to serve as witnesses.

## Book 28 Chapter 21: Eulaeus Convinces Ptolemy to Give Up the Kingdom

Eulaeus the eunuch persuaded Ptolemy to gather up the money, surrender the kingdom to his enemies, and withdraw to Samothrace.

Who, if he paused to consider this, would not admit that bad companionships do the greatest harm to people?

For when a man, far removed from danger and at so great a distance from his enemies, with such great resources at hand, in control of such strong positions and such multitudes of subjects, did not even make one move toward what duty required, but immediately and without a struggle gave up a kingdom so famous and so fortunate—who would not say that this was the sign of a spirit made soft and wholly corrupted? If this had really been Ptolemy's natural disposition,

then we should have blamed nature and laid the responsibility on nothing external.

But since his later actions cleared nature of the charge, showing that Ptolemy was in fact steadfast enough and brave in danger, it is plain that one may reasonably trace the cause of the cowardice he showed at that time, and of his impulse to flee to Samothrace, to the eunuch and to the companionship in which he had been raised.

## Book 28 Chapter 22: Antiochus Leaves Alexandria

After leaving off the siege of Alexandria, Antiochus sent envoys to Rome.

These were Meleager, Sosiphanes, and Heracleides.

He set aside a hundred and fifty talents: fifty as a ceremonial gift to the Romans, and the rest to be distributed as presents to certain of the Greek cities.

## Book 28 Chapter 23: Envoys from Rhodes to Antiochus

During those same days envoys sailed from Rhodes to Alexandria to negotiate a settlement, the delegation led by Praxon; and not long afterward they also arrived at Antiochus's camp.

When the audience took place, they spoke at length, putting forward both the goodwill of their own city toward both kingdoms, the close connection of the kings with one another, and the advantage that peace would bring to each side.

But while the envoy was still speaking, the king cut him short and said there was no need for many words.

The kingdom, he said, belonged to the elder Ptolemy; with him he had long ago made terms and stood on friendly terms. If those in the city now wished to bring him back, Antiochus would not prevent it.

And he did indeed act accordingly.
