# Polybius — The Histories, Book X

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 10 Chapter 1: The Hannibalian War — The Recovery of Tarentum

The distance from the strait and the city of Rhegium to Tarentum is more than two thousand stades, and as a result this whole side of Italy has no harbor at all except those at Tarentum.

It faces the Sicilian Sea, leans toward the regions opposite Greece, and contains both the most populous non-Greek peoples and the most distinguished Greek cities.

For the Bruttians, the Lucanians, and parts of the Daunians live in this quarter of Italy, as do the Calabrians and many other peoples besides.

Likewise among the Greek cities, Rhegium, Caulonia, Locri, Croton, Metapontum, and Thurii occupy this coast.

So anyone sailing either from Sicily or from Greece to any one of the places just named is necessarily driven to put in at the harbors of Tarentum, and it is in that city that exchanges and commercial business are carried on with all who inhabit this side of Italy.

One may judge the convenience of the place from the prosperity once enjoyed by the people of Croton. Though they had only summer anchorages and a very limited place for landing, they seem to have acquired great wealth for no other reason than the natural advantage of their position—yet it is not even worth comparing that with the harbors and site of Tarentum.

Tarentum was also well placed in relation to the harbors of the Adriatic, and formerly it was even more so.

For from the Iapygian promontory as far as Sipontum, everyone who crossed over from the opposite shore and made landfall in Italy used to make Tarentum the crossing-point and used that city as a trading center for exchange and transfer of goods.

At that time Brundisium had not yet been founded.

That is why Fabius, attaching great importance to the enterprise, set all other matters aside and concentrated his planning on this one region.

## Book 10 Chapter 2: Publius Cornelius Scipio Africanus in Spain, B.C. 210-206. His Character

Since I am about to relate the deeds performed by Publius in Iberia, and indeed the whole series of actions he accomplished in his life, I think it necessary first to prepare my readers by explaining the man's character and natural bent.

For because he became almost the most illustrious man of all his predecessors, everyone wants to know what sort of man he was and what kind of nature or training enabled him to carry through so many and such great achievements.

Yet they are forced into ignorance and false opinions because those who tell his story have trifled with the truth.

That what I now say is sound will become clear from my own account to anyone able to notice what was finest and boldest in his actions.

All the other writers present him as some favorite of fortune, a man who for the most part succeeded unexpectedly and by pure chance in the designs he undertook.

They imagine such men to be more divine and more wonderful than those who act rationally in each case, not understanding that of the two, the one is merely fortunate while the other deserves praise.

Good luck may belong even to ordinary men, but what deserves praise belongs only to men of sound judgment and intelligence; and those are the men we ought to regard as most godlike and most beloved by the gods.

To my mind Publius had a nature and purpose very like Lycurgus, the lawgiver of the Spartans.

For we should not suppose that Lycurgus organized the Spartan constitution out of superstition and by paying attention to every oracle from Delphi, any more than we should think that Publius won so great an empire for his country by relying on dreams and omens.

Rather, both men saw that most people neither readily accept what is extraordinary nor dare to face danger without some hope derived from the gods.

So Lycurgus continually attached the authority of the Pythia to his own plans and thereby made his ideas more acceptable and more trustworthy.

Publius acted in much the same way, always creating among the common people the impression that his plans were formed under some divine inspiration, and by this he made those under his command bolder and readier to face the dangers of action.

But that he did everything with calculation and foresight, and that the outcomes of his enterprises always came about according to reason, will be clear from what follows.

## Book 10 Chapter 3: Scipio's First Brilliant Success

That he was generous and high-minded is generally admitted; but that he was keen in intelligence, self-possessed, and wholly intent in mind upon the object before him, no one would readily grant except those who lived with him and saw his character in full daylight, so to speak.

One of these was Gaius Laelius, who from youth to the end of his life shared in all his actions and conversations. It was he who gave me this view of Publius, because what he said seemed both likely in itself and consistent with the man's actual deeds.

He said that Publius' first notable exploit came when his father fought the cavalry engagement against Hannibal near the river called the Padus.

At that time, apparently, he was in his eighteenth year and was serving his first campaign in the field. His father had assigned him a troop of picked horsemen for protection; but when in the crisis of the battle he saw his father surrounded by the enemy with only two or three cavalrymen beside him and dangerously wounded, he first tried to urge those with him to go help his father.

When they shrank back in fear because of the number of enemies massed around him, he himself, it seems, charged recklessly and boldly into the men encircling his father.

Then, once the others too were compelled to charge in, the enemy, struck with panic, opened their ranks, and Publius the elder, saved beyond all expectation, was the first to hail his son as his savior in the hearing of all.

After winning an acknowledged reputation for courage through this exploit, he henceforth always gave himself without reserve to personal danger whenever his country placed the hopes of all upon him. That is the mark not of a commander trusting to luck, but of one with a mind fit to lead.

## Book 10 Chapter 4: His Election To the Aedileship

After this, when he had an elder brother, Lucius, and Lucius was standing for the aedileship—which among the Romans is considered almost the most distinguished office open to a young man—

and when it was customary for two patricians to be chosen, while on this occasion several candidates were seeking the office, Publius for a long time did not dare to stand for the same magistracy as his brother.

But as the election drew near, judging from the temper of the crowd that his brother would not easily gain the office, and seeing that the people felt great goodwill toward himself, he concluded that Lucius could achieve his aim only if the two of them made their bid together in full agreement. So he devised the following plan.

He saw his mother going about the temples and sacrificing to the gods for his brother, full of expectation about the outcome; and she was the only parent whose feelings mattered to him at the time—

for his father had by then sailed to Iberia after being appointed general for the operations already described. So he told his mother that he had now seen the same dream twice.

In the dream, he said, he and his brother had both been elected aediles and were coming up from the Forum toward their house, when she met them at the doorway and embraced and kissed them.

She at once reacted as women often do and cried out, "If only I might live to see that day!"

"Mother," he said, "shall we put it to the test?" When she agreed, supposing that he would never really dare but was only joking in the moment—for he was still very young—he asked her at once to prepare for him a splendid toga, since that is the customary dress for candidates seeking office.

## Book 10 Chapter 5: Publius and Lucius Become Aediles

His mother gave no further thought to what he had said, but Publius, after getting the splendid candidate's dress first, went to the Forum while she was still asleep.

The crowd, astonished both by the unexpectedness of it and by the goodwill they already felt toward him, welcomed him enthusiastically; and when he stepped forward to the appointed place and stood beside his brother,

the people not only invested Publius with the office, but because of him gave it to his brother as well, and so both of them came back to the house as elected aediles.

When the news suddenly reached their mother, she ran in delight to meet them at the doorway and embraced the young men with overflowing joy.

So to everyone who had previously heard of the dream, Publius seemed to have spoken with the gods not only in sleep, but even more in waking life and broad daylight.

In fact there had been no dream at all. He was simply kind, generous, and winning in his manner, and he had calculated from this the goodwill the crowd felt toward him.

Then, by fitting his move shrewdly both to the mood of the people and to his mother's expectations, he not only achieved his purpose but also appeared to be acting under some divine prompting.

For those who cannot accurately observe occasions, causes, and the disposition of each situation—whether through dullness of nature, or through inexperience and laziness—attribute to the gods and to fortune things that are actually accomplished by intelligence, calculation, and foresight.

I have said this for the sake of my readers, so that they may not be swept along by the common but false opinion about him and so pass over the noblest and finest of his qualities—I mean his resourcefulness and his tireless energy.

This will become even clearer from his actual deeds.

## Book 10 Chapter 6: Speech of Publius Scipio to the Soldiers

When the forces had been assembled, he urged them not to be cast down by the disaster that had already happened.

Romans, he said, had never been defeated by Carthaginians in courage, but only through the treachery of the Celtiberians and through rashness, when the generals had been cut off from one another because they trusted the alliance of those peoples.

Now, however, both of those disadvantages belonged to the enemy. Their camps were widely separated from each other, and by treating their allies with arrogance they had estranged them all and made them hostile to themselves.

That was why some were already sending messages to the Romans, while the rest, as soon as they took heart and saw them across the river, would gladly come over—not so much out of goodwill toward Rome as from eagerness to punish Carthaginian outrage.

Most important of all, the enemy commanders were quarreling among themselves and would not choose to fight against the Romans all together; if forced to face danger separately, they would be easy enough to handle.

With these facts in view, he urged the men to cross the river confidently; as for what was to come afterward, he said that he and the other commanders would take care of it.

After saying this, he left Marcus, his colleague, with three thousand infantry and five hundred cavalry to wait by the crossing and support the allies on the near side of the river; he himself took the rest of the army across, keeping his true purpose hidden from everyone.

For he had decided to do none of the things he had publicly spoken of, but instead intended to attempt a sudden siege of the city in Iberia called Carthage.

This may be taken as the first and greatest proof of the judgment I have just described.

At only twenty-seven years of age, he first committed himself to an enterprise which, because of the scale of previous disasters, most people regarded as entirely hopeless.

Second, once he had undertaken it, he passed over the obvious and commonplace measures that everyone could see and instead devised and proposed actions that the enemy themselves did not anticipate.

None of this was done apart from the most exact calculation.

## Book 10 Chapter 7: He Determines To Attack Carthagena

From the very beginning, while still at Rome, he investigated carefully and learned about the treachery of the Celtiberians and the separation of the Roman camps, and from this he reasoned that the disaster that had overtaken his father had happened because of exactly those causes.

So he was not terrified by the Carthaginians, nor was his spirit broken as happened with most men.

Later, when he heard that the allies north of the Ebro remained loyal to Rome, while the Carthaginian commanders were quarreling among themselves and abusing the peoples under their rule, he felt confident about the expedition, trusting not in luck but in his own reasoning.

And when he arrived in Iberia, stirring everyone up and asking each person what he knew about the enemy, he discovered that the Carthaginian forces had been divided into three parts.

He learned that Mago was staying this side of the Pillars of Heracles among the people called the Conii, that Hasdrubal son of Gisco was near the mouth of the Tagus in Lusitania, and that the other Hasdrubal was besieging a certain city among the Carpetani; and none of them, he found, was less than a ten days' march from New Carthage.

He therefore judged that, if he decided to engage the enemy in open battle, it would be altogether too dangerous to risk fighting all three at once, both because his predecessors had been defeated and because the enemy greatly outnumbered him.

But if he hurried to fight one of them and that commander avoided battle, while the other forces came up and enclosed him somewhere, he feared lest the same disasters that had fallen on his uncle Gnaeus and his father Publius might happen to him as well.

## Book 10 Chapter 8: Why New Carthage is a Desirable Target

So he rejected that course. But when he learned that the Carthage just mentioned was of the greatest importance to the enemy and at the same time was doing him the greatest harm in the present war, he spent the winter investigating every detail about it through men who knew the place.

First he heard that it alone, or almost alone, among the cities of Iberia possessed harbors suitable for a fleet and naval forces, and that for the Carthaginians it was extremely well situated for the voyage across the open sea from Libya.

Next, he learned that the Carthaginians kept there the bulk of their money and all the baggage of their armies, and also the hostages from the whole of Iberia.

Most important of all, the fighting men guarding the citadel numbered about a thousand, because no one could possibly imagine that, while the Carthaginians held almost all Iberia, anyone would so much as conceive the idea of besieging that city.

As for the rest of the population, although it was very large, it consisted entirely of craftsmen, laborers, and seafaring people, men far removed from military experience; and he judged that this would actually tell against the city if an enemy appeared unexpectedly.

Nor was he ignorant of the position of Carthage, its fortifications, or the character of the lagoon surrounding it.

Through certain fishermen familiar with those waters he found out that the lagoon was generally muddy and for the most part fordable, and that toward late afternoon it usually receded each day.

From all this he reasoned that, if he succeeded in the attempt, he would not only damage the enemy but also bring great advantage to his own cause.

And even if he failed in his design, he believed he could still save his men because he controlled the sea, provided that once he arrived he secured his camp—and this would be easy, since the enemy forces were drawn off far away.

So, setting every other concern aside, he devoted himself wholly to this enterprise.

## Book 10 Chapter 9: Laelius and Scipio Proceed to New Carthage

He made all his preparations during the winter. Though the plan he had conceived was so bold, and though he was of the age I have just mentioned, he kept it hidden from everyone except Gaius Laelius until he himself judged it time to reveal it.

Yet although historians agree that these calculations were his, once they come to the successful outcome of the operation they somehow cease to credit the man and his foresight and instead assign the success to the gods and to fortune.

And this despite probability, despite the testimony of those who lived with him, and despite the fact that Publius himself clearly stated in a letter to Philip that it was by using the very calculations I have just set out that he undertook both the Iberian campaign as a whole and, in particular, the siege of Carthage.

At that time, then, he gave secret orders to Gaius Laelius, commander of the fleet, to sail against the city I have mentioned.

For Laelius alone knew his design, as I said above.

He himself took the land forces and marched in haste. He had with him twenty-five thousand infantry and two thousand five hundred cavalry.

Arriving on the seventh day, he encamped on the northern side of the city and fortified the outer face of his camp with a double ditch and palisade running from sea to sea, but on the side toward the city he made no fortification at all, since the nature of the place itself gave him sufficient security.

Since I am about to describe both the siege and the capture of the city, I think it necessary to indicate for my readers, at least in outline, the surrounding country and the position of the place itself.

## Book 10 Chapter 10: Topography of Carthagena

It lies about halfway along the coast of Iberia in a gulf that opens toward the southwest. The gulf extends about twenty stades inland and is about ten stades wide at its entrance; and the whole gulf takes on the character of a harbor for the following reason.

An island lies at its mouth, leaving only a narrow entrance on each side.

Because this island receives the force of the open sea, the whole gulf remains calm, except when southwest winds, driving in through either channel, raise a swell.

With all other winds it is free from surf because the surrounding land encloses it.

At the inner end of the gulf there projects a hill, almost like a peninsula, on which the city stands. It is enclosed by the sea on the east and south, and on the west by a lagoon that also reaches round toward the north.

So the remaining strip of ground as far as the sea on the other side—the strip that joins the city to the mainland—is no more than two stades across.

The city itself lies in a hollow. On the southern side it has a level approach from the sea, but on the other sides it is enclosed by hills, two of them steep and rugged, the other three much lower but rocky and difficult to climb.

The largest of these lies to the east of the city and stretches out toward the sea; on it stands a temple of Asclepius.

Opposite it on the west is another hill in a corresponding position, on which a richly built palace stands, said to have been made by Hasdrubal when he was aiming at monarchical power.

The remaining three lower hills enclose the city on the north.

Of these three, the one sloping eastward is called the Hill of Hephaestus; next to it is that of Aletes—who, it seems, received honors equal to those of the gods because he discovered the silver mines—and the third is called the Hill of Cronus.

The lagoon has been artificially connected with the neighboring sea for the use of seafaring people.

And over the opening cut through the bank between them a bridge has been built, so that pack animals and carts can carry in the necessities brought from the countryside.

## Book 10 Chapter 11: Scipio Briefs His Troops

Given this arrangement of the terrain, the Romans' camp on its inner side was secured without any artificial work by the lagoon and the sea beyond it.

He left unfenced the strip of ground between them that joined the city to the mainland and lay opposite the middle of his camp,

either to overawe the enemy or because the arrangement suited his plan, so that he might have unimpeded movement both when sending troops out and when drawing them back into camp.

The city wall had formerly had a circuit of not more than twenty stades—though I know many have said forty; that is false, and I speak not from hearsay but from personal inspection—and in our own day it has been reduced still further.

When the fleet also arrived at the proper time, Publius decided to gather the troops and encourage them, using not some other set of arguments but the very reasoning by which he had persuaded himself, and which I have just explained in detail.

After showing that the undertaking was feasible and summing up how their success would weaken the enemy and strengthen their own position, he went on to promise golden crowns to the first men who mounted the wall, and the customary rewards to those who showed distinguished bravery.

Finally he said that Poseidon had appeared to him in a dream at the very beginning and had indicated this enterprise to him, promising that at the actual moment of action he would cooperate in so visible a way that the whole army would plainly recognize his help.

This appeal, blending exact reasoning with promises of golden crowns and, beyond all, the providence of a god, aroused great eagerness and zeal among the young soldiers.

## Book 10 Chapter 12: The Attack On Carthagena Begun

On the next day he stationed ships fitted with every kind of missile along the places accessible from the sea, giving the command to Gaius; on land he assigned about two thousand of the strongest men to accompany the ladder-carriers and began the siege at the third hour.

Mago, who had charge of the city, divided the force of a thousand men under him: half he left on the citadel, and the rest he posted on the eastern hill.

Of the remaining inhabitants he armed about two thousand of the strongest with whatever weapons were available in the city and stationed them at the gate leading to the isthmus and the enemy camp, while ordering the rest to help wherever they could along every part of the wall.

As soon as Publius gave the signal for the assault by trumpet, Mago sent these armed men out through the gate, convinced that he would terrify the enemy and altogether break up their attack.

When they charged boldly against the Romans drawn up along the isthmus in front of the camp, a fierce battle began, with a hard-fought shouting match on both sides, each side encouraged by its own men—the Romans by those in the camp, the Carthaginians by those in the city.

But the help brought up by reserves was not equal, since for the Carthaginians reinforcement had to come out through a single gate and from nearly two stades away, whereas the Romans had their support close at hand and across a broad front. For this reason the battle was uneven.

Publius had deliberately stationed his men close to the camp in order to draw the enemy as far out as possible, clearly knowing that if he destroyed these men, who were like the cutting edge of the city's population, the whole body would lose heart and no one inside would dare to come out of the gate again.

For a while the fighting remained evenly matched, since picked men on both sides had been chosen for the struggle. But at last the Carthaginians were forced back by sheer weight because fresh troops kept coming in from the Roman camp, and they turned to flight.

Many of them were killed in the battle itself and in the retreat, but most of all they were crushed by one another as they jammed into the gate.

When that happened, the whole crowd inside the city was so panic-stricken that even the men on the walls fled.

So the Romans came very close at that moment to bursting in together with the fugitives; in any case, they were able to plant their ladders against the wall in complete safety.

## Book 10 Chapter 13: Double Assault By The Romans

Publius threw himself into the danger, yet he did so with as much safety as circumstances allowed.

For he had with him three men carrying shields, who set them in place and screened him from the line of the wall, thus providing him protection.

So by moving along the flanks and the higher ground he contributed greatly to what was needed.

He could both see what was happening and, by being seen by everyone himself, inspire zeal in those engaged in the struggle.

As a result, nothing necessary for the crisis was left undone; whenever the moment indicated that something was needed for the task at hand, it was always immediately supplied.

Now when the foremost men boldly began the ascent on the ladders, it was not so much the number of the defenders as the sheer height of the walls that made the assault dangerous.

For that reason the defenders on the walls took even more courage, seeing the distress into which the attackers were falling.

Some ladders broke because so many men were mounting them at once on account of their great length; on others, the first climbers became dizzy from the height and, with very little resistance from the defenders, flung themselves from the ladders.

And when the defenders tried hurling down beams or anything of that kind from the battlements, the assailants all at once recoiled and were driven to the ground.

Yet even when such obstacles arose, nothing was enough to check the onward rush and fury of the Romans; as soon as the front men fell, those behind stepped into the space left open.

But when the day had already advanced and the soldiers were exhausted by the hardship, the general sounded a recall with the trumpets for those making the assault.

## Book 10 Chapter 14: Scipio Creates a Distraction

The men inside the city were overjoyed, believing that they had beaten off the danger.

But Publius, expecting that the time of the ebb was now approaching, prepared five hundred men with ladders by the lagoon.

At the gate and along the isthmus he refreshed the soldiers, encouraged them, and supplied more ladders than before, so that the wall was again packed continuously with men mounting it.

And when the signal for battle was given and they set the ladders against the wall and began climbing boldly at every point, great confusion and panic arose among those inside.

For just when they thought they had been freed from the pressure, they saw the danger beginning all over again in a fresh assault.

At the same time their missiles were already giving out, and the loss of so many men had driven them into discouragement; though distressed by what was happening, they still defended themselves as best they could.

At the very height of the struggle on the ladders, the ebb began.

The water little by little left the edges of the lagoon, and through the mouth the current poured strongly and in a mass into the sea beyond, so that to those who saw it without forethought the sight seemed unbelievable.

Publius, with guides ready, urged the men prepared for this task to enter the water and take courage.

For he was naturally gifted, if in anything, in the power to inspire boldness and make those he addressed share his confidence.

So when they obeyed and raced one another through the marsh, the whole camp supposed that what was happening came from some divine providence.

Remembering what had been said about Poseidon and Publius' promise in his address, they were so fired in spirit that, locking shields together and forcing their way up to the gate from outside, they tried to chop through the doors with axes and hatchets.

Meanwhile those who had come through the marsh reached the wall, found the battlements deserted, not only set up the ladders safely, but climbed up and took possession of the wall without a fight.

For the defenders inside were distracted by the attacks elsewhere, especially those on the isthmus and at the gate there, and had never expected that the enemy would come near the wall on the side of the lagoon.

Most of all, the confused shouting and the mixed tumult of the crowd prevented them from either hearing or seeing anything they needed to do.

## Book 10 Chapter 15: Carthagena Captured and Looted

Once the Romans had gained control of the wall, they first moved along the circuit, pushing off the enemy as they went, their equipment being especially useful for that kind of work.

But when they came to the gate, some climbed down and cut through the bars, others burst in from outside, and those who were forcing their way up the ladders toward the isthmus, now getting the better of the defenders, took possession of the battlements.

In this way the walls were finally taken, while the men who entered through the gate captured the hill to the east, after routing the guards stationed there.

When Publius judged that enough troops had now entered, he let most of them loose against those inside the city, according to Roman custom, ordering them to kill everyone they met and spare no one, and not to turn to plunder until the signal was given.

I think they do this to strike terror. That is why in cities captured by the Romans one may often see not only human beings lying slaughtered, but even dogs cut in half and the limbs of other animals hacked off.

On this occasion such slaughter was especially great because of the large numbers caught in the city.

Publius himself, with about a thousand men, advanced against the citadel. When he came near, Mago at first tried to defend it, but then, realizing clearly that the city had now been taken for good, sent to ask for his own safety and surrendered the citadel.

When this had happened and the signal was given, the men stopped killing and turned to plunder.

When night came, those to whom it had been assigned remained in camp; the general himself bivouacked on the citadel with the thousand men, while the rest he had called out of the houses through the tribunes and ordered to gather all the booty in the marketplace by standards and sleep there beside it.

He then summoned the light-armed troops from the camp and posted them on the eastern hill.

In this way the Romans became masters of Carthage in Iberia.

## Book 10 Chapter 16: How the Romans Distribute Booty

This, then, is how the Romans became masters of the city. On the next day, when the baggage of those who had served with the Carthaginian armies, along with the property of the townsmen and the artisans, had been collected in the marketplace, the tribunes divided it among the several camps according to Roman custom.

The Roman method in capturing cities is somewhat as follows. Sometimes a certain number of men from each maniple are assigned to the task, according to the size of the city; sometimes whole maniples are assigned in turn.

But never are more than half the men detailed for this work. The rest remain in their ranks as a reserve, sometimes outside the city and sometimes inside, always ready for whatever is indicated.

Since their force is for the most part divided into two Roman camps and two allied camps, though sometimes and more rarely all four are assembled together, all who are assigned to plunder bring what they obtain back to their own camps.

Then, after the booty is sold, the tribunes distribute the proceeds equally to all, not only to those who stayed behind in reserve, but also to those guarding the tents, to the sick, and to those sent away on some service.

As for the rule that no one is to embezzle anything from the plunder, but must keep faith according to the oath

which all swear when they first assemble in camp and are about to march into enemy territory, I have spoken of that more fully before in the discussion of the constitution.

So when half the army turns to plunder while the other half preserves the ranks and serves as reserve, the Romans never put the whole operation at risk through greed.

For since the hope of profit is not a matter of distrust between them, but stands equally open both to those remaining in reserve and to those doing the plundering, no one abandons his rank.

## Book 10 Chapter 17: Scipio's Treatment of the Prisoners

This is the thing that most often harms other armies. Since most men endure hardship and face danger for the sake of gain, it is obvious that when such an opportunity appears, those left in reserve or in camp can scarcely be expected to hold back, because among most peoples the common belief is that whatever a man seizes belongs to the one who took it.

For even if a king or careful general orders the profits to be brought into the common stock, everyone still thinks whatever he can conceal is his own.

So when the mass of soldiers rushes after plunder and cannot be controlled, whole armies are put in danger.

Indeed, many who have successfully carried out their attack—sometimes storming an enemy camp, sometimes taking a city—have not only been driven out again, but completely ruined, for no reason other than this.

That is why commanders ought to show more care and foresight about nothing else so much as this: that whenever such an occasion arises, the many should feel that the hope of profit stands equally open to all.

At that time, then, while the tribunes were occupied with the disposal of the spoils, the Roman general, when the whole number of prisoners had been assembled—it came to a little less than ten thousand—ordered them first to be separated into the citizen men and women and their children, and second, the craftsmen.

Once this had been done, he addressed the citizens, urged them to be well disposed toward Rome and remember the favor done them, and released them all to go back to their homes.

They departed weeping and rejoicing together at their unexpected deliverance, after bowing in reverence before the general.

To the craftsmen he said that for the present they were public property of Rome; but if each showed goodwill and zeal in his own craft, he promised them freedom once the war against the Carthaginians had been brought to a successful conclusion.

He ordered these men to be enrolled with the quaestor and appointed one Roman overseer for every thirty of them, their total number being about two thousand.

From the rest of the prisoners he selected the strongest, those in the prime of age and appearance, and assigned them to his crews.

In this way he increased all the sailors by one half and manned the captured ships too, so that on each vessel the number of men was now only a little short of double what it had previously been.

For the captured ships numbered eighteen, while those he had from the beginning were thirty-five.

To these men too he promised freedom on similar terms, if they showed loyalty and zeal until they had defeated the Carthaginians in the war.

By handling the prisoners in this way he inspired the citizens with strong goodwill and confidence both toward himself and toward Roman policy, while he stirred great eagerness in the craftsmen by the hope of freedom.

And by increasing his naval strength by half at just the right moment through his foresight—

## Book 10 Chapter 18: Scipio's Treatment of Women

After this he separated Mago and the Carthaginians who were with him. Of these, two belonged to the council of elders and fifteen to the senate.

He entrusted them to Gaius Laelius, ordering him to take proper care of the men.

Next he summoned the hostages, who were more than three hundred in number. He brought the children forward one by one,

stroked them with his hand, and told them to take courage, because in a few days they would see their parents.

He urged all the rest together to be cheerful and to write letters to their own cities and relatives, first saying that they were safe and well treated, and second that the Romans wished to restore them all safely to their homes if their kinsmen chose alliance with Rome.

After saying this, and having previously selected from the spoils whatever would be most useful for his purpose, he distributed to each suitable gifts according to family and age: to the girls earrings and bracelets, to the young men daggers and swords.

Among the captive women, the wife of Mandonius—who was brother of Andobales, king of the Ilergetes—fell at his feet and with tears begged him to show more regard for their honor than the Carthaginians had done. Moved with sympathy, he asked what necessities they lacked.

For the woman was older and bore herself with a certain noble dignity.

When she said nothing, he called those who had been assigned to care for the women.

When they came and explained that all necessary provisions were being supplied to them in abundance, the woman again clasped his knees and repeated the same plea. Publius, now still more perplexed and suspecting that the appointed guardians were neglecting their duty and falsely reporting otherwise for the moment, told the women to take courage.

He himself, he said, would appoint others to look after them, so that they should lack nothing necessary.

After pausing a little, she said, "General, you do not understand what we are asking if you think it is food we now beg from you."

Then at last Publius grasped the woman's meaning, and as he saw before his eyes the youthful bloom of Andobales' daughters and of many other noble maidens, he was compelled to weep, while the woman in a few words made clear the danger of their situation.

So he showed plainly at once that he understood what had been said, and taking her by the right hand told both her and the others to be of good courage.

He would take thought for them as though they were his own sisters and daughters, and would appoint trustworthy men to care for them in accordance with what he had promised.

## Book 10 Chapter 19: The Money

After this he handed over to the quaestors whatever public money of the Carthaginians had been captured.

This amounted to more than six hundred talents, so that when it was added to the four hundred he had brought with him from Rome, the entire sum of funds at his disposal came to more than a thousand talents.

At about this time some young Romans came upon a maiden in the full bloom of age and beauty, far surpassing the other women. Knowing that Publius was fond of women, they brought her to him and, placing her before him, said they gave the girl to him as a gift.

He was struck and amazed by her beauty, and said that if he had been a private man, he could have received no gift more pleasing than this; but as a general, none could have been less welcome.

By this answer, as it seems to me, he meant to suggest that while in times of rest and leisure such pleasures and pastimes may be among the sweetest delights for young men, in times of action they become very great hindrances both to body and mind.

So he thanked the young men, but called the girl's father and, giving her back into his hands, told him to marry her to whichever of her fellow citizens he chose.

Thus, by displaying both self-command and moderation, he won great admiration from those under his authority.

After settling these matters and handing over the rest of the prisoners to the tribunes, he sent Gaius Laelius to Rome on a quinquereme, entrusting to him the Carthaginians and the most distinguished of the other prisoners, to report at home what had taken place.

For since most people there had already given up hope of success in Iberia, he knew clearly that once this news was announced they would take heart again and support the enterprise with much greater vigor.

## Book 10 Chapter 20: Scipio's Training Regimen

He himself stayed for some time at Carthage and continually trained the naval forces, while he also laid down for the tribunes something like the following method for exercising the infantry camps.

On the first day he ordered them to run thirty stades in full armor; on the second, all were to clean, maintain, and inspect their equipment openly; on the next they were to rest and take their ease.

On the day after that some were to fence with wooden swords covered in leather and tipped with buttons, while others were to cast javelins with blunted heads; on the fifth day they were to return again to the same runs and begin the cycle from the start.

And so that there should be no lack either of weapons for practice or of those needed for actual war, he devoted the greatest care to the craftsmen.

He had assigned careful men over this work in divisions, as I said before; but he himself also made the rounds every day and personally saw to the supplies furnished to each group.

So while the infantry around the city were busy with drills and exercises, the naval forces with practice cruises and rowing at sea, and the people in the city sharpening, forging, carpentry work, and in general every sort of effort in the making of arms,

no one who saw that city then, to use Xenophon's phrase, would have hesitated to call it a workshop of war.

And when he thought that everything had been well prepared and adequately exercised for the tasks ahead, he secured the city by garrisons and repairs to the walls, then broke camp with both army and fleet and advanced on the march toward Tarraco, taking the hostages with him.

## Book 10 Chapter 21: Philopoemen of Megalopolis

Euryleon, the Achaean general, was timid and unsuited to military action.

And now that the course of my narrative has brought me to the beginning of Philopoemen's deeds, I think it proper, just as I have tried to indicate the training and character of other notable men, to do something similar for him as well.

For it is absurd that historians should describe the foundations of cities—when, how, and by whom they were founded—and even set out their circumstances and difficulties in detail, yet keep silence about the upbringing and ambitions of the men who managed whole states, though this subject is of far greater importance.

For in proportion as one can be stirred more readily to emulation and imitation by living men than by lifeless constructions, in that same proportion the account of such men is more valuable for correcting and improving its hearers.

If I had not already composed a separate work about him, in which I explained who he was, what family he came from, and what principles guided him in youth, it would now be necessary to discuss each of those points.

But since I have already treated him in three books outside the present history, setting forth both his early education and his most distinguished deeds,

it is plain that in the present narrative it is fitting to omit a detailed account of his youthful upbringing and ambitions, and instead to add in detail the achievements of his prime, which there were given only in summary, so that I may preserve the proper character of each work.

For just as that earlier composition, being encomiastic, required a condensed account of his actions with deliberate heightening, so history, which shares in both praise and blame, demands what is true, demonstrated, and accompanied by the reasoning that belongs to each action.

## Book 10 Chapter 22: His Birth and Education

Philopoemen, then, was well born to begin with, for he came from one of the most distinguished families in Arcadia. He was brought up and educated by Cleander of Mantinea, who had been his father's guest-friend and was at that time living in exile, but was one of the foremost men of the Mantineans.

When he came of age, he became a devoted follower of Ecdemus and Demophanes, men of Megalopolis by birth who, after fleeing from tyrants and spending their exile with the philosopher Arcesilaus, not only freed their own fatherland by plotting against Aristodemus the tyrant,

but also helped to overthrow Nicocles, tyrant of Sicyon, by sharing in Aratus' enterprise; and later, when invited by the Cyrenaeans, they came forward in a brilliant way and preserved liberty for them as well.

Living for a long time in early youth with such men, Philopoemen immediately distinguished himself from his contemporaries both in endurance and daring on the hunt and in military exercises.

He was also careful and frugal in his way of life, having learned from those men the conviction that no one can lead public affairs well if he neglects his own private life, nor can anyone keep his hands off public property if he lives more extravagantly than his own means allow.

Later, when the Achaeans appointed him hipparch at the time in question, and he took over the cavalry squadrons in a state of complete ruin and the men utterly discouraged,

he not only made them better than they had been themselves, but in a short time stronger than their enemies, by bringing them all into a regime of real training and purposeful ambition.

For most of those appointed to this office either, because of their own incompetence in cavalry matters, do not dare to command even what is necessary to those under them,

or else, because they are aiming at the generalship, court the young men through this office and try to secure them as friendly supporters for the future, not rebuking where rebuke is needed—the very thing by which public interests are preserved—but covering over faults and, for the sake of a petty favor, greatly harming those who trust them.

And if ever some magistrates are both physically capable and eager to keep their hands off public wealth, even these often do more harm by misguided zeal to negligent infantrymen, and still more to cavalrymen.

## Book 10 Chapter 23: Philopoemen's Reforms

The maneuvers which he thought cavalry ought to become accustomed to, as suitable for every occasion, were these:

for the individual horseman, wheeling to the rein and then back toward the spear side, and besides this turning right round and changing front;

for the squadron, wheeling about, turning, and also making a wider turning movement;

besides these, rapid deployments by troop and by double troop from either wing, and sometimes from the center, and then rallying again with a check into troops, squadrons, and whole cavalry commands;

and further, extension of the line on either wing, either by filling intervals or by carrying the rear ranks outward.

As for oblique movements, he said they needed no special drill, since they are almost the same as ordinary marching order.

After this, cavalry had to be drilled in charges against the enemy and in withdrawals in every sort of maneuver, to such a point that they could advance at tremendous speed, so long as they remained aligned side by side and in rank, while also preserving the proper intervals between squadrons.

For when cavalry breaks up its order in squadrons and then chooses to risk an engagement, nothing is more dangerous or more useless.

After laying these instructions before both the rank and file and their officers, he again went about the cities, examining first whether the men were carrying out the commands they had been given, and second whether the magistrates in each city had mastered the proper way to give those commands clearly and correctly.

For he judged that nothing was more necessary in practice than experienced leadership in the subordinate officers.

## Book 10 Chapter 24: How to Drill a Large Company

After making these preliminary arrangements in the way described, he assembled the cavalry from the cities into one place, and himself carried out the maneuvers and personally directed the whole handling of the drill.

He did not ride in front of them all, as commanders do nowadays, imagining that the foremost position is the proper one for a leader.

For what could be more unscientific and at the same time more dangerous in a commander than to be seen by all those under him while seeing none of them himself?

In such maneuvers a hipparch ought to display not military grandeur, but command experience and practical ability, placing himself sometimes in front, sometimes in the rear, and sometimes in the middle.

That is exactly what this man did, riding along the line and personally inspecting everyone, giving further explanation to those who were at a loss and correcting at once every mistake made at the beginning.

Such mistakes, however, were very few and infrequent because of the earlier care bestowed on each part of the training.

Demetrius of Phalerum has expressed the same principle in words, saying that just as in building, if each brick is laid individually and each course receives proper attention, so in an army, precision in each man and each company makes the whole force strong.

## Book 10 Chapter 25: Philip's Increasing Deterioration

For the present situation, he said, is very like the arrangement and handling of troops in battle.

In battle it is usually the light-armed and most active part of the force that first runs the risk and is first destroyed, while the phalanx and the heavily armed take the credit for the result.

So now in the same way the Aetolians and those Peloponnesians allied with them are the ones who run the danger, while the Romans remain in reserve, standing like a phalanx.

And if these allies fail and are destroyed, the Romans will simply turn away from the affair and go off unharmed.

But if they win—which may the gods prevent!—then together with them the Romans will bring the rest of the Greeks also under their power.

For every democratic alliance has great need of friendship and support because of the irrationality that arises among multitudes.

## Book 10 Chapter 26: Philip V

When King Philip of Macedon had finished presiding over the Nemean games, he returned again to Argos and laid aside the diadem and the purple robe, wishing to present himself as a man equal to the crowd, mild, and democratic.

But the more democratic the dress he put on, the greater and more monarchical was the power he claimed.

For he was no longer merely trying widows, nor content with seducing married women; any woman who pleased him he now summoned by direct order, and those who did not readily obey he insulted by leading drunken revels to their houses.

He would summon some women's sons and others' husbands on absurd pretexts and bully them, displaying great licentiousness and lawlessness.

And so, by abusing the freedom he enjoyed while staying among them, he deeply offended many of the Achaeans, and especially the respectable men among them.

Yet because war hemmed them in on every side, they were compelled to endure and bear what was against their nature.

No previous ruler, I think, possessed greater natural advantages for kingship than Philip, nor greater faults than this same king.

And it seems to me that his good qualities belonged to him by nature, while his bad ones came upon him as he advanced in age, just as some horses acquire faults as they grow old.

Though I do not, like other historians, put such judgments in prefaces,

but rather fit the appropriate remarks to the events themselves whenever occasion calls and pronounce on kings and notable men there, because I think that is the more suitable method both for writer and reader.

## Book 10 Chapter 27: Medea and the Palace at Ecbatana

Media, then, is among the most important of the Asiatic kingdoms both in the size of its territory and in the number and quality of its men, and likewise of its horses.

For this country supplies almost all Asia with these animals, and the royal breeding establishments for horses are entrusted to the Medes because of the natural excellence of the land.

It is surrounded by Greek cities founded at Alexander's direction, to guard it against the neighboring barbarians, with the exception of Ecbatana.

This city is built in the northern parts of Media, opposite those regions of Asia that face the Maeotis and the Euxine.

It had originally been the royal residence of the Medes, and in wealth and magnificence of construction it seems to have greatly surpassed the other cities.

It lies under the foothills by Mount Orontes and has no wall, but within it possesses an artificial citadel, built with remarkable strength for defense.

Below this stands the royal palace, about which both speaking in detail and passing over in silence present certain difficulties.

For to those who like to produce astonishing narratives and are accustomed to describing things with amplification and show, the city offers a splendid subject; but to those who approach with caution anything that goes beyond common belief, it causes hesitation and embarrassment.

Still, the palace is in size almost seven stades in circuit, and in the costly splendor of its individual structures shows clearly how rich its original founders were.

For since all the timber was cedar and cypress, none of it was left bare: the beams, the ceiling-panels, and the columns in the porticoes and peristyles were sheathed, some with silver plates and others with gold, and all the roof-tiles were of silver.

Most of these had been stripped off during Alexander's invasion with the Macedonians, and the rest under the rule of Antigonus and Seleucus Nicator.

Yet even when Antiochus arrived, the temple itself, called that of Aene, still had the surrounding columns gilded, and a number of silver tiles were piled up within it; a few gold bricks remained, and more of silver.

From all these objects the coined royal money that was struck came to a sum little short of four thousand talents.

## Book 10 Chapter 28: Antiochus the Great In Media

Arsaces expected that Antiochus would come as far as these regions, but would not dare to push across the desert adjoining them with so large an army, especially because of the lack of water.

For no water appears on the surface in the district just mentioned, but there are many underground channels with well-shafts sunk through the desert, places unknown to those who lack experience of the country.

The true account handed down by the natives about these works is that in the days when the Persians ruled Asia, they granted to those who brought spring water into districts previously unirrigated the right to enjoy the land for five generations.

So, since Taurus sends down many large streams, men undertook every expense and hardship and from great distances built these underground channels, with the result that even in our own day those who use the water do not know where the channels originally receive their flow.

But when Arsaces saw that Antiochus was determined on the march through the desert, he then tried to fill in and destroy the wells.

The king, once this had been reported to him, sent out Nicomedes again with a thousand horse. They found that Arsaces had withdrawn with the main force, though some cavalrymen were destroying the mouths of the channels. Charging them at once, they routed them and forced them to flee, then returned to Antiochus.

The king crossed the desert and came to the city called Hecatompylos, which lies in the middle of Parthia; and because the roads leading to all the surrounding regions meet there, the place has received its name from that fact.

## Book 10 Chapter 29: Antiochus Moves into Hyrcania

After giving his army a rest there, Antiochus reasoned that if Arsaces had been able to decide matters by battle against him, he would neither have withdrawn from his own country nor have sought ground better suited to his own forces than that around Hecatompylos.

But since he was retreating, it was clear to anyone judging correctly that he had some other plan in mind. Antiochus therefore decided to advance into Hyrcania.

When he reached Tagae and learned from the natives how difficult the country was that he had to traverse until he should come to the ridges of Mount Labus descending into Hyrcania, and how many barbarians were posted at the difficult points,

he resolved to array the light-armed troops and divide their officers according to the routes each section would need to take. He did the same with the workmen, who as they marched along were to make the ground seized by the light troops passable for the phalanx and the baggage animals.

Having formed this plan, he gave the first division to Diogenes, assigning him archers, slingers, and such mountain men as could throw javelins and stones—men who kept no fixed order, but were always engaging man by man as occasion and terrain required, and rendered the most practical service in rough country.

Behind these he ordered about two thousand Cretan shield-bearers under Polyxenidas of Rhodes, and in the rear mailed troops and thureophoroi under Nicomedes of Cos and Nicolaus the Aetolian.

## Book 10 Chapter 30: Fighting On Mount Labus

But as these men advanced, the ruggedness and narrowness of the ground proved far more difficult than the king had expected.

The whole ascent was about three hundred stades in length, and for most of that distance the march had to be made through a deep winter-torrent ravine, in which many rocks and trees hurled down naturally from the cliffs above had made the passage hard, while much more had been added by the barbarians for the same purpose.

For they had cut down continuous stretches of trees and piled up masses of enormous stones. They themselves occupied all along the gorge the advantageous heights from which they could both attack and remain secure; so that, if they had not made a mistake, Antiochus would have found the attempt wholly beyond his power and given it up.

They had prepared and occupied the ground on the assumption that the enemy would necessarily be forced to make the ascent by the gorge itself.

What they did not consider was that, while the phalanx and baggage indeed could only march as they supposed, the light-armed and active troops were not incapable of climbing by the bare rocks themselves.

So as soon as Diogenes and his men came up to the first outpost and made their ascent outside the ravine, the whole situation took on a different shape.

For the action itself immediately showed what had to be done: Diogenes' men, climbing above and working round on the flanks of the positions, got higher than the enemy and badly harassed the barbarians with showers of javelins and hand-thrown stones, while the slings did the greatest damage by striking from a distance.

And when they had forced back the first group and occupied their position, the workmen had an opportunity to clear away and smooth everything underfoot in safety. This was done quickly because of the number of hands.

In this way the slingers, archers, and javelin-men advanced over the heights in scattered order, sometimes gathering together and seizing the advantageous points, while the shield-bearers kept reserve and marched in formation and at a steady pace beside the ravine itself. The barbarians did not stand their ground, but all abandoned their positions and gathered on the summit.

## Book 10 Chapter 31: Antiochus Reaches Tambrax

In this way Antiochus and his men got safely through the difficult ground, though slowly and with effort, for they only reached the heights of Labus on the eighth day.

The barbarians had assembled there and were convinced they would stop the enemy from crossing, so a fierce struggle took place. The barbarians were driven out for the following reasons.

While massed together they fought boldly face to face against the phalanx; but during the night the light-armed troops had already made a wide circuit and occupied the higher ground and positions on their rear, and as soon as the barbarians saw what had happened they were at once terrified and turned to flight.

The king, however, with great urgency checked any prolonged pursuit by sounding recall with the trumpets, because he wished to descend into Hyrcania in a compact and orderly mass.

Having arranged the march as he wished and coming to Tambrax, an unwalled city that nevertheless possessed a royal palace and considerable size, he encamped there.

Most of the people, having withdrawn from the battle and from the surrounding countryside into the city called Syrinx—which lay not far from Tambrax and served as something like the capital of Hyrcania because of its strength and general convenience—

he determined to take this place by force. So he gathered the army, advanced, and after encamping around it began the siege.

The main part of the attack lay in the use of covered sheds for filling operations. There were three ditches, each no less than thirty cubits broad and fifteen deep; on the edge of each stood double palisades, and finally a strong outer wall in front.

So there were continual clashes around the works, in which neither side could carry off its dead and wounded, because the fighting took place not only above ground but also underground in the mines, hand to hand.

Yet through the sheer numbers employed and the king's own energy, it soon came about that the ditches were filled and the wall collapsed through the mining.

When this happened, the barbarians, completely disheartened, slaughtered the Greeks inside the city, plundered the most valuable possessions, and withdrew by night.

The king, seeing this, sent Hyperbasas with the mercenaries; when he met them, the barbarians threw away their booty and fled back into the city.

But when the peltasts pressed vigorously through the breach, they despaired and surrendered themselves.

## Book 10 Chapter 32: Fall of M. Claudius Marcellus

The consuls, wishing to reconnoiter clearly the parts of the hill sloping toward the enemy camp, ordered the men within the palisade to remain at their posts,

while they themselves took two troops of cavalry and about thirty light-armed men with the lictors and advanced to inspect the terrain.

Now some Numidians, accustomed to lie in ambush for skirmishers and in general for those moving out from the enemy camp, had by chance concealed themselves under the hill.

When their scout signaled that some men were coming over the crest above them, they rose and, moving along the flank, cut off the generals and shut them off from their own camp.

They struck down Claudius at the first clash, together with some others, and after wounding the rest forced them to flee in different directions down the rough ground.

The men in camp saw what was happening but were in no way able to come to the rescue of those in danger. While they were still shouting out and reeling from the shock of what had happened—some bridling their horses, others putting on armor—the affair was already over. Claudius' son, though wounded, only barely and unexpectedly escaped the danger.

Thus Marcus fell because he had behaved in a way more innocent than general-like in the circumstances I have described.

Throughout this work I am often compelled to remind my readers about such matters, because I see that, if commanders fail in any other part of the art of generalship, they especially fail in this one, though the ignorance involved is obvious.

For what use is a leader or general who has not grasped that, among partial dangers in which the whole issue is not at stake, the commander ought to keep as far away as possible?

And what use is one who does not know that, even when circumstances compel him to take part in some limited operation, many of those with him ought to die first before the danger comes near the men in command of the whole?

For, as the proverb says, the experiment ought to be made on a Carian, not on the general.

To say, "I would never have thought it," or, "Who could have expected this to happen?" seems to me the clearest sign of inexperience and slowness in generalship.

## Book 10 Chapter 33: Hannibal's Greatness

So although I judge Hannibal to have been a great commander in many ways,

one might especially note this point: after spending many years in enemy territory and passing through many varied situations, he repeatedly brought disaster on his opponents in minor operations by his own quick intelligence, yet he himself was never ruined despite directing so many and such great struggles.

It seems that he took such care over his own safety, and rightly so.

For if the man in command remains unharmed and alive, even if the whole undertaking suffers a reverse, fortune still offers many opportunities to recover from the loss caused by disaster.

But if he falls, like the helmsman of a ship, then even if fortune grants the mass of men victory over the enemy, no real advantage results, because all men's hopes hang upon their leaders.

Let this be said for those who, from vanity, boyish display,

or from inexperience or contempt, fall into such irrational errors.

For one or another of these causes always produces such disasters.

Then they suddenly let down the portcullis, which they had suspended a little above by means of machinery, trapped the men, and crucified them before the wall.

## Book 10 Chapter 34: The Submission of the Edetani to Scipio

In Iberia, Publius, the Roman commander, wintered at Tarraco, as I explained earlier, and first attached the Iberians to his friendship and loyalty by restoring the hostages to each of them.

In this he found an unexpected ally in Edeco, prince of the Edetani. As soon as the capture of Carthage had occurred and Publius had become master of his wife and children, Edeco at once reflected on the coming change among the Iberians and wished to take the lead in the same movement,

fully convinced that in this way he would recover his wife and children and would seem to have chosen the Roman side not under compulsion but by deliberate intention. And so it turned out.

For as soon as the armies had been dismissed into winter quarters, he came to Tarraco with his household and friends.

When he came into conference with Publius, he said that for this above all he gave the gods the greatest thanks: that he was the first of the rulers in the country to come to him.

The others, he said, were still sending envoys and looking toward the Carthaginians, while at the same time stretching out their hands to the Romans. He, however, had come to place not only himself but also his friends and kinsmen under Roman protection. Therefore, if he were judged worthy to be a friend and ally,

he would be of great use to him both immediately and in the future.

For as soon as the Iberians saw that he had been admitted to friendship and had obtained what he asked, all would come in the same way, eager to recover their relatives and gain the alliance of Rome.

And in the time to come, once won over by such honor and humane treatment, they would be eager and unhesitating partners in the rest of his enterprises.

Therefore he asked to recover his wife and children and, once recognized as a friend, to return home, so that with a reasonable occasion in hand he might show, to the full extent of his power, the goodwill of himself and his friends both toward Publius personally and toward the Roman cause.

Such, then, were Edeco's words.

## Book 10 Chapter 35: General Defection to the Romans

When he had finished speaking, Publius, who had long been ready for this very course and had reasoned much as Edeco had done, restored to him his wife and children and concluded the alliance of friendship.

Besides this, by his varied charm in conversation he won over the Iberian and held out great hopes for the future to all his followers, and so sent him back to his own country.

When this affair quickly became famous, it happened that all those dwelling north of the Ebro who had not already been friends of Rome chose the Roman side as if by a single impulse.

So far, then, everything was proceeding well according to Publius' wishes.

After these men had gone, he dismissed the naval forces, seeing that nothing opposed him at sea, and selecting the most suitable men from the sailors, distributed them among the maniples. In this way he increased his infantry strength.

Andobales and Mandonius,

who were at that time the greatest dynasts in Iberia and were thought to be the truest friends of the Carthaginians, had long been secretly hostile and were watching for an opportunity ever since Hasdrubal and his party, pretending to distrust them, had demanded a large sum of money and their wives and daughters as hostages, as I described earlier.

Now, thinking that the moment was favorable, they gathered their own forces and in the night withdrew from the Carthaginian camp to certain strong positions capable of securing their safety.

When this happened, most of the other Iberians likewise abandoned Hasdrubal, having long been burdened by Carthaginian arrogance and now at last finding an opportunity to reveal their true intentions openly.

## Book 10 Chapter 36: Difficulty of Making Good Use of a Victory

This, indeed, has often happened before. For though, as I have frequently said, it is a great thing in action to succeed and gain the upper hand over one's enemies in an enterprise, it requires far greater experience and caution to make good use of successes.

So one would find many times more men who have won advantages than men who have used those advantages well. That is exactly what happened then with the Carthaginians.

For after defeating the Roman armies and killing both generals, Publius and Gnaeus, they supposed Iberia to be indisputably theirs and treated the peoples of the country with arrogance.

Accordingly, instead of allies and friends they made enemies of their subjects.

And they suffered this quite deservedly. For while they thought one sort of conduct necessary for gaining empire and another for preserving it, they failed to learn that those preserve superiority best who remain most faithful to the same principles by which they first won power.

Yet it is obvious and has already been seen in many cases that men gain advantages by doing good and by holding out fair hope to their neighbors,

but whenever, after getting what they wanted, they begin to do wrong and rule despotically over those under them, it naturally follows that as the rulers change, the dispositions of the subjects change with them. That is what happened then to the Carthaginians.

## Book 10 Chapter 37: Hasdrubal Comes to a Decision

Hasdrubal, then, in such difficult circumstances turned over many varied thoughts about the dangers pressing upon him.

He was distressed by the revolt of Andobales, distressed also by the rivalry and estrangement existing between himself and the other generals, and he was anxious too about the presence of Publius.

Expecting that Publius would now shortly arrive with his forces, and seeing himself abandoned by the Iberians while all were flocking eagerly to the Romans, he came to the following conclusions.

He decided that he must prepare as strong a force as possible and fight the enemy in battle; if fortune granted him victory, he could then safely deliberate about the next step.

But if the battle turned out against him, he would withdraw with those saved from it into Gaul, and there take up as many barbarians as possible and go to Italy, sharing the same hopes as his brother Hannibal.

While Hasdrubal was engaged in these plans, Publius, after receiving Gaius Laelius and hearing the Senate's instructions he brought, led out the forces from winter quarters, the Iberians meeting him on the road and eagerly joining the expedition.

Andobales and his followers had long been sending messages to Publius, and now when he came near their region they left their camp and came to him with their friends. In conference they defended themselves concerning their former friendship with the Carthaginians, and likewise set forth the services and loyalty they had shown them.

After this they recounted the injustices and insults they had suffered at their hands.

Therefore they asked Publius himself to judge what they said, so that if it should appear they were making unjust accusations against the Carthaginians, he might know clearly that they would not be able to keep faith with the Romans either.

But if, forced by many wrongs, they had necessarily withdrawn their goodwill from the Carthaginians, he might hold fair hopes that, having now chosen the Roman side, they would keep their loyalty firmly toward Rome.

## Book 10 Chapter 38: Hasdrubal and Scipio Prepare to Fight

After they had said much more to this effect and then stopped speaking, Publius in reply said that he believed what they had told him, and that he knew well enough the insolence of the Carthaginians, both from their treatment of the other Iberians and especially from the outrage they had done to their wives and daughters.

These women he himself had found living not as hostages but as captives and slaves, and yet had so faithfully protected their honor that even their own fathers could scarcely have guarded them better.

When they acknowledged this and, bowing before him, all addressed him as king, those present approved the words; but Publius, embarrassed, urged them to take courage, for from the Romans, he said, they would receive every kind of humane treatment.

He at once restored the daughters with his own hand, and on the next day concluded the agreement with them.

The substance of the terms was that they should follow the Roman commanders and obey their orders.

Once this was settled, they withdrew to their own camps, took up their forces, came to Publius, and, encamping together with the Romans, advanced against Hasdrubal.

The Carthaginian general happened to be staying in the region around Castulo near the town of Baecula, not far from the silver mines.

When he learned of the Romans' arrival, he shifted camp and chose a position with a river secure in his rear, and in front of the camp a level place with a projecting brow, with depth enough for safety and breadth enough for deployment. There he stayed on the lower ground, always posting reserves along the brow.

When Publius came near, he was eager to risk battle, but was at a loss because he saw how favorable the ground was for the enemy's security.

Even so, after waiting two days and fearing that if Mago and Hasdrubal son of Gisco should also arrive the enemy might surround him from every side, he decided to hazard the attempt and test the enemy.

## Book 10 Chapter 39: Scipio Attacks Hasdrubal

He made the rest of his army ready for battle and kept it inside the camp, but sent out the light-armed troops and picked infantry with orders to attack the brow of the hill and test the enemy reserves.

When they carried out the command boldly, at first the Carthaginian general merely watched what was happening; but when he saw that, because of the Romans' audacity, his own men were under pressure and suffering badly, he brought out his force and stationed it along the brow, trusting in the ground.

At that moment Publius sent all the light-armed troops to support those already engaged, while holding the rest in readiness. Taking half himself, he went round the brow to the enemy's left and attacked the Carthaginians,

while the other half he gave to Laelius with similar orders to fall upon the enemy's right.

As this was happening, Hasdrubal was still in the act of leading his troops out from the camp. Up to that time he had been waiting because he trusted the position and was convinced the enemy would never dare attempt an assault; so, because the attack came unexpectedly, he was late in making his deployment.

The Romans, attacking on the wings before the enemy had yet occupied those positions, not only reached the brow in safety, but while the Carthaginians were still moving into place and trying to form up, they pressed on and killed some on the flank as they came up, while others still maneuvering they forced to turn and flee.

Hasdrubal, accordingly, reverting to the plan he had formed from the start and seeing his forces wavering and thrown into confusion, rejected the thought of staking everything on a last desperate struggle.

Taking the money and the elephants, and drawing together as many fugitives as he could, he retreated by way of the Tagus toward the passes of the Pyrenees and the Galatians living there.

Publius did not think it advantageous to pursue Hasdrubal at once, because he feared an attack by the other commanders; instead, he let his soldiers plunder the enemy camp.

## Book 10 Chapter 40: Scipio Refuses the Title "King"

On the next day he gathered together the prisoners, of whom there were about ten thousand infantry and more than two thousand cavalry, and busied himself with arranging their disposition.

And all those Iberians who in the districts just mentioned had been fighting then on the Carthaginian side came and placed themselves under Roman protection; and in their interviews they addressed Publius as king.

The first to do this and to bow before him had been Edeco, and after him those with Andobales. At that time, however, the word had passed him by without special notice.

But after the battle, when all alike kept calling him king, the repeated title forced the matter upon Publius' attention.

So he gathered the Iberians and said that he did indeed wish to be and to be called kingly by all men in character and in truth, but that he neither wished to be a king nor to be called one by anyone. Having said this, he ordered them to address him as general.

Even at that moment one might perhaps justly have marked the greatness of the man's spirit: though he was very young and fortune had run so strongly with him that all those coming under his rule were led by themselves to this judgment and this title, nevertheless he kept his self-command and pushed away such an impulse and such outward show.

But one would admire the extraordinary greatness of soul in this man much more if one considered the closing period of his life, when in addition to his deeds in Iberia he overthrew the Carthaginians, brought under his country's control the greatest and finest regions of Libya from the Altars of Philaenus to the Pillars of Heracles, subdued Asia and the kings of Syria, and made the best and greatest part of the inhabited world subject to Rome, while at the same time he had opportunities to acquire royal dominion in whatever regions of the world he might choose.

For such achievements would have made not only a human being but, if one may say so, something more divine grow proud.

Yet Publius surpassed other men so greatly in greatness of soul that what no man would dare ask the gods for as a greater good—I mean kingship—that very thing, though often offered him by fortune, he rejected; and he valued his country and his loyalty to it more highly than that conspicuous and enviable royalty.

At that time, however, after selecting from the captives the Iberians, he released them all without ransom to their own homes, and ordering Andobales and his people to choose three hundred of the horses, he distributed the rest among those who had none.

Afterward, taking over the Carthaginian camp because the site was so favorable, he remained there himself watching for the movements of the remaining Carthaginian generals, while he sent men to the passes of the Pyrenees to keep watch on Hasdrubal.

Later, as the season was already drawing to its close, he withdrew with the army to Tarraco to winter in that region.

## Book 10 Chapter 41: Affairs in Greece: Philip V. Called In Against the Aetolians

The Aetolians, recently elated by the arrival of the Romans and King Attalus, were alarming everyone and pressing hard against all by land, while Attalus and Publius did the same by sea.

Accordingly the Achaeans came urging Philip to help, for they were anxious not only about the Aetolians but also about Machanidas, because he was encamped with his force on the borders of Argos.

The Boeotians too, fearing the enemy fleet, asked for a commander and assistance. Those inhabiting Euboea were especially insistent that some precaution be taken against the enemy. The Acarnanians made similar appeals.

There was also an embassy from the Epirotes. At the same time it was reported that Scerdilaidas and Pleuratus were taking the field, and further that the Thracians bordering Macedonia, especially the Maedi, were preparing to invade Macedonia if the king should be drawn even a little way from his own kingdom.

The Aetolians were also preoccupying the narrows around Thermopylae with ditches, palisades, and heavy guards, convinced that they would shut Philip in and altogether prevent him from coming to the aid of the allies inside the pass.

It seems to me quite reasonable to call attention to such situations and fix the reader's notice upon them, since in them a true test and proof is made of the emotional resolve and bodily powers of commanders.

For just as in hunting the strength and courage of animals become most visible when danger closes round them from every side, so too with leaders. And what Philip did on this occasion made this especially clear.

For he dismissed all the embassies, promising each to do what he could, and fixed his attention on the war pressing him from every side, watching where and against whom he would need to make his first move.

## Book 10 Chapter 42: Reinforcements Sent to Various Cities

At this time word reached him that Attalus and his men had crossed over and, after putting in at Peparethus, had occupied the island. So he sent forces there to guard the city.

To Phocis and the regions of Boeotia he dispatched Polyphantas with a suitable force, and to Chalcis and the rest of Euboea Menippus with a thousand peltasts and five hundred Agrianians.

He himself advanced on a march to Scotussa, and likewise ordered the Macedonians to meet him in that city.

When he learned that Attalus and his men had sailed into Nicaea, and that the Aetolian leaders were gathering at Heraclea to confer with one another about the present situation, he took up the army from Scotussa and set out in haste, eager to appear there and throw their meeting into panic and confusion.

He arrived too late for the gathering, but after destroying and carrying off the grain of the inhabitants around the Maliac Gulf, he returned.

He left the main army again in Scotussa, but himself, taking the light-armed troops and the royal squadron, went to Demetrias and stayed there, watching the enemy's designs.

And so that nothing they did might escape him, he sent messages to Peparethus and to those in Phocis, and likewise to those in Euboea, ordering them to report all events to him by beacon-fires directed toward Mount Tisaeum.

This is a mountain in Thessaly, well situated for viewing the regions just mentioned.

## Book 10 Chapter 43: Fire signals

Since the use of beacon-signals is of the greatest service in war, and since the subject had previously remained undeveloped, I think it useful not to pass it by but to make suitable mention of it.

That timely action plays a great part in all undertakings, and the greatest in war, is clear to everyone. Among all the devices that contribute to this, beacon-fires have the greatest power.

For by them one may learn of events that have just happened or are actually taking place, if one takes heed, at a distance of three or four days' journey, and sometimes even more.

So it often happens that help arrives unexpectedly where it is needed because the news has been conveyed by fire-signals.

Formerly, however, since the method of beacon-signalling was in most cases simple, it turned out to be useless to those employing it.

For the system had to operate by fixed conventions, while most events are indefinite and varied, so that most of them escaped the use of beacons—as in the cases just mentioned.

That a fleet had arrived at Oreus or Peparethus or Chalcis could indeed be made clear to those who had agreed on such a signal.

But if certain citizens are changing sides, or betraying the city, or a massacre has occurred,

or any other such thing—events which often happen, but for which it is impossible to make prior provision, though such unexpected developments especially demand prompt deliberation and aid—

all such matters escaped the use of fire-signals. For where no prior foresight is possible, no agreed sign for the event can be devised either.

## Book 10 Chapter 44: Methods of Signalling

Aeneas, then, the man who composed the manuals on tactics, wishing to correct this difficulty, made some progress, but his device still fell very far short of what was required.

One may see this from the following. He says that those who wish to communicate urgent information to one another by beacon-fires should prepare earthenware vessels exactly equal in width and depth, the depth being best at three cubits and the width at one.

Then they should prepare corks a little narrower than the mouths of the vessels, and in the middle of these should fix rods divided into equal sections of three fingers' breadth, with each section clearly marked.

On each section should be written one of the most obvious and general events that occur in war, for example, immediately on the first,

"cavalry have entered the country," on the second "heavy infantry," on the third "light troops," and after these,

"infantry with cavalry," then "ships," after that "grain," and so on continuously, until in all the spaces there are written those events which according to reasonable foresight are most likely to occur in the war then on hand.

When this has been done, he directs that both vessels be pierced accurately so that the outlets are equal and discharge equally; then, after filling them with water, the corks with the rods are to be placed upon them, and the outlets are to be opened at the same moment.

If this happens, then clearly, since everything is equal and alike, the fluid will run off in equal measure, the corks will sink by the same amount, and the rods will disappear into the vessels in the same proportion.

And when by experiment this operation has proved equal in speed and in agreement, the two vessels are to be taken to the places where the parties will watch for the beacon-signals and each side is to set down its vessel.

Then whenever one of the items written on the rod occurs, he says a torch should be raised and held until the observers opposite raise one in answer. Once both torches are visible, the outlets are to be opened immediately.

Then, when the cork and the rod sinking with it bring the desired inscription level with the rim of the vessel, he directs that the torch should be raised.

The others are then to stop their outlet at once and look to see what words on the rod stand level with the rim of their own vessel.

That will be the message conveyed, provided everything on both sides has moved at the same speed.

## Book 10 Chapter 45: An Improved Method

This method, then, made some small improvement on signalling by prior convention, but it still remains indefinite.

For it is clear that one can neither foresee everything that may happen nor, even if foreseen, write it all upon the rod. So whenever unforeseen events arise out of circumstances, it is plain that they cannot be signaled by this device.

Nor are even the things written on the rod themselves definite. For it cannot make clear how many cavalry or infantry have arrived, in what part of the country, how many ships there are, or how much grain.

For matters that cannot be known before they happen cannot have fixed conventions laid down for them in advance.

And this is the essential point: how can anyone deliberate about sending help if he does not know how many enemies have arrived or where? Or how can he either take courage or the reverse, or form any plan at all, if he does not understand how many ships or how much grain have come from allies?

But the latest method, devised by Cleoxenus and Democleitus and worked out further by me, is completely definite and able to indicate every urgent circumstance precisely, though in operation it requires careful attention and very exact observation.

It is as follows. Take the alphabet in order and divide it into five groups of five letters each; the last group will be short by one letter, but that does not harm the practical use.

Then those who are to exchange signals with one another should each prepare five tablets and write one group of letters on each tablet in order.

After this they should agree that the man who is about to signal will first raise two torches together and hold them until the other raises two in answer.

This is so that by these beacons they may acknowledge to one another that they are paying attention.

When those are lowered, the signaller next raises torches on the left to show which tablet must be consulted—for instance, one if the first tablet is meant, two if the second, and so on in order.

Then he raises torches on the right according to the same principle to show which letter on that tablet the receiver must write down.

## Book 10 Chapter 46: Improvements In Signalling

Once these arrangements have been agreed on and the parties have gone apart, each side at its station should first have a sighting device with two tubes, so that the observer receiving the returning signal may see through one the right-hand position and through the other the left.

Next to the sighting device the tablets should be fixed upright in order.

And both the right and left positions should be screened off for ten feet, with a depth about the height of a man, so that when torches are raised beside them the appearance is exact, and when lowered they are hidden from view.

When these preparations have been made on both sides, if one wishes, for example, to indicate that "about a hundred soldiers have deserted to the enemy," one must first choose words that express the same thing in the fewest letters,

for instance, instead of the phrase just given, "A hundred Cretans have deserted from us." In this way the letters are fewer by more than half, while the same message is made clear.

Once this has been written on a tablet, it will be conveyed by the torches as follows. The first letter is kappa.

Now this belongs to the second group and the second tablet. So one must raise two torches on the left, so that the receiver knows he must look at the second tablet.

Then one raises five torches on the right, indicating kappa, since it is the fifth letter in the second group, and the receiver must write that down on his tablet.

Then four torches on the left, since rho is in the fourth group; then again two on the right, since it is the second letter in the fourth group. Thus the receiver writes rho, and the rest in the same manner.

By this device, then, every occurrence is indicated in a precise way.

## Book 10 Chapter 47: Importance of Practice

The number of torches used is large because for each letter two sets of signals must be made.

Even so, if one prepares properly for the task, the necessary result can be achieved.

And in either system the operators must practice beforehand, so that when the need arises they can communicate clearly to one another without mistake.

How great the difference is between first attempts and actions performed from habit can easily be learned from many examples.

For many things that at first seem not only difficult but impossible are later, through time and habit, carried out more easily than anything else.

There are many examples that could confirm this point, but the clearest is what happens in reading.

If one were to place beside each other a clever man who knew no letters and had no practice in reading, and a child who had acquired the habit, and then give the child a book and tell him to read what is written,

it is clear that the untrained man would scarcely believe that the reader must first fix his eyes upon the appearance of each letter, second upon its sound-value, and third upon the combinations of letters with one another, each of which takes a certain amount of time.

So when he sees the child, without pause, stringing together five or seven lines in a breath, he will not readily believe that the child has not read the book before.

And if he can also preserve the delivery, the divisions, and even the rough and smooth breathings, he will believe it still less.

Therefore one should not give up any useful art because of its apparent difficulties, but should acquire the habit by which all fine achievements become attainable to men, especially in matters of this sort, on which safety itself often depends.

This, then, is what I have gone on to say in accordance with my original promise. For I said that in our own time all the arts have advanced so far that most branches of knowledge have in a way become methodical sciences.

And that is why properly organized history is supremely useful in this matter too.

## Book 10 Chapter 48: Antiochus Crosses the Arius

The Apasiacae live between the Oxus and the Tanais. Of these rivers, the one flows into the Hyrcanian Sea, while the Tanais empties into the Maeotic Lake, and each is navigable because of its size.

So it seems surprising how the Nomads cross the Oxus and come into Hyrcania on foot with their horses.

Two accounts are given of this matter, one plausible and the other remarkable, though not impossible.

The Oxus has its sources in the Caucasus and, after being greatly swollen in Bactria by the waters flowing into it, moves through flat country with a great and muddy stream.

When it reaches the desert, it drives its current violently against certain projecting rocks, and because of the mass of water and the descent from the higher ground, it leaps so far from the rock below that its fall is more than a stade away at the lower level.

It is through this place, they say, that the Apasiacae pass on foot with their horses into Hyrcania, keeping close to the rock beneath the river's fall.

The other account offers a more plausible explanation. Since the ground below contains broad hollows into which the river plunges, they say that the force of the current scoops out and breaks through them downward and then carries the stream for a short distance underground, after which it appears again.

And the barbarians, because they know the place, cross the dry interval on horseback into Hyrcania.

## Book 10 Chapter 49: Antiochus Engages the Bactrians

When news came that Euthydemus and his army were in Tapuria, and that ten thousand cavalry were posted in advance guarding the crossings of the river Arius, Antiochus decided to abandon the siege and attack these forces.

Since the river was three days' march away, he made a moderate march for two days; on the third, after the others had dined, he ordered them to break camp at daybreak, while he himself took the cavalry and light-armed troops,

together with ten thousand peltasts, and advanced by night at a vigorous pace.

For he had learned that the enemy cavalry kept guard by day along the riverbank, but at night withdrew to a city not less than twenty stades away.

Having covered the remaining distance by night,

and since the plains were suitable for cavalry movement, he succeeded in getting most of the force with him across the river by daybreak.

The cavalry of the Bactrians, once their scouts had signaled what had happened, came out to help and met their opponents while still on the march.

The king, seeing that he must receive the enemy's first onset, summoned the two thousand cavalrymen around him who were accustomed to face danger with him, and ordered all the rest to form by standards and squadrons and take up their usual positions.

Then he himself, with the cavalry just mentioned, rode out to meet and engage the first Bactrian charge.

Antiochus seems on this occasion to have fought with the most distinguished gallantry of anyone with him.

Many on both sides were killed. The king's men overcame the first squadron, but when the second and third came on, they were hard pressed and suffered badly.

At that moment, when most of the cavalry had by then drawn up, Panaetolus ordered a forward movement, received the king and those fighting with him, and forced the Bactrians, who were charging on in disorder, to turn and flee in confusion.

Pressed by Panaetolus and his men, they did not stop until they rejoined Euthydemus, having lost most of their number.

The king's cavalry,

after killing many and taking many alive, withdrew and bivouacked that night by the river.

In this engagement the king's horse was killed after being wounded, while Antiochus himself was struck in the mouth and lost some teeth, and above all won a high reputation for courage on that day.

After this battle Euthydemus, in alarm, withdrew with his army to the city of Zariaspa in Bactria.
