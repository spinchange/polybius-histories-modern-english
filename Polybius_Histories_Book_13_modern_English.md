# Polybius — The Histories, Book XIII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 13 Chapter 1: The Aetolians

I. Greek Affairs

Because of the unbroken succession of wars and the extravagance of their way of life, the Aetolians had slipped into debt without realizing it themselves, much less anyone else.

So, being naturally inclined toward a change in their own constitution, they chose Dorimachus and Scopas to draft laws for them,

thinking that these men were, by temperament, eager for upheaval and were also deeply entangled in many ordinary financial obligations. After receiving this authority, they wrote the laws.

## Book 13 Chapter 1a

Alexander the Aetolian opposed the legislation being drafted by Dorimachus and Scopas, showing by many examples that wherever this growth had once taken root, it never stopped until it had brought great disasters on those who had made use of it.

Therefore he urged them to look not only at the present—whether they would get relief from their current obligations—
but also at the future.

For, he said, it was absurd that men should be ready in war to give up even their breath for the safety of their children, yet when they were deliberating, take no thought at all for the time that would come afterward.

## Book 13 Chapter 2: Scopas Goes to Egypt

Scopas, the Aetolian general, having failed to win the office for the sake of which he had dared to draft those laws, turned expectantly toward Alexandria, persuaded that the prospects there would restore what was lacking in his livelihood and satisfy the greedy craving in his soul. He did not understand that,

just as in the case of people suffering from dropsy, supplying moisture from outside never brings an end or satiety to their thirst unless someone first heals the diseased condition within the body itself, so too it is impossible to satisfy the desire for more unless one first corrects, by reason, the vice rooted in the soul. This became especially clear in the case of the very man now under discussion.

For when he arrived in Alexandria, besides the profits from the countryside over which he had authority because he was entrusted with major affairs, the king also assigned him a daily allowance of ten minas, and to those ranked just below him in command, one mina a day.

Yet even this was not enough for him, since he had long trained himself to press for more and more,

until, because of his greed, he became hated even by those who were giving him these things, and at last lost his life together with his gold.

## Book 13 Chapter 3: Philip's Treacherous Conduct, B. C. 204

This was the kind of villainy that followed—a thing no one could call kingly, though some nowadays choose to describe it as necessary to practical statesmanship because of the bad faith now so widely prevalent.

The men of old were very far from behaving in such a way. They were so alien to plotting against friends for the sake of increasing their own power that they did not even choose to conquer enemies by deceit,

because they believed that no success was either glorious or secure unless a man, fighting openly, had defeated his opponents by breaking their spirit.

Therefore they even came to agreements with one another not to use hidden missiles or long-range weapons against each other, holding that the only true decision in war was a battle fought hand to hand at close quarters.

For the same reason they used to announce wars and even battles beforehand, naming both the time when they intended to hazard all and the places where they were going to draw up their armies.

But now people say that any general who carries out military business openly is a poor one.

Among the Romans there still remains a faint trace of the ancient view in matters of war: they proclaim their wars in advance, make only sparing use of ambushes, and fight hand to hand at close quarters.

Let this be said against the excessive zeal now fashionable among leaders, in both political and military administration, for underhanded methods beyond what is necessary.

## Book 13 Chapter 4: Philip Intrigues Against Rhodes

Philip gave Heracleides, as though setting him a problem, instructions to consider how he might injure and destroy the Rhodian fleet,

and at the same time sent envoys to Crete to stir up and incite war against the Rhodians.

Heracleides, a man naturally fitted for wickedness, regarded the order as a godsend; and after devising whatever schemes he could in his own mind, he set out after a time and sailed to Rhodes.

This Heracleides was by birth a Tarentine, descended from mechanics and craftsmen, but he possessed exceptional gifts for shamelessness and unscrupulous dealing.

For first of all, in his early youth, he had openly prostituted his body; later he proved sharp-witted and possessed of a strong memory, overawing his inferiors with extreme boldness and audacity, while toward his superiors he was exceedingly fawning.

At first he was driven from his native city on suspicion of trying to betray Tarentum to the Romans. He had no political power, but as an architect, and through certain repairs to the walls, he had gained control of the keys of the gate leading inland.

After fleeing to the Romans, and then again carrying on correspondence and sending messages from there to Tarentum and Hannibal, he was detected; foreseeing what would follow, he fled once more to Philip.

There he gained such credit and influence that he became one of the chief causes of the ruin of so great a kingdom.

## Book 13 Chapter 5: Distrust between Philip and the Rhodians

The prytaneis, already distrustful of Philip because of his misconduct in Cretan affairs, also suspected that Heracleides was an agent planted among them.

But when he came before them, he explained the reasons
for which he had fled from Philip.

For Philip wished above all either to manage the whole business himself or at least to keep his intentions in these matters from becoming apparent to the Rhodians. And by this means he cleared Heracleides of suspicion.

It seems to me that nature has shown truth to be the mightiest of divinities for mankind and has added to it the greatest power.

For although everyone may struggle against it, and although every appearance may at times be arrayed on the side of falsehood, still somehow truth makes its way by its own force into human minds,

and sometimes reveals its power at once, while at other times, though long obscured, it finally prevails by its own strength and overcomes falsehood—as happened in the case of Heracleides, who had come to Rhodes from King Philip.

Damocles, who was sent with Pythion as a spy to the Romans, was a capable instrument by nature and had many resources for the management of affairs.

## Book 13 Chapter 6: Nabis, Tyrant of Sparta, B.C. 207-192

Nabis, the tyrant of the Lacedaemonians, now in the third year of his rule, did not yet venture to undertake or attempt anything on a grand scale, because the defeat of Machanidas by the Achaeans was still recent,

but he was laying the groundwork and setting the foundation for a tyranny that would be long-lasting and oppressive.

He destroyed outright what remained in Sparta, drove into exile those who stood out most in wealth or ancestral distinction, and distributed their property and wives among the most prominent of the others and among his mercenaries.

These men were murderers, brigands, robbers, and burglars. In general, this was the class of people he carefully gathered to himself from the whole inhabited world—men for whom the land that had reared them was closed because of impiety and lawlessness.

By setting himself up as the patron and king of such men, and by using them as his spear-bearers and bodyguards, he plainly ensured that his rule and his reputation for impiety would endure for a long time.

Nor, apart from what has already been mentioned, was he satisfied with merely banishing citizens; there was no place safe for the exiles, no refuge they could rely on.

Some he had murdered by sending men after them on the roads; others he killed by dragging them back from the places to which they had fled.

In the end, in whatever cities one of the exiles happened to be living, he secretly rented the neighboring houses through men unlikely to arouse suspicion, and sent Cretans into them; these made openings in the walls and, shooting through existing windows,

destroyed some of the exiles while they were standing and others while they were lying in their own houses, so that there was no place of escape and no moment of safety for the wretched Lacedaemonians.

## Book 13 Chapter 7: Nabis's Wife

By this method he did away with most of them. He also had a certain device constructed—if indeed one ought to call it a device.

It was a female figure clothed in expensive garments and fashioned with extraordinary accuracy to resemble the wife of Nabis.

Whenever he summoned one of the leading citizens because he wanted to extract money from him, he began with lengthy and courteous speeches,

pointing to the danger hanging over the countryside and the city from the Achaeans, explaining the number of mercenaries maintained for their protection, and adding the expenses owed to the gods and to the public needs of the city.

If by such arguments they gave way, that was enough for his purpose.

But if any of them refused and tried to put off his demand, he would make a speech like this: “Perhaps I am not able to persuade you, but I think this Apéga here will persuade you.” Apéga was the name of Nabis’s wife.

When he had said this, the figure I mentioned a little earlier was brought in.

After greeting the man, the figure, once he had raised the woman from her seat and embraced her with his arms, would gradually draw him toward her breasts.

Its forearms and hands were full of iron spikes beneath the garments, and so were its breasts.

When Nabis pressed the hands against the back of the figure and then, by means of mechanical devices, tightened the embrace and little by little forced the victim against the breasts, he compelled the man under torture to utter anything at all.

In this way he destroyed many who refused his demands.

## Book 13 Chapter 8: Nabis Finds a Pretext for War

In all his other actions as ruler he was of the same sort and character as in these beginnings.

He joined with the Cretans in piracy at sea, and throughout the whole Peloponnese he maintained temple-robbers, highwaymen, and murderers; taking a share of the profits from their crimes, he offered Sparta to them as a base and refuge.

Now at this time some visitors from Boeotia who were staying in Lacedaemon persuaded one of Nabis’s grooms to leave with them, taking a white horse that was thought to be the finest in the tyrant’s stables.

When the man agreed and did as they asked, Nabis’s agents pursued them as far as Megalopolis and, finding them there, at once led away the horse and the groom—no one interfering—but after that they laid hands on the foreign visitors as well.

At first the Boeotians demanded to be taken before the magistrates; but when no one paid attention, one of the visitors shouted,

“Help!” The local people came running up and protested that the men should be brought before the authorities, so Nabis’s agents were forced to let the men go and depart.

Nabis, who had long been looking for grounds of accusation and a plausible pretext for conflict, seized on this occasion at once and raided the livestock of Proagoras and certain others. From this came the beginning of the war.

## Book 13 Chapter 9: Antiochus in Arabia, B.C. 205-204

II. Asian Affairs

Labae, like Sabae, is a city of Chattenia, as Polybius says in his thirteenth book. The ethnic is Labaean, just as from Sabae comes Sabaean. Both belong to the same region, for Chattenia is territory of the Gerrhaeans.

Chattenia is the third district of the Gerrhaeans.

Polybius, Book 13: “Chattenia is otherwise a bleak land, but because of the prosperity of the Gerrhaeans it has been furnished with villages and towers; for they are the ones who occupy it.”

It lies on the Erythraean Sea. The inhabitants are called Chattenians, as he himself says: “For to these men he gave orders to spare the land of the Chattenians.”

The Gerrhaeans asked the king not to destroy what the gods had given them—that is, perpetual peace and freedom. When their letter had been interpreted to him, he said that he granted what they requested.

When freedom had been confirmed for the Gerrhaeans, they immediately honored King Antiochus with gifts of five hundred talents of silver, a thousand of frankincense, and two hundred of the substance called stacte. He then sailed on to the island of Tylos and from there departed for Seleuceia. These aromatic products came from the Erythraean Sea.
