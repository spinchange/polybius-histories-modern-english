# Polybius — The Histories, Book IX

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 9 Chapter 1: Extract from the Preface

These, then, are the most notable actions included in the Olympiad already mentioned, and in the four-year span which, as we say, must be counted as an Olympiad. I will try to explain them in two books.

I am not unaware that my work has something austere about it, and that because of the uniform character of its plan it is fitted for, and judged by, only one class of readers.

For almost all other historians, or at least most of them, draw many people to their books by making use of every branch of history.

Genealogical writing attracts the lover of such tales; accounts of colonies, city-foundations, and kinships among peoples attract the inquisitive and antiquarian reader, as one may see in Ephorus; political readers are drawn by narratives of the deeds of nations, cities, and dynasts.

I, however, have directed myself solely to this last kind of subject and have arranged the whole of my work around it. So, as I said before, I have made it especially suited to one kind of reader, but have also made the reading less entertaining for the majority.

Why I rejected the other parts of history and chose instead to write only about actions, I have explained more fully elsewhere; still, there is no harm in briefly reminding my readers of it here once again, simply to make the point clear.

## Book 9 Chapter 2: Why Focus on Actions

Since many writers, and in many different ways, have already treated genealogies, myths, colonies,

and also kinships and city-foundations, any man who now takes up those subjects must either present what belongs to others as if it were his own—which is the most disgraceful course of all—or else, if he refuses to do that, plainly waste his labor by working over matters that earlier writers have already explained well enough and handed down to posterity.

For these reasons, and for many others besides, I left such themes aside.

Pragmatic history, on the other hand, deserved approval first because events are always producing something new and therefore require a fresh account; one generation cannot simply report the actions that come after it.

Secondly, this kind of history is the most useful of all—and though it always was so, it is especially so in our own day, now that experience and the arts have advanced so far that eager students can handle whatever circumstances arise almost methodically, as if by rule.

Therefore I aimed not so much at giving pleasure to casual readers as at benefiting those who pay serious attention; and so, leaving the rest aside, I devoted myself to this branch of history.

Those who read my work with care from beginning to end will be the surest witnesses to the truth of what I say here.

## Book 9 Chapter 3: The Hannibalian War

Hannibal surrounded Appius's camp on every side and at first confined himself to skirmishing and testing the Romans, wanting to draw them out into battle.

But since no one responded, what followed came to resemble a siege. The cavalry charged in squadrons and, with loud shouting, hurled their javelins into the camp, while the infantry advanced in companies and tried to tear down the palisade.

Even so, he could not move the Romans from their fixed resolve. Their light-armed troops beat back the men attacking the rampart, while the heavily armed soldiers, sheltering themselves from the storm of missiles with their armor, stayed in formation by their standards.

Hannibal, dissatisfied with the whole situation because he could neither break into the city nor draw the Romans out, began considering what he ought to do next.

And in my opinion these events would have seemed puzzling not only to the Carthaginians, but to anyone else who heard of them.

For who would not have found it hard to believe that the Romans, after being defeated in many battles by the Carthaginians and not daring any longer to meet them in open combat, still could neither be forced to give way nor induced to abandon the open country?

Up to this time they had merely shadowed Hannibal, always keeping to the foothills; but now they had taken their stand on the plains and in the most conspicuous region of Italy were besieging its strongest city, even while the enemy pressed them on every side—an enemy whom they could not even bear to face in pitched battle.

Meanwhile the Carthaginians, though they were winning battles without interruption, at some moments were in difficulties no less than the defeated side.

It seems to me that the cause lay partly in the deliberate policy of both sides, since both had recognized that Hannibal's cavalry formation was the reason why the Carthaginians won and the Romans were beaten.

That is why, after the battles, the defeated Roman camps naturally followed at a distance: they moved through terrain where the enemy cavalry could do them no harm.

And for the same reason, what happened around Capua at that time followed naturally for both sides alike.

## Book 9 Chapter 4: The Siege of Capua

For the Roman army did not dare to come out and fight because it feared the enemy cavalry,

but it remained boldly inside its camp, clearly understanding that the cavalry by which it had been beaten in battle would there be powerless to hurt it.

The Carthaginians, on the other hand, quite naturally could not remain encamped for long with their cavalry, because the Romans had destroyed all the fodder in the neighboring country for this very purpose, and it was impossible for so many horsemen and so many pack animals to bring hay and barley on their backs from a great distance.

Yet they also did not dare, if camped without their cavalry, to assault enemies protected by ditch and palisade, since even apart from the cavalry the danger of an evenly matched engagement seemed uncertain enough.

Besides this, Hannibal was anxious that the incoming consuls might arrive, pitch camp against him, and put him in severe difficulty by cutting off his supplies.

Reasoning from all this, Hannibal concluded that it was impossible to lift the siege by direct force and adopted another plan.

He thought that if he made his march in secret and suddenly appeared in the region around Rome, he might perhaps achieve some useful result against the city itself by frightening the inhabitants with the unexpectedness of his arrival.

And if not that, he would at least force Appius either to raise the siege in haste to help his country, or to divide his forces—making both the relieving troops and those left behind easier to defeat.

## Book 9 Chapter 5: Hannibal Advances Upon Rome

With this in mind, Hannibal sent a letter-carrier into Capua. He persuaded one of the Libyans to desert to the Roman camp and from there make his way into the city, taking precautions to ensure that the letter would be delivered safely.

For he was very anxious that, if the Capuans saw him departing without understanding the plan, they might lose heart as though he had given them up and so surrender to the Romans.

So he wrote to explain the purpose behind his withdrawal and sent the Libyan off, in order that once the Capuans understood his intention and the reason for the separation, they might endure the siege with confidence.

Meanwhile, when news reached Rome from the region of Capua that Hannibal had pitched camp opposite their lines and was besieging their forces, everyone in the city was thrown into alarm and fear, since the crisis seemed likely to affect the whole war.

Accordingly all alike bent their efforts toward this one object, devoting themselves to dispatches, reinforcements, and military preparations.

The Capuans, however, after receiving the letter brought by the Libyan and learning the Carthaginian plan, held firmly to their former resolve, deciding to put this fresh hope to the test.

Then, on the fifth day after his arrival, Hannibal had his men take supper, left the campfires burning, and so conducted the withdrawal that none of the enemy noticed what was happening.

He marched through Samnium at speed and without interruption, constantly sending his advance troops ahead to scout the route and seize key positions.

Thus, while the minds of those in Rome were still fixed on Capua and the operations there, he crossed the Anio unnoticed, came close to the city, and encamped no more than forty stades from Rome.

## Book 9 Chapter 6: Rome Saved by Luck

When this happened and the news reached Rome, the people of the city were thrown into complete confusion and terror.

The whole affair was sudden and wholly unexpected, since Hannibal had never before advanced so near the city; and another thought also crept in among them—that the enemy could never have dared come so close and with such confidence unless the Roman armies around Capua had been destroyed.

So the men occupied the walls and the strong positions in front of the city, while the women went around the temples imploring the gods, brushing the floors of the sanctuaries with their hair.

For this is their custom whenever some overwhelming danger overtakes their country.

But just when Hannibal had encamped and was planning on the following day to test the city itself, an extraordinary and fortunate accident occurred that helped save Rome.

Gnaeus and Publius had already completed the enrollment for one army, and the soldiers had sworn to appear in arms at Rome on that very day; and for the other army the enrollment and examination were then in progress.

As a result, a large number of men happened to be assembled at Rome at exactly the right moment. The commanders boldly led them out, encamped in front of the city, and checked Hannibal's advance.

At first the Carthaginians pushed forward, not altogether despairing of taking Rome itself by storm. But when they saw the opposing force drawn up in battle order and quickly learned from a prisoner what had happened, they abandoned the attack on the city and instead marched through the countryside, ravaging the land and burning the houses.

At the beginning they gathered into their camp an immense quantity of plunder, as though they had come upon a hunting ground of exactly the kind no one had ever expected an enemy to enter.

## Book 9 Chapter 7: Hannibal In Sight of Rome

But afterward, when the consuls boldly ventured to encamp only ten stades away, Hannibal—having now amassed a great quantity of booty, having lost hope of any success against the city, and above all calculating that by this time Appius ought, according to the original plan, to have learned of Rome's danger and either to have raised the siege altogether and come to help, or at least to have left part of his force behind and hurried off with the greater part—broke camp at dawn.

He believed that whichever of these things happened would serve his purpose well, and so set his army in motion before daybreak.

Publius and his men, however, broke down the bridges over the river just mentioned and forced Hannibal to take his army across the stream itself.

As the Carthaginians were making the crossing they pressed hard upon them and caused them great trouble.

They could not accomplish anything decisive because of the number of Hannibal's cavalry and the readiness of the Numidians on every kind of ground; but after stripping away a fair part of the booty and killing about three hundred of the enemy, they then withdrew to their camp.

After that, thinking that the Carthaginians were retreating in haste out of fear, they followed in their rear along the foothills.

Hannibal at first kept up his speed, eager to carry out the plan before him; but on the fifth day, when word was brought that Appius and his army were still remaining at the siege, he halted, waited for the pursuers to come up, and while it was still night attacked their camp.

He killed many of them and drove the rest out of the camp.

When day came and he saw that the Romans had withdrawn to a strong hill, he gave up any thought of continuing the attack against them.

Instead he marched through Daunia and Bruttium and suddenly appeared in the region of Rhegium, coming so unexpectedly that he came within a little of taking the city itself, cut off all those who had gone out into the countryside, and in that visit made himself master of a large number of Rhegines.

## Book 9 Chapter 8: Epaminondas and Hannibal Compared

At this point, I think, one may fairly pause to note the courage and ambition both of the Carthaginians and of the Romans in war.

For just as everyone admires the Theban Epaminondas because, after coming to Tegea with the allies and observing that the Lacedaemonians themselves had all assembled at Mantinea, together with their allies, in order to fight the Thebans,

he ordered his own men to take supper early and led out the army as night was just coming on, pretending that he was hurrying to seize certain advantageous positions for the coming battle,

but after creating that impression among the rank and file actually marched straight toward Sparta itself,

and, arriving unexpectedly at about the third hour, found Sparta empty of defenders, forced his way as far as the marketplace, and occupied the parts of the city that slope toward the river,

yet then suffered a reversal because some deserter got through during the night to Mantinea and informed King Agesilaus what was happening, and help arrived just at the critical moment when the city was on the point of being taken,

so that he failed in that hope; but afterward, having had breakfast by the Eurotas and allowed his troops to recover from their exertions, he again set out by the same road in the opposite direction,

calculating that since the Lacedaemonians and their allies had gone off to help Sparta, Mantinea would in turn be left undefended—which is exactly what happened.

So he encouraged the Thebans, pushed on with a vigorous night march, and came up to Mantinea about midday, when it was completely abandoned by those who might have defended it.

But at this very moment the Athenians, eager under their alliance to take part with the Lacedaemonians in the struggle against the Thebans, arrived.

And just as the Theban vanguard was reaching the sanctuary of Poseidon, which lies seven stades in front of the city, it so happened that the Athenians appeared on the hill above Mantinea.

At the sight of them, the Mantineans who had been left behind only then found courage enough to mount the walls and block the Theban assault.

For this reason historians are justified in their criticism of those events, when they say that the commander did everything that a good general could do, and that Epaminondas was superior to his enemies but inferior to Fortune.

## Book 9 Chapter 9: When Audacity is the Truest Safety

The same remark may be made about Hannibal as well.

First, he attacked the enemy and tried by a series of partial engagements to raise the siege.

Then, after failing in that attempt, he rushed straight at Rome itself; and when that purpose also was frustrated by accidents beyond calculation, he turned back again, crushed the troops pursuing him, and held himself ready in case any movement should occur among those besieging Capua.

And finally, without abandoning his design, he flung himself against the enemy to injure them, and all but wiped out the people of Rhegium.

Who would not mark such a commander out for admiration?

And yet at this moment one might judge the Romans superior to the Lacedaemonians.

For the Lacedaemonians, thrown into confusion at the first message, saved Sparta indeed, but so far as they were concerned they lost Mantinea.

The Romans, however, both preserved their own country and did not raise the siege. Instead, they remained steadfast and immovable in their original resolve, and from then on pressed the Capuans with still greater confidence.

I do not say this so much for the sake of praising either Romans or Carthaginians—for I have already noted their qualities more than once—as for the sake of those who hold command among either people, and of all who in later times will have charge of public affairs.

By remembering such actions, or by having them set before their eyes, they may become eager to imitate them. For what seems rash and dangerous often turns out, on the contrary, to be real safety; and boldness of conception, if only it is guided by reason, remains admirable, noble, and worthy of remembrance whether it succeeds or fails.

Atella is a city of the Opici in Italy, lying between Capua and Neapolis. The ethnic name is Atellanus, as Polybius says in the ninth book: "The Atellani surrendered themselves."

When the Romans were besieging Tarentum, Bomilcar the Carthaginian admiral came to help with a very large force. But since he could do nothing to assist those inside, because the Romans had secured their camp and blockade so well, he consumed his supplies without accomplishing anything. Though he had first been compelled to come by urgent appeals and great promises, he was later forced by the entreaties of the people within the city to sail away.

## Book 9 Chapter 10: The Spoils of Syracuse: Works of Art Taken To Rome

A city is not adorned by things brought in from outside, but by the virtue of its inhabitants.

For this reason the Romans decided to transfer the objects just mentioned to their own country and leave nothing behind.

Whether they acted rightly and to their own advantage, or the reverse, would require a long discussion. But the stronger argument, I think, is that they did not act properly then, and are not acting properly now when they do such things.

If it had been by means of such objects that they had first advanced their own country, then it would plainly have made sense to bring home the things through which they had grown great.

But if, while living in the utmost simplicity and keeping themselves far removed from the extravagance and luxury connected with such things, they nevertheless conquered people who possessed them in greatest abundance and of finest quality, how are we not to regard what they did as a mistake?

For to abandon the customs of the conquerors and take up admiration for those of the conquered, while at the same time taking on the jealousy that always follows such things—and that is the most dangerous thing for those in positions of superiority—must surely be acknowledged to be an error.

A spectator never praises those who possess what belongs to others so purely that pity for the original losers does not at once mingle with envy.

And when power continues to grow, and one people gathers everything belonging to others into its own hands, and in a sense invites the dispossessed to come and look at it all, the evil becomes double.

For then the spectators no longer pity their neighbors, but themselves, as the sight recalls their own disasters.

From this there springs not only envy, but a kind of anger against the prosperous, because the memory of their private misfortunes acts like a spur driving men to hatred of those who caused them.

There may perhaps be some reasonable argument for bringing gold and silver together into one's own possession; for no one can aspire to universal power without at once weakening the resources of others and preparing that kind of strength for himself.

But the things that lie outside this kind of power ought to have been left in their original places. In that way they would both have avoided stirring up envy and would have made their own country more glorious, adorning it not with paintings and statues, but with dignity and greatness of soul.

Let me say this for the sake of those who continually inherit positions of power, so that when they plunder cities they may not suppose that other people's disasters are an ornament to their own fatherlands. The Romans, however, after transferring these objects, used them to decorate private life in individual households and to embellish the public spaces of the city.

## Book 9 Chapter 11: Spain

The Carthaginian commanders, though they had mastered their enemies, could not master themselves.

Thinking that they had already brought the war with Rome to an end, they fell into internal quarrels, continually led into conflict by the Phoenicians' inborn greed and love of power.

Among them Hasdrubal son of Gesco, abusing the authority he possessed, went so far in wickedness that he demanded a large sum of money from Andobales, the most loyal of their Iberian friends—a man who had once lost his rule because of the Carthaginians and had only recently recovered it because of his goodwill toward them.

And when Andobales refused, confident because of his long-standing loyalty to Carthage, Hasdrubal brought a false accusation against him and forced him to hand over his daughters as hostages.

## Book 9 Chapter 11a

The Romans sent envoys to Ptolemy because they wanted grain supplies, since there was a severe shortage among them.

All the countryside in Italy had been devastated by the armies as far as the very gates of Rome, and no help had come from outside, because wars were in progress and armies were encamped in every part of the inhabited world except the regions of Egypt.

So extreme had the scarcity at Rome become that a Sicilian medimnus was selling for fifteen drachmas.

And yet, though their situation was like this, they did not neglect the war.

## Book 9 Chapter 12: On the Art of Commanding Armies

Military enterprises are attended by many circumstances that require careful thought; yet in each case it is possible to hit upon the right course, if a man carries out his plan with intelligence.

That operations in war openly and forcibly executed are fewer than those accomplished by stratagem and by the timely use of opportunity is easy for anyone to learn from past events.

And that even among those operations that do depend on seizing the right moment, failures are more numerous than successes is also not hard to see from experience.

Nor would anyone doubt that most such failures come about through the ignorance or carelessness of commanders.

So we may now examine what method governs this sort of undertaking.

Events in war that occur unexpectedly ought not really to be called operations at all, but rather accidents and chance occurrences.

For that reason we may leave aside anything that cannot be treated methodically or reduced to a fixed principle. What should be explained is what is done according to deliberate design; and it is of this that the present discussion speaks.

Every action has its definite opportunity, duration, and place, and requires secrecy, fixed signals, and also clear knowledge of through whom, with whom, and in what manner it is to be carried out.

It is therefore plain that the man who gets each of these points right will not fail in his enterprise, whereas one who neglects even a single point will ruin the whole design.

So much has nature made even one small detail enough to bring an intended plan to failure...

## Book 9 Chapter 13: Scientific Strategy

Success demands that everything be adequately provided for; for that reason commanders must neglect nothing in undertakings of this kind.

The first principle of all is silence: a man must not, whether from delight at some unexpected hope, or from fear, or from familiarity, or from affection, share the plan with anyone outside the circle,

but only with those without whom the design cannot be brought to completion—and not even with them before the moment when the need for each person's part compels disclosure.

And one must keep silence not only with the tongue, but even more with the mind.

For many men, though they concealed their words, have betrayed their designs by their very expression, or even by the actions they took.

The second requirement is to know the capacities of marches by day and by night, and the distances that can be covered in each, not only on land but also at sea.

Third, and most important, one must understand the opportunities provided by the seasons and be able to adapt the plan rightly to them.

Nor should the place of action be treated as a small matter, since it often happens that because of the ground what seems impossible becomes possible, while what seemed possible turns out impossible.

Finally, one must not neglect signals and counter-signals, or the choice of the persons through whom and with whom the decided action is to be carried out.

## Book 9 Chapter 14: Generals Also Need to Know Science

Of the matters just mentioned, some are learned through practice, some from history, and some by systematic experience.

Best of all is for the commander himself to know the roads, the place to which he must come, the nature of the ground, and in addition the men through whom and with whom he is going to act.

Second best is to make careful inquiry and not trust random people; indeed, the reliability of guides in such matters should always be secured in some definite way.

These things, and others like them, perhaps can be acquired by commanders through military practice itself, some by direct experience and some by history.

But what comes from scientific knowledge requires instruction and principles, especially from astronomy and geometry. The actual labor of these sciences is not great for the purpose in view, yet their value is great and they can contribute greatly to the operations I have described.

Their most necessary use lies in reckoning night and day. If those periods were always equal, the matter would require no study and the knowledge would be common to all.

But since they differ not only from each other, but also vary in themselves, it is clearly necessary to know the increase and decrease of each.

For how can anyone correctly estimate a march and the distance possible by day or by night unless he has understood those differences?

And indeed it is impossible to arrive at the proper moment without such knowledge; one must otherwise either be late or arrive too early.

In these operations, arriving too early is an even greater mistake than being late.

For the man who passes beyond the appointed moment merely misses his opportunity, since he can learn from a distance what has happened and withdraw again safely.

But the man who comes too early, by approaching before the time and being noticed, not only fails in the enterprise but risks everything.

## Book 9 Chapter 15: Mode of Calculating Time

In all human actions the critical moment has power, but most of all in war.

Therefore a general ought to have ready at hand the summer and winter solstices, the equinoxes, and the intervals between them in which days and nights increase and diminish.

Only in this way can he properly measure the distances that can be covered, whether by land or by sea.

He must also know the divisions of time both by day and by night, so as to understand when reveille should be sounded and when a march should begin.

For no one can attain the end if he has failed to get the beginning right.

The times of day can be observed without difficulty from the shadow, from the sun's course, and from the distances it traverses across the sky.

Those of night are harder to judge unless one can follow, from what is visible, the arrangement and order of the twelve signs of the zodiac; yet even this is easy for those who have carefully studied the heavens.

For although nights are unequal in length, it nevertheless happens that in every night six of the twelve signs rise above the horizon. It is therefore clear that in corresponding parts of every night equal portions of the zodiac must rise.

And since the sun's position is known each day, it is clear that the sign lying opposite it must be the one rising.

What remains is that whatever part of the zodiac appears to have risen after that point indicates how much of the night has elapsed.

And once the signs of the zodiac are known both in number and in size, the particular divisions of the night also become known accordingly.

On cloudy nights one must pay attention to the moon, because by reason of its size its light is almost always visible wherever in the sky it may be.

Sometimes one must estimate from the times and places of its rising,

and sometimes again from those of its setting, provided one has enough understanding in this matter to keep pace with the daily changes in those risings.

There is an easy way to observe this as well, for one month marks out a single evident cycle, perceptible to the senses...

## Book 9 Chapter 16: Example: Ulysses

For this reason one may praise the poet for representing Odysseus, that most capable of leaders, as drawing inferences from the stars not only for navigation, but also for operations on land.

For unexpected events, which cannot be fully anticipated by exact foresight, are enough by themselves to produce great and repeated difficulties,

such as heavy rains, floods of rivers, severe frosts and snowfalls, thick and cloudy air, and all other things of that sort.

So if we also neglect matters that can in fact be foreseen, how can it fail to be our own fault when we miss success in most cases?

Therefore none of the things just mentioned should be neglected, if we do not want to fall into the kind of blunders into which many others are said to have fallen, as well as those men whom I am now about to mention by way of example.

## Book 9 Chapter 17: Failures Arising From Ignorance

When Aratus, the Achaean general, planned to seize the city of Cynaetha by treachery, he agreed with his collaborators inside the city on a day when Aratus was to come by night to the river flowing from Cynaetha and wait there quietly with his force.

The men inside, about midday when they got the chance, were to send one of their number out through the gate quietly, wrapped in a cloak, and tell him to stand before the city on the tomb that had been agreed upon as the signal.

The rest were to lay hands on the officials who, around midday, when they were asleep, were accustomed to guard the gate.

Once that was done, the Achaeans were to hurry out from their ambush and make straight for the gate.

When all had been arranged and the time came, Aratus arrived and remained hidden by the river, waiting for the signal.

But about the fifth hour a man who kept fine sheep near the city happened to need to ask a shepherd some ordinary question. He came out through the gate wearing a cloak and stood on the very same tomb, looking around for the shepherd.

Aratus and his men thought the agreed signal had been given and all rushed toward the city in haste.

But the gate was quickly shut by the guards, since the men inside had not yet made any preparations. So Aratus not only failed in the attempt, but brought the gravest misfortunes upon those within who were helping him: for once they were exposed, they were immediately seized and put to death.

What, then, was the cause of this disaster? It was that the general had made the signal too simple, being still young and inexperienced in the precision provided by double signals and counter-signals.

So narrow is the margin by which military operations incline toward success or failure.

## Book 9 Chapter 18: Cleomenes Tries to Take Megalopolis

Likewise Cleomenes the Spartan, intending to capture Megalopolis by stratagem, arranged with the men guarding the stretch of wall near the place called Pholeus that he would come by night with his force during the third watch; for at that hour his accomplices happened to be on guard at the wall.

But because he failed to take into account that around the rising of the Pleiades the nights are already very short, he set his army in motion from Sparta at sunset.

As a result he could not get there in time, and when daylight overtook him he made a rash and senseless attempt, failed disgracefully, lost many men, and put everything at risk.

Yet if he had struck the agreed time correctly and had brought in his troops while his supporters controlled the entrance, he would not have failed in the attempt.

Again, King Philip too, as I said earlier, made two mistakes in his intrigue against the city of the Melitaeans: he brought ladders shorter than the need required, and he also failed in timing.

For though he had arranged to arrive around midnight, when everyone would already be asleep, he started too early from Larisa and reached the territory of the Melitaeans before the proper moment.

He could neither remain where he was, for fear that his arrival would be reported to those inside, nor withdraw again and still escape notice.

So, compelled to continue forward, he reached the city while the people were still awake. Consequently he could neither force an entry by ladders, because they were out of proportion, nor get in through the gate, because the men inside could not cooperate at that hour.

In the end he merely stirred up the city, lost many of his own men, and returned in disgrace without accomplishing anything, having done nothing except warn everyone else to distrust him and stay on their guard.

## Book 9 Chapter 19: Example: Why Nicias Failed at Syracuse

Again Nicias, the Athenian general, was in a position to save the army at Syracuse. He had chosen the proper moment of the night to escape the notice of the enemy and withdraw to safety, but when an eclipse of the moon occurred he became superstitious, as though it portended some grave danger, and halted the departure.

Because of this, when he set the army in motion on the following night, the enemy had already sensed what was happening, and both the camp and the commanders fell into the hands of the Syracusans.

And yet, if he had only asked those who had experience in such matters, he could not only have avoided missing his proper opportunity for so foolish a reason, but could even have used the phenomenon to his own advantage because of the enemy's ignorance.

For the ignorance of one's opponents is a great advantage to those who understand.

So much, then, for the need to study astronomy up to the point already described. As for the proper proportion of ladders, the method of calculating it is as follows.

If the height of the wall is supplied by one of the collaborators inside, the proper size of the ladders is obvious: if the wall's height is, say, ten units, the ladders ought to be twelve of the same units, with some room to spare.

And the distance of the foot of the ladder from the wall should be half the ladder's length, so that the ladders may neither stand too far off and break easily under the weight of those climbing, nor lean too upright and become dangerously insecure for the men mounting them.

But if it is impossible either to measure the wall or even to get near it, then one must estimate from a distance the height of any object that stands upright at right angles to a level surface.

The method of making this estimate is both practicable and easy for those willing to study what mathematicians teach.

## Book 9 Chapter 20: Need of Some Knowledge of Mathematics

Therefore it is again clear that those who wish to succeed in military enterprises and operations must have studied geometry—not with full professional mastery, but enough to understand proportion and the theory of similarity.

For this knowledge is necessary not only in the cases already mentioned, but also in rearranging the shapes of camps, so that one may sometimes alter the whole form while preserving the same proportions among all the enclosed parts,

and at other times keep the same shapes while increasing or reducing the ground occupied by the camp, always in proportion to the numbers added to or removed from it.

I have explained these matters more exactly in my notes on tactical formations.

And I do not think anyone can reasonably blame me for adding many requirements to the art of generalship by insisting that those who aim at it must study astronomy and geometry.

For while I strongly reject what is superfluous in such disciplines—what is dragged in merely for display and empty talk—and likewise reject carrying them further than practical usefulness demands, I am very earnest and serious about what is necessary.

It would be absurd if those who aspire to dancing or flute-playing willingly accept preliminary training in rhythm and music,

and likewise the corresponding preparation required for athletics, because the final achievement in each field seems to need the help of such studies,

while those who claim to pursue generalship should feel annoyed if they must take up outside disciplines to some limited extent. That would mean that practitioners of vulgar crafts are more diligent and more ambitious than men who choose to distinguish themselves in the noblest and most dignified of pursuits.

No sensible person would admit such a thing. Let this be enough on these matters.

## Book 9 Chapter 21: The Computation of the Size of Cities

Given the state of feeling on both sides, among Romans and Carthaginians alike, and since the turns dealt by Fortune kept alternately reversing themselves for each, it was natural, as the poet says, that sorrow and joy should enter their hearts together.

## Book 9 Chapter 22: Estimate of Hannibal

In all that befell both sides—Romans and Carthaginians alike—there was one man and one mind responsible: I mean Hannibal.

For he was admittedly the one directing affairs in Italy, and in Spain he acted through his elder brother Hasdrubal, and afterward through Mago.

It was these men who killed both Roman commanders in Spain.

And in Sicily too he managed events, at first through Hippocrates and later through Myttonus the Libyan.

Likewise in Greece and Illyria: by magnifying the fear arising from those regions he alarmed and distracted the Romans through his cooperation with Philip.

So great and marvelous a force does one man become, and one soul properly fitted from the beginning to whatever human undertaking it turns itself.

But since the course of events has brought us to consider Hannibal's nature, the occasion seems to require that I should explain the qualities in him that have been most disputed.

For some think him excessively cruel, while others think him greedy for money. Yet to tell the truth about him—or about any man actively engaged in public affairs—is not easy.

Some say that men's natures are exposed by circumstances, and that some become manifest while holding power, even if they were previously reserved, while others are revealed in adversity.

I, however, do not think this saying sound. It seems to me that men are often—indeed for the most part—forced, sometimes by the influence of friends and sometimes by the complexity of events, to speak and act against their real intention.

## Book 9 Chapter 23: Examples of Actions Contrary to Principles

Anyone may recognize this if he pays attention to many events that have already happened.

Who has not heard that Agathocles, tyrant of Sicily, though he had the reputation of being extraordinarily savage in the first measures by which he established his power, later, once he believed his rule over the Siceliots securely fixed, appeared to become remarkably gentle and mild?

And again, was not Cleomenes the Spartan an excellent king, a most bitter tyrant, and then, as a private man, exceedingly gracious and humane?

Yet it is not likely that the most opposite dispositions truly existed in the same nature. Rather, some rulers, compelled by changes in circumstances to change with them, often display to the outside world a temper contrary to their real character; so that such behavior does not reveal their natures at all, but rather obscures them.

The same thing is accustomed to happen through the influence of friends, not only to commanders, dynasts, and kings, but to cities as well.

Thus one may find that the Athenians committed few harsh acts and many noble and honorable ones when Aristides and Pericles led the state, but the opposite when Cleon and Chares did so.

And when the Lacedaemonians held supremacy in Greece, everything done under King Cleombrotus had an allied and cooperative character, while what was done under Agesilaus had the opposite tendency.

So even the character of cities changes with the differences among their leaders.

King Philip too, when Taurion or Demetrius were advising him, was most impious, but when Aratus or Chrysogonus were with him, he was most gentle.

## Book 9 Chapter 24: Estimate of Hannibal

Something similar, it seems to me, happened in Hannibal's case as well.

For he found himself in extraordinary and shifting circumstances, and his closest friends differed from one another greatly; so that from his conduct in Italy it is very hard to discern his true character.

What influence circumstances had upon him can easily be learned both from what has already been said and from what is still to be said. But one should not pass over the influence of his friends, especially since a single example is enough to make the matter clear.

At the time when Hannibal was planning to march with his forces from Iberia into Italy, the difficulty of food and of preparing supplies for the army appeared immense, both because the route seemed impossibly long and because of the number and savagery of the barbarian peoples living between.

At that time, when this difficulty was being discussed again and again in council, one of his friends, called Hannibal Monomachus, is said to have declared that he saw one and only one way by which it would be possible to reach Italy.

When Hannibal told him to speak, he said they must teach the army to eat human flesh and accustom them to it.

Hannibal could say nothing against the daring and practical force of the proposal, but he could not persuade either himself or his friends to entertain the idea.

Yet it is said that the deeds in Italy that were later attributed to Hannibal as proofs of cruelty were in fact the work of this man, though circumstances were responsible as well.

## Book 9 Chapter 25: Hannibal's Greed

He does seem, however, to have been markedly fond of money, and to have employed a money-loving associate, Mago, the man in charge of operations in Bruttium.

This account I received from the Carthaginians themselves.

For natives know best, as the proverb says, not only which way the winds are blowing, but also the character of the people among whom they live.

And I heard it even more precisely from Masannasus, who brought charges in general against all Carthaginians, but especially against Hannibal and Mago the so-called Samnite, for greed.

Among other things, he said that from early youth these men had divided their operations between themselves in a very grand fashion, and although each had taken many cities both in Iberia and in Italy—some by force and some by surrender—they never shared in the same action together.

Instead, they were always scheming more against each other than against the enemy, so that one might not be present when the other captured a city, and they might neither quarrel over such matters nor have to divide the profit, since their rank and standing were equal.

## Book 9 Chapter 26: Hannibal Treats Different Cities in Different Ways

That Hannibal's nature was often constrained and altered not only by the influence of his friends, but still more by the force of circumstances, is clear both from what has already been said and from what is about to be told.

For as soon as Capua came into Roman hands, the cities at once, and quite naturally, became unsettled and began looking for opportunities and pretexts to change sides back to Rome.

It was then, it seems, that Hannibal, under the greatest pressure, fell into deep perplexity about the situation before him.

For he could neither remain in one place and keep watch over all the cities, since they lay so far apart and the enemy were moving against him with several armies,

nor could he divide his own force into many parts, because he would thereby become easy prey for the enemy both from inferiority in numbers and from his inability to be personally present everywhere.

Accordingly he was compelled to abandon some cities openly and to withdraw garrisons from others, fearing that in the shifting course of events he might lose his own soldiers along with them.

Some cities he even treated with treacherous violence, transferring their inhabitants elsewhere and exposing their property to plunder.

Because of this, some men condemned him for impiety and others for cruelty.

For together with these measures there came robberies of money, murders, and acts of violence, on one pretext or another, committed both by soldiers going out of cities and by those entering them, since each side assumed that the inhabitants left behind were on the very point of going over to the enemy.

For these reasons it is extremely difficult to pronounce on Hannibal's true character, because of both the influence of his friends and the pressure of events.

The common report, however, was that among the Carthaginians he had the reputation of being greedy for money, and among the Romans of being cruel.

## Book 9 Chapter 26a

Most people judge the size of the places just mentioned from the mere length of their perimeter.

So if someone says that the city of the Megalopolitans has a circuit of fifty stades, while that of the Lacedaemonians is forty-eight, and yet that Sparta is twice as large in area as Megalopolis, the statement seems unbelievable to them.

And if, wanting to increase their perplexity, someone adds that a city or a camp whose circuit is forty stades can be twice as large as one whose perimeter is a hundred stades, what is said appears altogether astonishing to them.

The reason for this is that we do not remember the lessons taught us in childhood through geometry.

I have been led to speak about these matters because not only ordinary people, but also some of those engaged in public life and in positions of command, are bewildered, sometimes wondering whether the city of the Lacedaemonians can really be larger—indeed much larger—than that of the Megalopolitans, though it has the smaller circuit,

and sometimes trying to estimate the number of men in camps by guessing from the perimeter alone.

A similar mistake also happens in judging the appearance of cities. Most people suppose that cities full of bends and hills contain more houses than level cities.

But this is not so, because the walls of buildings are constructed at right angles, not to the slopes of the ground, but to the level surfaces beneath them; and it turns out that the hills themselves stand upon those same surfaces.

Still, one can grasp the point in a simple way from what is visible.

For if one imagines the houses on slopes rising upward so that all of them are of equal height, it is clear that the single plane formed at the level of the roofs will be equal and parallel to the plane lying beneath the hills and beneath the foundations of the walls.

As for those who wish to command and take part in public life, yet are ignorant of such matters...

## Book 9 Chapter 27: Agrigentum

Let what has been said so far be enough on that subject. The city of the Agrigentines differs from most cities not only in the respects already mentioned, but also in strength, and especially in beauty and construction.

It is built eighteen stades from the sea, so that it lacks none of the advantages the sea provides.

Its circuit of walls is exceptionally secure both by nature and by art.

For the wall stands on a rock that is steep and broken all around, in some parts naturally so and in others made so by human labor, and the city is enclosed by rivers.

Along its southern side runs the river that bears the same name as the city, and along the side facing west and the southwest runs the river called Hypsas.

The citadel of the city lies above it toward the summer sunrise, enclosed on the outer side by an inaccessible ravine, and on the inner side having only one approach from the city.

On its summit stand a sanctuary of Athena and one of Zeus Atabyrius, as at Rhodes.

For since Acragas was colonized by Rhodians, it is natural that this god should bear the same title there as among the Rhodians.

The rest of the city too is splendidly adorned with temples and colonnades. And though the temple of Olympian Zeus has not been completely finished, in conception and size it seems inferior to none of those in Greece.

Polybius says that both the river and the city were called Acragas from the fertility of the land.

Marcus, after giving guarantees of safety, persuaded them to withdraw to Italy, on the condition that they should receive rations from the people of Rhegium while they ravaged Bruttium, and that they should keep whatever profit they won from enemy territory.

## Book 9 Chapter 28: Greece: Philip Reduces Thessaly

That the Macedonian domination, men of Lacedaemon, proved to be the beginning of slavery for the Greeks, I am persuaded no one would dare to deny; and you may consider the matter in this way.

There was a federation of Greeks in the Thracian region, colonized by Athenians and Chalcidians, among whom the city of Olynthus possessed the greatest standing and power.

When Philip enslaved it and made it an example, he became master not only of the cities in Thrace but also brought the Thessalians under his control through fear.

Not long afterward he defeated the Athenians in battle and behaved magnanimously in his success—not because he wished to benefit the Athenians, far from it, but in order that by his kindness toward them he might induce the rest to do willingly what he commanded.

At that time the prestige of your own city still remained, and it looked likely that in a suitable moment Sparta would place herself once more at the head of Greece.

Therefore Philip seized on every plausible pretext, came with his forces, and ravaged your land by cutting it down and by burning your houses.

In the end, after stripping you of both cities and countryside, he assigned part of your territory to the Argives, part to the Tegeans and Megalopolitans, and part to the Messenians, wishing to confer benefits on all of them, even against justice itself, on one condition only—that he should injure you.

Alexander succeeded him in power. And how he in turn destroyed Thebes, thinking that some small spark of Greek spirit still remained there, I suppose you all know well enough.

## Book 9 Chapter 29: Speech of Chlaeneas

And as for the successors who inherited his power, why need I speak in detail of the way they have treated the Greeks?

For there is no one alive so unconcerned with public affairs that he has not heard how Antipater, after defeating the Greeks in the Lamian War, treated the wretched Athenians most cruelly, and the others likewise.

His violence and lawlessness went so far that he appointed men to hunt down exiles and sent them to the cities after all who had spoken against, or in any way offended, the house of Macedon.

Some of these men were dragged by force from sanctuaries, others torn away from altars and put to death under torture, while those who escaped were driven out of all Greece; for they had no refuge except among the Aetolian people alone.

And who does not know what Cassander and Demetrius did, and with them Antigonus Gonatas? Since these events happened not long ago, the memory of them is perfectly clear.

Some of them planted garrisons in the cities, others installed tyrants, and so left no city untouched by the name and reality of slavery.

But passing over these, I will now come to the last Antigonus, so that none of you, by viewing his conduct too innocently, may suppose yourselves under an obligation of gratitude to the Macedonians.

For Antigonus did not take up war against you because he meant to save the Achaeans, nor because he was offended by the tyranny of Cleomenes and wished to set the Lacedaemonians free.

That way of thinking would be very simple-minded indeed, if any of you hold it.

No: he saw that his own power would not be secure if you won mastery over the Peloponnesians; and he also saw that Cleomenes was naturally well fitted for that task, while Fortune was splendidly aiding you.

So he came in fear and jealousy, not to help the Peloponnesians, but to deprive you of your hopes and to humble your superiority.

Therefore you ought not so much to feel gratitude to the Macedonians because, after taking your city, they did not plunder it, as to regard them as enemies and hate them, because more than once already they have prevented you, when you were capable, from taking the leadership of Greece.

## Book 9 Chapter 30: Impiety of Philip

And as for Philip's lawless conduct, what need is there to say more?

Of his impiety toward the gods, the outrages committed against the temples at Thermus are enough; and of his cruelty toward men, his bad faith and treaty-breaking toward the Messenians.

For the Aetolians alone among the Greeks stood face to face with Antipater for the safety of those unjustly deprived of security; they alone resisted Brennus and the barbarians with him; and they alone, when called upon, fought beside you,

wishing to help restore to you your ancestral leadership of the Greeks.

Let that be enough on these matters. As for the present proposal before you, it is in one sense necessary to write and vote as though you were deliberating about war; yet in truth you must not think of this as a war at all.

For the Achaeans are not even going to imagine that they can harm your territory, and I think they will be deeply grateful to the gods if they can preserve their own when war closes around them—war from the Eleans and Messenians because of their alliance with us, and from us as well.

And I am fully persuaded that Philip will cease from his attack when he is being warred upon by land by the Aetolians and by sea by the Romans and King Attalus.

What is coming can be very easily inferred from what has already happened.

For if he was never able to subdue the Aetolians when he was fighting them alone, how could he reasonably be expected to hold out in the present war?

## Book 9 Chapter 31: Conclusion of the Speech

Let what I have said stand, then, in keeping with my original purpose: namely, to show you all that, even if you are not already committed, but are deliberating from a free starting point, it is more fitting for us to be allied with the Aetolians than with the Macedonians.

But if you are already prepossessed and have made up your minds about these matters, what argument is left?

For if you had made the alliance you now have with us before the benefits Antigonus conferred on you, there might perhaps have been room to question whether you ought, in deference to later favors, to disregard something of your earlier obligations.

But since it was after Antigonus had brought about that much-trumpeted freedom and safety—which these men throw in your teeth at every turn—that you then deliberated, repeatedly considered with yourselves whether you ought to join the Aetolians or the Macedonians, and chose to side with us Aetolians, giving us guarantees on this point, receiving the same from us, and even fighting beside us in the last war against the Macedonians, who can reasonably still be in doubt?

For whatever kindly relations you had with Antigonus and Philip were then set aside.

What remains is either to show that some wrong has since been done to you by the Aetolians, or some favor by the Macedonians; or else, if neither of these things has happened, how can you now, under the persuasion of those to whom you once rightly refused to listen when you were still free, be about to undo treaties, oaths, and the strongest pledges known among men?

With words like these Chlaeneas ended his speech, having spoken, it seemed, in a way that left little room for reply.

## Book 9 Chapter 32: Lyciscus Replies To Chlaeneas

After this Lyciscus, envoy of the Acarnanians, came forward. At first he paused, seeing that most of the audience were talking among themselves about what had just been said.

But when at last they fell silent, he began to speak more or less as follows:

"We, men of Lacedaemon, have come to you sent by the Acarnanian league; and since we have almost always shared the same hopes as the Macedonians, we regard this embassy as common to us and to them.

For just as in danger, because of the superiority and greatness of Macedonian power, our safety is included in their strength, so also in diplomatic contests the interest of the Acarnanians is included in the justice of the Macedonians.

Therefore you should not be surprised if we speak mostly on behalf of Philip and the Macedonians.

Now Chlaeneas, in bringing his speech to a close, gave a sharp summary of the claims that stand between you and his people.

For he said that if, after you had made your alliance with them, some fresh injury and offense had been committed by the Aetolians, or, by Zeus, some act of kindness by the Macedonians, then the present proposal might properly be considered as though from a new starting point.

But if nothing of that sort had happened, and we think that by bringing forward the services of Antigonus and your former decisions we shall now persuade you to undo oaths and treaties, then we are the simplest fools alive.

I for my part admit that, if nothing has happened since, as he says, and affairs in Greece remain just as they were before when you made the alliance with the Aetolians, then I am indeed the greatest fool of all and am about to offer you arguments that are empty.

But if the situation has taken exactly the opposite turn, as I shall clearly show as the speech goes on, then I think it will appear that I am giving you useful advice, and that Chlaeneas is mistaken.

For we have come persuaded that we ought to make our argument precisely on this point: to show why it is both fitting and advantageous for you, if possible, after hearing of the danger now threatening Greece, to resolve upon some noble and worthy course by sharing our hopes.

And if not..."

## Book 9 Chapter 33: Defence of Macedonian Policy

If you cannot do that, then for the present at least remain quiet. But since these men have dared from ancient times onward to accuse the house of Macedon, I think it necessary first to speak briefly on those matters, so as to remove the ignorance of those who have trusted what was said.

Chlaeneas said, then, that Philip son of Amyntas became master of Thessaly through the misfortune of the Olynthians.

I, however, believe that through Philip not only the Thessalians, but the rest of the Greeks as well, were saved.

For when Onomarchus and Philomelus seized Delphi in impious and lawless fashion and made themselves masters of the god's treasures, who among you does not know that they assembled so great a force that no Greek any longer dared face them?

Indeed, together with their impiety toward the divine, they were on the point of becoming masters of all Greece as well.

At that moment Philip voluntarily offered himself, overthrew the tyrants, secured the sanctuary, and became the cause of Greek freedom, as the facts themselves have testified both then and to posterity.

For it was not because Philip had wronged the Thessalians, as this man dared to claim, but because he was a benefactor of Greece, that all chose him as commander by land and sea—an honor no human being before him had received.

Yes, by Zeus, but he came into Laconia with an army.

Not by his own choice, as you know, but because the friends and allies in the Peloponnese repeatedly invited and summoned him, until at last he consented.

And once he came, Chlaeneas, look at how he handled the situation. Though he could have made use of the eagerness of the neighboring cities to destroy this people's land and humble their city, and could have done so with the greatest gratitude from others,

he did not at all give himself over to such a course. Instead, after overawing both sides, he compelled them by argument, in the common interest, to bring their disputes before an assembly.

And he did not appoint himself judge over the matters in dispute, but set up a common court selected from all the Greeks. Is that something worthy of reproach and denunciation?

## Book 9 Chapter 34: Contrast Between Alexander and the Aetolians

Again, you bitterly reproached Alexander because, believing himself to have been wronged, he punished the city of Thebes.

But you made no mention of the fact that he exacted vengeance from the Persians for the insults done to all the Greeks,

or that he delivered all of us together from great evils by subduing the barbarians and taking away from them the resources with which they had ruined the Greeks—sometimes by setting the Athenians and their ancestors against others, sometimes the Thebans—and in the end made Asia subject to the Greeks.

And as for his successors, how do you dare even mention them? In the circumstances of their times they often became the cause of good to some and of harm to others.

Perhaps other people might have some right to resent them for this. But it is not at all fitting for you to do so, since you have never been responsible for any good to anyone, but for many evils to many people and many times over.

Who was it that called in Antigonus son of Demetrius to divide up the Achaean nation?

Who was it that made oaths and treaties with Alexander of Epirus for the enslavement and partition of Acarnania?

Was it not you? And what other people has ever sent out such commanders as you have—men who even dared to lay hands on sacred and inviolable sanctuaries?

Timaeus plundered the sanctuary of Poseidon at Taenarum and the sanctuary of Artemis at Lusi.

Pharycus and Polycritus robbed, one the precinct of Hera at Argos, the other that of Poseidon at Mantinea.

And what of Lattabus and Nicostratus? Did they not, in time of peace, violate their agreement at the assembly of the Pam-Boeotians, carrying out deeds worthy of Scythians and Gauls? Nothing of this kind was done by the Successors.

## Book 9 Chapter 35: Services of Macedonians To Greece

And though you can make no defense against any of these charges, you still speak grandly of how you resisted the barbarians in their attack on Delphi, and claim that for this Greece ought to be grateful to you.

But if gratitude is owed to the Aetolians for this one service, what honor, and how great an honor, should be judged due to the Macedonians, who spend most of their lives never ceasing to fight the barbarians for the safety of the Greeks?

For who does not know that Greek affairs would always have been in great danger if we had not possessed the Macedonians as a barrier and the ambition of their kings?

And the clearest proof of this is that, as soon as the Gauls learned to despise the Macedonians after defeating Ptolemy called Ceraunus, they at once despised the rest as well, and Brennus marched with his army straight into central Greece. And this would often have happened if the Macedonians had not stood in the way.

Still, though I could say much about past events, I think this is enough.

Yet of all Philip's acts, these men chose to reproach him with the destruction of a temple as evidence of impiety, without adding a word about their own outrage and lawlessness in what they did to the temples and sacred precincts at Dium and Dodona.

That was the first thing that ought to have been said. But the wrongs you yourselves suffered you recount to these men at length, exaggerating what happened, while the many times greater wrongs that you committed first you pass over in silence,

well knowing that everyone always lays the blame for acts of violence and damage upon those who first set their hands to unjust deeds.

## Book 9 Chapter 36: Sparta Should Have Allied Herself with the Macedonians

I wish to mention Antigonus only so far as not to seem to despise what happened or to treat so great a deed as a side issue.

For my own part, I do not believe that any greater benefaction than the one Antigonus conferred on you is even recorded in history; indeed, the deed seems to me beyond all comparison.

And one may see this from the facts. Antigonus went to war with you and afterward defeated you in battle. By force of arms he became master of both your territory and your city.

He might have exercised all the rights of war. Yet so far was he from doing anything terrible against you that, besides other acts, he expelled the tyrant and restored your laws and ancestral constitution.

In return, at the common festivals, calling the Greeks as witnesses, you yourselves proclaimed Antigonus your benefactor and savior.

What, then, ought you to have done? I will say what seems true, gentlemen; and you, I trust, will bear with me. For I say this now not from any desire to heap insults on you without cause, but because the pressure of events forces me to speak for the common good.

What do I mean? That both in the previous war you ought to have allied yourselves not with the Aetolians but with the Macedonians, and that now too, when invited, you ought to attach yourselves to Philip rather than to these men.

Yes, by Zeus—but then you would be violating treaties.

And which would be the worse thing to do: to disregard the private agreements you have made with the Aetolians, or the arrangements ratified before all the Greeks and consecrated on a public stele?

And how is it that you shrink from breaking with these men, from whom you have received no prior benefit, while you feel no shame toward Philip and the Macedonians, through whom you possess even the power to deliberate at all today?

Or do you think it necessary to do what is just toward friends?

Yet it is not so holy a thing to keep written pledges secure as it is unholy to make war on those who saved you—and that is exactly what the Aetolians have now come here to urge you to do.

## Book 9 Chapter 37: A Mightly Cloud is Coming From the West

Let what I have said be granted for the moment, even if stricter judges decide it lies outside the immediate issue. I will return to the central point, as these men define it.

Their point was this: if circumstances now are the same as when you made your alliance with them, then your own policy ought to remain as it is now; for that was their starting principle.

But if the situation has been completely altered, then it is just that you should now deliberate afresh about what is being urged upon you.

So I ask you, Cleonicus and Chlaeneas, what allies did you have when you first invited these men into joint action? Was it not all the Greeks?

And with whom do you now share your hopes, or into what alliance are you now calling them?

Is it not into an alliance with barbarians? You seem to think the present circumstances are exactly like the former ones, though in fact they are the opposite.

Then you were contending for leadership and prestige against the Achaeans and Macedonians, men of your own race, and against Philip their leader. But now war confronts the Greeks over the issue of slavery, against men of another race.

You think you are drawing these people in against Philip, but you do not realize that you have drawn them in against yourselves and against all Greece.

For just as men in wartime, by bringing into their cities garrisons stronger than their own forces for the sake of safety, both ward off the fear that comes from enemies and at the same time make themselves subject to the power of their friends, so the Aetolians are acting now.

Desiring to prevail over Philip and humble the Macedonians, they have failed to notice that they have drawn in from the west so great a cloud that, for the present, it may first cast its shadow over Macedonia, but in the end will become the cause of great evils for all Greece.

## Book 9 Chapter 38: Contrast of the Aetolian Policy

All Greeks, then, ought to foresee the crisis that is coming, but the Lacedaemonians above all.

For why do you suppose, men of Lacedaemon, that when Xerxes sent an envoy to you demanding earth and water, your ancestors threw him into a well and, tossing earth on top of him, told him to report back to Xerxes that from the Lacedaemonians he had received exactly what he demanded—earth and water?

And why again did Leonidas and his men set out willingly and openly to die?

Was it not so that they might seem to risk themselves in advance not only for their own freedom, but also for that of the rest of the Greeks?

It would indeed be worthy of the descendants of such men, and now in your present condition, to make an alliance with barbarians, march with them, and make war on Epirotes, Achaeans, Acarnanians, Boeotians, Thessalians—in short, on nearly all the Greeks except the Aetolians.

For the Aetolians it is their custom to do such things and to regard nothing as shameful so long as there is some profit in it. But that is not your way.

And what are we to expect these men to do now that they have taken the Romans into alliance?

Why, when they gained strength and support from the Illyrians, they at once set about piratical violence at sea and seized Pylus in violation of treaty, while on land they besieged the city of Cleitor and enslaved the people of Cynaetha.

Earlier too, as I said above, these same men made agreements with Antigonus against the Achaean and Acarnanian peoples; and now they have made an agreement with the Romans against all Greece.

## Book 9 Chapter 39: Sparta Must Be On Guard Against Attack from Rome

Who, on hearing these facts, would not both be suspicious of the Roman advance and hate the recklessness of the Aetolians for daring to make such treaties?

Already they have stripped the Acarnanians of Oeniadae and Nesus; and quite recently they seized the city of the unfortunate Anticyreans, enslaving it together with the Romans.

The Romans carry off the children and women, who will plainly suffer the usual fate of those who fall under the power of foreigners; while the Aetolians inherit the lands of the unfortunate victims.

A fine alliance indeed this is to join by deliberate choice—especially for Lacedaemonians,

who once decreed that the Thebans, because alone among the Greeks they had resolved to remain quiet during the Persian invasion, should pay a tithe to the gods after the barbarians had been defeated in war.

The honorable and fitting course for you, men of Lacedaemon, is to remember your ancestors, to beware the Roman advance, to suspect the wicked enterprise of the Aetolians, and above all to remember what Antigonus did for you; then even now show your hatred of baseness, turn away from friendship with the Aetolians, and share the same hopes as the Achaeans and Macedonians.

But if some of the more powerful men among you oppose this, then at least incline toward neutrality and do not take part in the wrongdoing of these Aetolians.

## Book 9 Chapter 40: Philip in Thessaly

For the Athenian city always wishes to preserve that kind of character.

For zeal shown by friends at the right moment gives great help, but if it drags behind and comes too late it makes assistance altogether useless.

At least if they wish to keep their alliance not merely in writing, but in deeds as well...

The Acarnanians, when they learned of the Aetolian attack against them, partly despairing in their expectations and partly fighting with their anger, came to a terrible resolve.

If any man, left alive, should fail to die but instead flee the danger, he was not to be admitted into any city or given fire to kindle.

And they uttered curses against all alike, but most of all against the Epirotes, so that no one who fled should be received in their land.

## Book 9 Chapter 41: Investment of Echinus by Philip

Having decided to make his approach to the city opposite two towers, Philip constructed in front of them tortoise-shelters for earthworks and battering rams, and opposite the space between the towers he built a covered gallery between the rams running parallel to the wall.

When this design was completed, the outward appearance of the works came to resemble the arrangement of the city wall itself.

For the structures built on top of the tortoises took on the form and appearance of towers because of the way the wicker screens were fitted together, while the space between them looked like a wall because the upper wickerwork of the gallery had been woven into battlements.

In the lower part of the towers, the men who were filling in the uneven ground so that the ram-frames could advance kept throwing in earth, and the battering ram itself was pushed forward.

The second level contained jars of water, the equipment needed to fight fires, and the artillery besides.

On the third stood a large number of men posted to fight those who tried to damage the ram; and these were level in height with the towers of the city.

From the gallery between the towers double trenches were carried forward toward the space between the city towers.

And there were three artillery positions for stone-throwers, one of which discharged stones of a talent's weight, while the other two threw stones of thirty minas.

From the camp to the earthwork-tortoises roofed tunnels had been made, so that neither those coming from the camp to the works nor those returning from the works would suffer any harm from missiles shot from the city.

In very few days the entire work was completed, because the region had abundant supplies for everything needed in the operation.

For the Echinaians dwell on the Melian Gulf, facing south, opposite the territory of the Thronians, and they cultivate exceptionally fertile land. For this reason Philip lacked nothing needed for his assault.

And so, as I said, once the works were complete, they immediately brought up both the trenchworks and the siege engines.

## Book 9 Chapter 42: Asia and Egypt

Publius, the Roman commander, and Dorimachus, the Aetolian leader, arrived while Philip was besieging the city of Echinus, after he had secured his approaches to the wall well and fortified the outer face of his camp with trench and wall.

Publius came with a fleet and Dorimachus with infantry and cavalry, and when they attacked the entrenchment they were repulsed, since Philip fought the more vigorously.

The people of Echinus therefore despaired and surrendered themselves to Philip.

For Dorimachus and his force were unable, through lack of supplies, to reduce Philip, who was provisioning himself by sea.

When Aegina was taken by the Romans, the Aeginetans who had not escaped gathered by the ships and begged the commander to permit them to send envoys to kindred cities to arrange ransom.

At first Publius answered harshly, saying that when they were still masters of themselves, then was the time to send embassies to their betters about their safety—not now, when they had become slaves.

And if, a little before, they had not thought ambassadors sent by him worthy even of a hearing, how was it not absurd that now, when they were captives, they should demand to send envoys to their kinsmen?

So on that occasion he sent them away with those words. But on the following day he called all the prisoners together and said that he owed the Aeginetans no kindness; nevertheless, for the sake of the rest of the Greeks, he granted them leave to send envoys about ransom, since that was their custom.

## Book 9 Chapter 43: Nature of the Euphrates River

For the Euphrates takes its beginning in Armenia and flows through Syria and the lands beyond toward Babylonia.

It seems to discharge itself into the Red Sea, but that is not in fact so. Its waters are spent first on the channels led out into the countryside before it can make an outlet into the sea.

Thus it turns out that this river has a nature contrary to that of most rivers. Most streams grow larger the farther they pass through more regions, and they are highest in winter and lowest at the height of summer.

But the Euphrates is fullest at the rising of the Dog Star and greatest in the Syrian regions, and then continually grows smaller as it advances.

The reason is that its increase does not come from the gathering of winter rains, but from the melting of snow, while its decrease comes from the diversions made into the country and from its division for irrigation.

That is why on that occasion the transport of the army was slow: the boats were heavily loaded, the river was at its lowest, and the force of the current contributed only very little to the voyage.

## Book 9 Chapter 44: Embassy from Rome to Ptolemy

Those who enter upon undertakings without goodwill and zeal are not likely to prove true allies when the actions themselves arrive.

And it is true, as I have often said, that it is impossible to comprehend or grasp in the mind the finest spectacle in history—I mean the ordering of events as a whole—from writers who record only separate actions.
