# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXXVIII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 38 Chapter 1: Character of Hasdrubal

This book contains the culmination of the Greeks' misfortune.

For although Greece as a whole, and in particular its individual states, had often suffered setbacks, still the term and idea of "misfortune" could not be applied more fittingly to any of the disasters of the past than to those that have happened in our own time.

For one would pity the Greeks not only for what they suffered, but would judge them even more unfortunate for what they did, once one has learned the truth about each case.

Indeed, although the disaster that befell Carthage may seem to have been the greatest of all, one would perhaps judge what happened in Greece at that time to have been no less, and in some respects greater.

For the Carthaginians at least left behind some room for later generations to plead their case; but these men gave no reasonable opening even to those who would have wished to help them for the wrongs they had done.

And while the Carthaginians, once overwhelmed by the catastrophe, became unaware of their future misfortunes, the Greeks, watching their own disasters unfold, handed down their misery to their children's children.

So, just as we think those who live on in suffering after punishment are more pitiable than those who lose life at the moment of their disaster, so too the calamities that then befell the Greeks must be judged more pitiable than what happened to the Carthaginians — unless someone disregards duty and honor and judges everything only by material advantage.

If anyone doubts that what I have just said is true, let him recall and compare the disasters commonly regarded as the greatest in Greece with what I have now said.

## Book 38 Chapter 2: Misery In Carthage

When Scipio said this to Gulussa, he laughed and replied: "So it was for this that you treated our prisoners with such shocking cruelty! And you trust in the gods after violating even human law?"

The king then went on to remind Scipio that the affair had to be finished quickly, above all because unforeseen events might intervene and because the consular elections were now close at hand; it was only right to bear that in mind, lest if winter found them where they were, another consul would come and replace him and take the whole credit for his work without effort.

Scipio then instructed that Hasdrubal should be given safe conduct for himself, his wife and children, and ten families of his friends and relatives, together with permission to take ten talents from his own property and whichever of his slaves he wished to bring out with him.

With these terms agreed, Gulussa went to meet Hasdrubal on the third day.

Hasdrubal again came forward with great display and in a dignified step, dressed in his purple robe and full suit of armor, so as to make the tyrants of tragedy look small by comparison.

He was naturally fat, but by that time he had grown extremely corpulent and had become more than usually red from exposure to the sun, so that he looked like a man living like a fat ox at a fair, and in no way like a commander in the midst of such terrible misery as can scarcely be described.

When he met the king and heard the offer made by the consul, he slapped his thigh again and again and, calling on the gods and Fortune, declared that the day would never come when Hasdrubal would behold the sun and his native city in flames; for to a noble spirit, one's country and its burning houses are a glorious funeral pyre.

These words compel us to admire the man and the nobility of his speech; but when we look at his administration of affairs, we cannot fail to be struck by his lack of courage and spirit.

For at a time when his fellow citizens were dying of hunger, he gave banquets and served expensive second courses, and by his own excellent physical condition made their misery all the more obvious.

The number of the dying, and the number of those who deserted daily because of hunger, was beyond belief.

Yet by harshly rebuking some and executing or abusing others, he kept the common people cowed; and by that means, in a country reduced to the lowest depths of disaster, he maintained authority that a tyrant would scarcely enjoy in a prosperous city.

So I think I was right to say that it would not be easy to find two men more alike than those who were then directing the affairs of Greece and Carthage. That will become clear when we come to a formal comparison between them.

## Book 38 Chapter 3: Consummation of the Misfortunes of Greece

After Alexander's campaign, they therefore, after a short time and with some help received, once again lived in their homeland in safety.

For the pity shown by outsiders is no small comfort to those who suffer undeserved hardship, especially when one often sees that with the impulses of the many, fortune too changes, and those in power repent and set right the reversals of those who have suffered unexpectedly.

Again, at certain times the Chalcidians and Corinthians and some other cities yielded to the Macedonian kings because of the favorable position of their places and kept garrisons there;

but everyone was eager to free those who were enslaved as far as possible, while those who had enslaved others were hated and regarded as enemies to the end.

In general, cities formerly tended to fail and go astray as a rule — some because they disputed over power and politics, others because they were betrayed by monarchs and kings.

So you would rarely find any on whom disgrace followed their downfall, or in whom the very name of misfortune remained.

For all who fall into unexpected disasters should be counted as unfortunate, both collectively and individually; but only those are truly called unfortunate whose actions bring shame because of their own folly.

In the circumstances then at issue, the Peloponnesians, Boeotians, Phocians, Locrians, some who lived by the Ionian Gulf, and after them the Macedonians too suffered alike.

Not only in number, but in every way, they were not merely unfortunate in the ordinary sense, but suffered a shameful sort of misfortune, as dishonorable as possible.

For at once they showed distrust and cowardice, and their actions brought about their downfall through ...

Thus, stripped of all good things, some perished from hunger, while others, driven to desperation, even then took rods and axes into the cities ...

For they were in great fear because of the excess of their own mistakes, if it is right to say so.

For I would say that the majority were simply ignorant and had gone astray from what was fitting, but that those who caused such widespread ignorance were themselves guilty.

## Book 38 Chapter 4: Previous Disasters

There is no need to be surprised if, by departing from the ordinary style of historical narrative, I seem to make my account of these matters more elaborate and more ambitious.

Yet some may perhaps criticize me as writing with hostility, when it was above all right to conceal the mistakes of the Greeks.

But I would never consider a true friend, in the judgment of sensible men, to be one who fears and shrinks from speaking plainly,

nor would I consider a good citizen to be one who abandons the truth because of the offense that may be caused at the time.

Nor should a writer of public affairs be accepted at all if he thinks anything more important than truth.

For the more widely the transmission of what is written reaches people, and the longer it lasts, the more the writer must value truth above all, and the readers must accept such a choice.

In moments of crisis it is proper for Greeks to help Greeks in every possible way — by defending them, by softening criticism, and by warding off the anger of those in power; and that is what we ourselves did in the actual events.

But in the written record that is passed on to later generations, the account of what happened must be left free of all falsehood, so that readers are not merely pleased for the moment by what they hear, but have their souls corrected so that they do not repeatedly make the same mistakes.

So let this be enough on this subject.

## Book 38 Chapter 5: Unjust Misfortune Distinguished from Self-Inflicted Loss

I am not unaware that some will criticize this work, saying that I have made the exposition of events incomplete and scattered.

For if, after setting out to describe the siege of Carthage, we then leave it aside and pass in between to Greek affairs, and from there to Macedonian or Syrian or some other events, they will complain.

But those who love learning seek continuity and look forward to hearing the end of the account; for in that way both pleasure and benefit accompany those who pay attention more fully.

For my part I do not think so at all; rather the opposite. I could call nature itself to witness, since she does not allow any of the senses to remain continuously fixed on the same things, but is always inclined to change and wants to encounter the same things only at intervals and with variety.

This is clear first from hearing, which takes no pleasure either in music or in spoken performance if it remains always in the same posture.

Rather, what moves it is a changing manner and, in general, anything scattered, varied, and full of frequent shifts.

Likewise one could find that taste cannot remain fixed even on the most luxurious foods, but grows weary of them and delights in changes, often welcoming plain fare more readily than rich fare because of novelty.
The same thing can be seen in sight as well:

for it can least of all remain fixed on a single object, and it is variety and change in what is seen that set it in motion.

Most of all one could see this happening in the soul: changes of attention and concentration are, as it were, rests for those who labor hard...

## Book 38 Chapter 6: Rome and the Achaean League

For those who are diligent in such matters, the greatest historians of old seem to me to have taken their rest in this manner: some by means of digressions into myth and storytelling, others by means of digressions into actual history,

so that they not only make transitions within the places of Greece itself, but also include matters beyond it.

I mean, for example, that when they are recounting events in Thessaly and the deeds of Alexander of Pherae, they interrupt themselves to narrate Spartan activity in the Peloponnese, and then again the deeds of the Thebans, and further still those in Macedonia or Illyria; and then, after spending time elsewhere, they tell of Iphicrates' expedition to Egypt and of Clearchus' crimes in the Pontus.

From this one could see that all of them use the same kind of handling, but use it without order, whereas we use it in an orderly way.

For they, after mentioning how Bardyllis, king of the Illyrians, and Cersobleptes, king of the Thracians, seized their power, do not continue the story straight on, nor do they return to it later after an interval to pick up what follows, but, like a poem, go back again to their original subject.

Whereas we, having divided up all the most notable regions of the inhabited world and the events that occur in them, and always proceeding by the same method in keeping to the order of our arrangement, and also setting out the fitting events of each year in turn,

make the return to the continuous narrative and the intervening events obvious to students, so that nothing incomplete or missing happens for those who are attentive to what has been said.

And that is enough on this subject.

## Book 38 Chapter 7: New Commissioners Sent to Achaia

Hasdrubal, the Carthaginian general, was vain, boastful, and very far removed from real military judgment and skill.

There were many signs of his recklessness. First, he was present in full armor, fastened with a sea-purple cloak, when he was meeting with Gulussa, the king of the Numidians, and he had ten sword-bearers with him.

Then he moved forward only about twenty feet, having set up a trench and palisade, and signaled to the king to come to him, when the opposite should have been the case.

Gulussa, however, taking a simple and nomad-like approach, came up to him alone; and when he drew near, he asked what he feared that he had come in armor.

When Hasdrubal said, "The Romans," Gulussa replied, "Then you should not have given yourself up to the city at all, since you had no need to do so. But what do you want, and what are you asking for?"

Hasdrubal answered: "I ask you to act as an envoy to the general and to take responsibility for everything, since we will do what is ordered; only spare this miserable city."

Gulussa replied, "You seem to me, my good sir, to be asking for a childish favor.

For when you were acting as ambassadors from an intact position and the Romans were still at Utica, you could not persuade them; how can you now expect these things to be granted to you, when you are surrounded on land and sea and have nearly given up all hope of safety?"

Hasdrubal said that he did not understand him. For he still had fair hopes in his external allies; he had not yet heard about the Maurusians or the troops outside the walls, saying that they were safe; and he was by no means despairing of his own situation. Above all, he trusted in the aid of the gods and in the hopes he placed in them.

For they would not, he said, allow themselves to be seen openly violating a truce, but would give many opportunities for salvation.

For that reason he urged the general, for the sake of the gods and of Fortune, to spare the city, knowing clearly that if they could not obtain this, they would be slaughtered before they surrendered it.

Then, after saying these and similar things, they parted, having arranged to meet again on the third day.

## Book 38 Chapter 8: The Commissioners Arrive in Achaia

When Gulussa reported what had been said to the general, Popilius laughed and said, "So this is what you were preparing to ask for after displaying such outrageous impiety toward our prisoners? And now you place your hopes in the gods, though you have broken the laws of men as well?"

The king then wanted to remind Scipio of a further point, especially that an end must now be brought to the business:

for apart from all the uncertain things, the season of the consuls was already drawing near, and he said that this ought to be taken into account, lest, if winter overtook him, another man should come and take, without effort, the credit for his labors.

Accordingly, when these things had been said, the general announced that he granted safety to Hasdrubal, to his wife and children, and to ten households of his relatives and friends; and in addition to these he was to receive ten talents from his own property and to take out as many as one hundred of his slaves, choosing whom he wished.

With these humane terms in hand, Gulussa went back to Hasdrubal on the third day.

Hasdrubal in turn came out again in great style, wearing his purple robe and full armor as he walked, so that he seemed to outdo the tyrants of tragedy by a wide margin.

He was naturally a fleshy man, but by then he had also developed a large belly and had been burned beyond his natural color by the sun, so that he looked as though he were living at some festival, like a fattened ox, and not at all like a commander amid such and so great a disaster — one that no one could even fully describe in words.

When he met the king and heard the terms offered by the general, he slapped his thigh repeatedly and, calling on the gods and Fortune, said that the day would never come on which Hasdrubal would see the sun and his fatherland ablaze at the same time.

For, he said, the country and its fire are a fine funeral pyre for those who think nobly.

So when one looks at his speeches, one marvels at the man and the greatness of soul in his words; but when one looks at his management of affairs, one is stunned by his baseness and cowardice.

For first, while the rest of the citizens were being utterly ruined by hunger, he held drinking parties and set out lavish second courses, displaying their misery through his own well-being.

The number of those dying was incredible, and so too the number of those who deserted each day because of hunger.

Then, by mocking some, abusing others, and killing others, he terrified the many, and by this means maintained power — a power that a tyrant would scarcely use even in a prosperous city, let alone in a city already brought low by disaster.

So I think it was rightly said by me that among the Greeks and among the Carthaginians of that time one would not easily find two men more alike as masters of affairs.

That will become clear when we compare them side by side."

## Book 38 Chapter 9: Foolish Policy In Achaia

When the envoys who had come from the Peloponnese to Aurelius arrived and explained what had happened to them,

saying that they had come very close to losing their lives altogether, and telling the story with exaggeration and novelty — for they did not describe the danger as something that had come upon them by chance, but as though the Achaeans had deliberately set out to make an example of them —

the Senate was angered by what had taken place, and at once appointed the commissioners around Julius, sending them with instructions of this sort:

that they should rebuke and censure what had happened, but in moderation; and that they should above all continue to exhort the Achaeans not to listen to those who urged them toward the worst course, and not to let themselves drift into hostility with Rome, but still now to make some correction for their previous ignorance by laying the blame for the error on those who caused it.

From this it became very clear that the Senate had given its instructions to Aurelius and his colleagues not because it wished to break the nation apart, but because it wanted to frighten and overawe the Achaeans' arrogance and hostility.

Some people, therefore, assumed that the Romans were acting with hypocrisy because the war in Carthage was still unfinished; but the truth was not like that.

The fact is that they had long regarded the nation with favor and considered it the most trustworthy of the Greek states; and although now they thought it necessary to alarm it because it had become overbearing, they by no means wished to make war on the Achaeans or have a full-scale quarrel with them.

## Book 38 Chapter 10: Riotous Scene at Corinth

When the party around Sextus, coming from Rome into the Peloponnese, met the envoys around Thearidas,

who had been sent by the Achaeans to ask pardon and to explain to the Senate the offenses committed against Aurelius,

the men just mentioned urged them to turn back to Achaia, saying that they themselves had instructions to discuss all these matters with the Achaeans.

When the party around Sextus had arrived in the Peloponnese and was speaking with the Achaeans in the city of the Aegians and using many friendly words,

and when they set aside the charge against the ambassadors and scarcely felt any need of a defense, but accepted the event more kindly even than the Achaeans themselves, they urged them above all not to go any further in their error, either against the Romans or against the Lacedaemonians;

the more sober-minded group welcomed what was said and was greatly ashamed, conscious of what it had done and taking before its eyes what happens to those who oppose Rome,

but the mass of the people had nothing to say in answer to the justice of what the envoys from Sextus were saying. They kept silent, but remained sick at heart and corrupted.

Meanwhile the party of Diaeus and Critolaus — and all who shared the same opinion — men who had been deliberately selected as the worst from every city, enemies of the gods and destroyers of the nation,

accepted the gifts of the Romans, as the proverb says, with the left hand, and in their calculations they went completely astray.

For they imagined that the Romans, because of their affairs in Libya and Iberia, feared a war with the Achaeans and would put up with anything and say anything.

Thinking, then, that the moment was theirs, they answered the envoys with friendly words, saying that they would nevertheless send Thearidas and his colleagues to the Senate, while they themselves would accompany the commissioners to Tegea and there confer with the Lacedaemonians so that some agreed settlement of the war might be reached.

By giving these answers they afterwards led the wretched nation into the folly that had long before been laid out for them.

And that, naturally enough, came about through the inexperience and wickedness of the men in power.

## Book 38 Chapter 11: Violent Policy of Critolaus

The end of the disaster was brought about in the following way.

When the commissioners around Sextus had arrived in Tegea and summoned the Lacedaemonians, in order to work out with them an agreement with the Achaeans regarding both the previous complaints and the pause in the war until the Romans should send commissioners to examine the whole matter,

the men around Critolaus decided in council that the others should avoid the meeting, while Critolaus himself should go forward to Tegea.

So the man just mentioned came, by then, when those around Sextus had almost given him up entirely;

and when the meeting with the Lacedaemonians took place, he agreed to nothing, saying that he had no authority to settle anything without the judgment of the many, but that he would carry the matter before the Achaeans at the next congress, which was to be held six months later.

Accordingly, when the men around Sextus had clearly recognized that Critolaus was acting in bad faith and were displeased by what he was doing, they dismissed the Lacedaemonians to their homeland and themselves returned to Italy, convinced of Critolaus' ignorance and madness.

After they left, Critolaus spent the winter going from city to city and holding assemblies, using as a pretext that he wanted to explain to them what he had said to the Lacedaemonians at Tegea,

but in fact he was denouncing the Romans and interpreting everything said by them in the worst possible way,

and by this he was working up hatred and hostility among the masses.

At the same time he ordered the magistrates not to proceed against debtors, not to accept those who were being carried off to prison for debt, and to suspend loans until the war should be decided.

From this kind of demagoguery everything he said came to be believed, and the masses were ready for whatever was ordered, unable to think ahead about the future and allured by the immediate favor and ease.

## Book 38 Chapter 12

Quintus Caecilius, hearing of these things in Macedonia and also of the confusion and turmoil that had arisen in the Peloponnese, sent as envoys Gnaeus Papirius and the younger Popilius Laenas, together with Aulus Gabinius and Gaius Fannius.

When they arrived at Corinth, where the Achaeans were assembled by chance at that very time, they were brought before the crowd and delivered many friendly speeches very much like those of Sextus,

showing every effort to prevent the Achaeans from moving on to a more complete hostility toward Rome, whether because of the pretext supplied by the Lacedaemonians or because of hostility toward the Romans themselves.

The majority, however, could not bear to listen at all; they mocked the ambassadors and drove them out amid uproar and shouting.

For an assembly of craftsmen and manual laborers had been gathered there such as had never before been seen: all the cities were in a state of sheer confusion, and the Corinthians above all en masse.

Only a very few were strongly pleased by what the envoys were saying.

Critolaus, thinking that he had achieved his purpose, in the midst of an audience as agitated and frenzied as himself began attacking the magistrates, abusing his political opponents, and openly defying the Roman envoys,

saying that he wished to be the friend of the Romans, but would not be pleased to have them as masters.

In general he urged them on, saying that if they proved themselves men they would not be lacking in allies, but if they proved effeminate they would not be lacking in masters.

With many such remarks, trading and maneuvering in this way, he stirred up and inflamed the crowd.

He also gave the impression that he was not acting at random in these measures, but that some of the kings and some of the governments shared his policy ...

## Book 38 Chapter 13

When some members of the council wanted to restrain him and prevent such speeches, he had the soldiers drawn aside, stood up, and told anyone who dared to come near, to approach, or even to touch his cloak.

In general he said that he had held himself in check for a long time already and could no longer bear it, but must speak what was plainly on his mind.

For it was necessary, he said, not for the Lacedaemonians or the Romans to be troubled in this way, but rather for those among their own people who cooperated with the enemy; for there were some who cared more for the Romans and the Lacedaemonians than for their own affairs.

He offered this as proof: he said that Evagoras of Aegium and Stratius of Tritaea revealed everything said in the secret meetings to the party around Gnaeus.

When Stratius admitted that he had indeed associated with those men and said that he would continue to meet with them because they were friends and allies, but swore that he had reported none of what was said in the meetings, few believed him, while the majority accepted the accusations.

Critolaus, by inflaming the crowd through these accusations, persuaded the Achaeans once again to vote for war — nominally against the Lacedaemonians, but in reality against the Romans.

He also added another unlawful decree, so that the men they chose as strategoi would have full authority. By this means he assumed a kind of monarchical power.

Having arranged all this, he went about stirring things up and laying hands on the Romans for no reason at all, but in the most impious and unlawful way possible.

As for the envoys, Gnaeus went off to Athens and from there to Lacedaemon to wait for events, Aulus went to Naupactus, and the other two remained in Athens until the arrival of Caecilius. That is how matters stood in the Peloponnese.

## Book 38 Chapter 14

Pytheas was the brother of Acastides the runner and the son of Cleomnastus. He had lived badly and seems to have squandered his youth.

Likewise in public life he was bold and grasping, and had been shaped by Eumenes and Philataerus for the reasons already mentioned.

## Book 38 Chapter 15

When Critolaus, the Achaean strategus, had died, and the law provided that if anything happened to the serving strategus the previous one should succeed to the office until the proper assembly of the Achaeans was held,

the burden of managing public affairs fell to Diaeus.

So he sent troops to Megara and then went to Argos, where he wrote to all the cities ordering them to free their household-born slaves of military age — a total of ten thousand men — arm them, and send them to Corinth.

He allotted the levy among the cities arbitrarily and unequally, as he did everything else.

Where the number of household-born slaves was insufficient, they were to make up the required quota from other slaves.

Seeing that public resources were severely strained because of the war with the Lacedaemonians, he forced the wealthy to make pledges and contribute privately, not only the men but the women as well.

Along with this he ordered the men of military age throughout the country to gather with arms at Corinth.

As a result, the cities became full of confusion, turmoil, and grief.

They praised the dead and pitied those who were setting out, and all wept as though they could already foresee what was coming.

They were especially distressed by the uproar and the burden placed on the slaves, since some had only just been freed and the others were already uplifted by that hope.

At the same time the men were forced to contribute against their own wishes whatever property they were thought to possess, while the women stripped themselves and their children of ornaments as though for a deliberate destruction.

## Book 38 Chapter 16

They were contributing to their own destruction.

For when all these things were happening at once, the shock of each separate event kept the people from taking a broader view of the whole and considering by what reasoning they ought to understand that they were all being led, with their children and women, into obvious ruin.

Instead, as though being driven and carried forward by some raging torrent, they followed the ignorance and confusion of their leaders.

The Eleians and Messenians stayed in their own territory, waiting for the danger from the fleet; nothing that happened then would have helped them if that cloud had appeared according to its original plan.

The people of Patrae and those with them had already suffered a setback in Phocis not long before, and what happened was far more pitiable than the disasters in the Peloponnese.

For some were driving themselves from life in reckless ways, while others were fleeing from the cities by back routes, leaving without any settled destination because they were stunned by what was happening in the cities.

Some were even handing one another over to the enemy as if they had become strangers to the Romans; others were informing and accusing their neighbors, though no one in the present situation had any need for such service; while others came as suppliants, admitting that they had broken agreements and asking what they would have to suffer, though no one was yet demanding an accounting.

Everything was full of a strange madness: people were throwing themselves into wells and off cliffs, so that, as the proverb says, even an enemy could be moved to pity by the calamity then overtaking Greece.

For in earlier times they had sometimes stumbled and gone wrong in general matters, sometimes through disputes over power and sometimes through betrayal by monarchs;

but in the present period they suffered a acknowledged disaster because of the foolishness of those in charge and because of their own ignorance.

The Thebans abandoned the city en masse and left it completely deserted; among them Pytheas, after retreating to the Peloponnese with his wife and children, was driven from the country.

The enemy response seemed strange to him.

But I think, as the proverb says, that empty men make empty calculations; naturally, therefore, such people find what is obvious to be strange.

And he was thinking about returning home,

as though someone who was ignorant of swimming and was about to throw himself into the sea would not be thinking first about whether to jump, but after jumping would then consider how to get back to land.

## Book 38 Chapter 17

As soon as Diaeus was present in Corinth and had been made strategus by the many, the men around Andronidas arrived from Caecilius.

Beforehand, a rumor had been spread against them that they were in agreement with the enemy; and he put them before the crowd, so that they were seized with every outrage and dragged away in chains.

Philon the Thessalian also arrived, making many friendly proposals to the Achaeans.

On hearing these, some of the men from the country cooperated, among whom was Stratius, now an old man, who, entangled in the matter and urging them on, begged Diaeus to be persuaded by what Caecilius was proposing.

Those who sat in council paid no attention to what Philon said.

For they did not think that their safety was a common matter, but believed that Philon was speaking because he privately placed his own interest and security above everything else. So they decided to think only of the immediate situation, even though they were all equally in the wrong.

For though they clearly knew what they had done, they were in no way able to believe that they might obtain any mercy from the Romans.

And they never even considered for a moment that they ought to suffer nobly whatever was necessary for the sake of the state and the salvation of the many — as befits men who claim to be leaders of Greece.

But how, and from where, was such a spirit to arise in the men already mentioned? The men in council were Diaeus and Damocritus, who had just gained influence because of the present confusion, and with them Alcamenes, Theodectes, and Archicrates.

What sort of men they were, what each one's character and way of life and disposition was, has been explained at greater length elsewhere.

## Book 38 Chapter 18

So when the council was in such a state, the outcome followed what those men had expected.

At once they imprisoned not only the men around Andronidas and Lagon, but also the subordinate general Sosicrates, accusing him of being the instigator of the plot and of agreeing to send to Caecilius, and in general of being responsible for all the evils.

The next day, after appointing judges, they condemned Sosicrates to death and, binding and torturing him, kept at it until they destroyed the man, though he said none of what they expected.

Lagon and Andronidas and Archippus were released, because the crowd had by then gathered in protest over the outrage done to Sosicrates and because Diaeus had taken a talent from Andronidas and forty minas from Archippus.

For even when he was, as the saying goes, in the ring, the man mentioned above could not stop his shamelessness and lawbreaking in this respect.

He had done something similar a short time before to Philinus of Corinth: he had brought a charge that Philinus was sending reports to Menalcidas and favored the Romans, and after flogging and torturing Philinus and his sons together, he did not stop until he had destroyed the young men and Philinus himself.

When such folly and such confusion were taking place among everyone — something one could hardly find even among barbarians — it is natural to ask how they did not all perish utterly.

For I would say that a crafty and resourceful fortune seems to have set itself against the folly and madness of the leaders; and, being pushed aside in every way by the ignorance of those in authority but wishing to save the Achaeans in every possible way, it came at the end like a good wrestler.

This was what allowed the Greeks to be brought down quickly and defeated easily; and that is what happened.

For this reason it came about that neither the Roman anger and fury were kindled still further, nor did the forces from Libya arrive, nor did those in authority — such men as I have described — get the upper hand and display their impiety toward their own people.

For what they would naturally have done against their own people, if they had seized any opening or advantage, is obvious from what has already been said.

And everyone at the time had this saying constantly on their lips: "If we had not been ruined quickly, we would not have been saved."

## Book 38 Chapter 19

When he had come through the wall and found the sea between them to be not very deep, while the Carthaginians were defending themselves from the citadel, and when Polybius advised him to scatter iron caltrops or drive in sharpened stakes so that the enemy could not cross and fight from the embankments, he said it would be ridiculous, after taking the walls and being inside the city, to proceed in such a way as not to fight the enemy.

## Book 38 Chapter 20

When Hasdrubal, the Carthaginian general, came as a suppliant and fell at Scipio's knees, the general looked at those around him and said, "Do you see, gentlemen, how Fortune is good at setting before people an example of the reckless?"

"This is Hasdrubal, the same man who only recently rejected many humane offers made by us, saying that his country and its fire were the finest funeral pyre, and who now comes here with wreaths on his head, begging us for his life and putting all his hopes in us.

Who would not, seeing this before his eyes, take it to heart that an overbearing man must never say or do anything proud?"

Then some of the deserters came forward to the edge of the roof and begged the men in front to hold back a little;

but when the general ordered them to stop, they began to abuse Hasdrubal — some accusing him of perjury, saying that he had often sworn before the gods that he would not abandon them, others attacking his cowardice and, in general, the baseness of his spirit;

and they did this with rough and hostile mockery and abuse.

At that moment, seeing Hasdrubal seated with the general, his wife came forward from among the deserters, dressed freely and modestly, holding the children by the hand on either side and carrying their clothing with her.

At first she addressed Hasdrubal by name, but when he remained silent and looked down at the ground, she called on the gods and thanked the general greatly, because not only she herself but also the children were being saved from his fate;

then, after a short pause, she asked Hasdrubal how, after saying that she herself would obtain no private salvation from the general by deserting, he could so shamelessly abandon affairs and the citizens who had trusted him and go over to the enemy,

and how, after that, he dared now to sit here crowned, while he had many times said that the day would never come when he would see Hasdrubal alive and his fatherland burning at the same time; and yet ...

[...] the rest of the passage is badly broken in the witness, but it clearly continues with reproaches about his shameful conduct and the children, and with the crowd's sympathy for the disaster and for Fortune's reversal.

## Book 38 Chapter 21

[...] The text here is badly broken in the witness, but the sense is that Polybius is completing Scipio's remark about the danger of overconfidence and the importance of good judgment.

No clearer or more practical lesson than this is easy to state.

For to take account, in the midst of great achievements and the calamities of one's enemies, of one's own affairs and of the opposite condition, and in general to keep the instability of Fortune before one's eyes amid success, is the mark of a great and complete man, and one worthy of remembrance. —

## Book 38 Chapter 22

When Scipio saw the city ... then utterly ending in complete destruction, it is said that he burst into tears and was plainly seen weeping over his enemies;

and for a long time he stood absorbed in thought, realizing that cities, nations, and all sovereignties must change, just as human beings do. So it happened to Ilium, once a fortunate city; so too to the empire of the Assyrians, Medes, and Persians, and to the Macedonian power that had lately shone so brightly — whether he said this willingly, or whether the verse came to his mind unbidden:

"The day shall come when sacred Ilium shall perish,

and Priam, and Priam's people of the spear-loving race."

Polybius asked him this boldly, for he had been his tutor;

and they say that, whatever he intended by the remark, he did not mention his own fatherland openly, though he was surely thinking of the fate of human affairs and was afraid for it. Polybius himself records these things as he heard them.
