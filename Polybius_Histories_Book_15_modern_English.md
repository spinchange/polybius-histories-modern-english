# Polybius — The Histories, Book XV

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 15 Chapter 1: Speech of Roman Envoys At Carthage

I. Affairs in Italy and Africa

When the Carthaginians seized the Roman transport ships, along with a vast quantity of supplies, Scipio was deeply angered, not only because those provisions had been taken from his own side, but because the enemy had been furnished with an abundant store of necessities.

He was even more indignant because the Carthaginians had broken their oaths and their treaty, and had started the war again from the beginning.

So he immediately chose Lucius Sergius, Lucius Baebius, and Lucius Fabius as envoys and sent them to speak to the Carthaginians about what had happened, while also announcing that the Roman people had ratified the treaty.

For letters had just reached Scipio making this clear.

When the envoys arrived at Carthage, they were brought first before the senate and then before the people, and in both settings they spoke openly about the situation.

They first reminded them that the Carthaginian ambassadors, when they had come to Tunes and entered the Roman council, had not merely appealed to the gods and kissed the ground, as men commonly do,

but had actually thrown themselves flat on the earth in a shameful way and kissed the feet of those sitting in council. After that, standing up again, they had accused themselves, admitting that they themselves had broken the treaties first made between Romans and Carthaginians.

Therefore, they said, they were not unaware that any punishment the Romans chose to inflict would be deserved; yet, for the sake of the changes of human fortune, they begged not to suffer anything beyond remedy, since their own folly would provide proof of Roman nobility and generosity.

Remembering all this, the envoys said, their general and all who had been present in that council were astonished that the Carthaginians could forget what they themselves had said then, and now dare to violate their oaths and treaty.

This, they said, could only mean that they were trusting in Hannibal and the forces with him, and in that they were badly mistaken.

For everyone knew perfectly well that for two years already he and his army had been driven from all Italy and shut up near the Lacinian promontory, almost under siege, and had now barely escaped with their lives.

And even if he had come as a conqueror, they said, and even if he were about to fight men who had already beaten the Carthaginians in two successive battles, they still ought to hold uncertain hopes about the future, and think not only about victory but also about defeat.

If that happened, what gods would they call upon?

With what words would they try to draw pity from the victors for their disasters? By their faithlessness and folly, they would cut themselves off from every hope, both from gods and from men.

## Book 15 Chapter 2: Treacherous Attempt on the Lives of the Roman Envoys

After speaking in this way, the envoys withdrew.

Among the Carthaginians only a few were willing to keep the agreement. Most of those active in politics and in the senate resented the terms of the treaty, were offended by the frankness of the envoys, and were unwilling to give back the captured ships and the provisions taken from them.

What chiefly sustained them was not a small hope but a great one: they believed they could still win by means of Hannibal.

So the general vote was to send the envoys away without any answer. But the political leaders who were determined, by any means at all, to throw the war back into confusion met together and devised the following treachery.

They said that proper care should be taken for the envoys, so that they might return safely to their own camp.

They therefore at once prepared two triremes to escort them, but also sent instructions to Hasdrubal the admiral to keep ships ready not far from the Roman camp, so that once the escort vessels had left the Romans, he could swoop down and sink them.

For the Carthaginian fleet was lying off the coast near Utica.

After arranging this with Hasdrubal, they sent the Romans off and instructed the officers of the triremes to abandon them and sail away as soon as they had passed the river Macara.

From that point the Roman camp could already be seen.

So, once they had gone beyond the river as agreed, the escorting crews saluted the Romans and turned back.

Lucius and his companions suspected nothing serious. They were merely annoyed, thinking the escorts had left them early out of carelessness or disrespect.

But as soon as they were sailing alone, three Carthaginian triremes came darting out from ambush and attacked. They could not ram and disable the Roman quinquereme, because it kept backing water and avoiding the blow, nor could they board it, because the men defended themselves bravely.

Still, by coming alongside and fighting all around the ship, they wounded the marines and killed many of them.

At last the Romans saw men from their own camp, who had been foraging along the coast, rushing down to help from the shore, and so they ran their ship aground.

Most of those aboard were killed, but the envoys themselves were saved in a most unexpected way.

## Book 15 Chapter 3: Last Year of the Second Punic War

These events gave the war a new beginning, harsher and more bitter than before.

The Romans, believing that the treaty had been treacherously violated, were fiercely determined to overcome the Carthaginians; and the Carthaginians, conscious of what they had done, were ready for anything rather than fall into enemy hands.

With both sides so disposed, it was obvious that the present crisis would have to be settled by battle.

As a result, not only everyone in Italy and Libya but also those in Iberia, Sicily, and Sardinia were left hanging in suspense, anxiously waiting to see what would happen.

At this time Hannibal, being short of cavalry, sent messages to a Numidian named Tychaeus, a friend of Syphax who was said to command some of the finest mounted troops in Libya.

He urged him to come to his aid and seize the opportunity, since, as he clearly knew, if the Carthaginians won he would be able to preserve his own power, but if the Romans prevailed he would put even his life in danger because of Massanissa’s ambition.

Tychaeus was persuaded by this appeal and came to Hannibal with two thousand cavalry.

## Book 15 Chapter 4: Scipio Sends the Envoys Home

After securing his naval force and leaving Baebius as his lieutenant, Scipio himself moved through the cities.

This time he no longer accepted into protection those who willingly surrendered themselves, but took the cities by force, sold their inhabitants into slavery, and made his anger visible because the Carthaginians had broken faith.

He kept sending messages to Massanissa, making clear how the Phoenicians had violated the truce and urging him to gather as large a force as possible and join him quickly.

For Massanissa, as was said earlier, had set out as soon as the treaty was made, taking his own troops along with ten Roman standards of cavalry and infantry and certain envoys from Scipio, in order not only to recover his ancestral kingdom but, with Roman help, to add Syphax’s realm as well. And this in fact came to pass.

It happened at the same time that the envoys sent from Rome sailed into the Roman naval camp.

Baebius immediately sent the Roman envoys on to Scipio, but kept the Carthaginian envoys under guard. They were thoroughly dispirited and thought themselves in extreme danger.

For when they learned of the outrage their countrymen had committed against the Roman ambassadors, they expected that the punishment for it would fall on them.

But Scipio, when he heard from the men just arrived that both senate and people at Rome had warmly approved the treaty he had made with the Carthaginians and were ready to grant all he requested, was greatly pleased.

He then ordered Baebius to send the Carthaginian envoys home with every mark of humanity, a decision that seems to me both admirable and prudent.

For knowing how highly his own country valued good faith toward envoys, he considered not so much what the Carthaginians deserved to suffer as what the Romans ought to do.

So, restraining his own anger and the bitterness caused by what had happened, he tried, as the proverb says, to preserve the fine traditions handed down by his fathers.

In this way he conquered all the Carthaginians in spirit, and Hannibal himself as well, by setting Roman nobility above their folly.

## Book 15 Chapter 5: Both Sides Prepare For Battle

When the Carthaginians saw their cities being sacked, they sent to Hannibal, begging him not to delay but to come to close quarters with the enemy and decide the matter by battle.

He answered the messengers that they should concern themselves with other matters and leave this to him, since he himself would judge the right moment.

A few days later he broke camp from the region near Hadrumetum, advanced, and encamped near Zama, a city about five days’ journey west of Carthage. From there he sent out three spies,

wanting to learn where the Romans were encamped and how their general had arranged his camp.

When these spies were brought before Scipio, he was so far from punishing them, as others normally would, that he assigned them a tribune and ordered him to show them everything in the camp plainly and in full.

When that had been done, he asked the men whether the officer had shown them everything carefully.

When they said he had, Scipio gave them supplies and an escort and sent them away with orders to report accurately to Hannibal all they had seen.

When they returned, Hannibal was so struck by the man’s greatness of spirit and daring that he felt a strong desire to meet Scipio in person.

So he sent a herald, saying that he wished to confer with him about the whole situation.

When Scipio heard the herald’s message, he agreed and said he would send word himself, naming the place and time, when he was ready to meet.

The herald, after hearing this, returned to Hannibal’s camp.

The next day Massanissa arrived with about six thousand infantry and four thousand cavalry.

Scipio received him warmly and congratulated him on having made all those who had formerly obeyed Syphax subject to his power.

Then he broke camp and, after reaching the city of Naragara, encamped there, having chosen a place well suited in other respects and with a water supply close at hand.

## Book 15 Chapter 6: Meeting of Hannibal and Scipio

From there Scipio sent to the Carthaginian general, saying that he was ready to meet and speak with him.

When Hannibal heard this, he broke camp and moved nearer, until he was no more than thirty stades away, and pitched his camp by a hill that seemed suitable enough for the occasion, except that the water supply was a little too distant, so that the soldiers suffered considerable hardship over it.

On the following day both commanders advanced from their camps with a small body of cavalry. Then, leaving their escorts behind, they came together in the space between, each accompanied only by an interpreter.

Hannibal greeted him first and began by saying that he wished the Romans had never desired any possession outside Italy, nor the Carthaginians any outside Libya.

For these, he said, were the finest dominions for each people, and in a sense seemed to have been marked out by nature itself.

But since they had first gone to war over Sicily, then again over Iberia, and at last, though fortune had already given them warning, had advanced so far that one side had risked the very soil of its fatherland while the other was even now doing so,

nothing remained but, if possible, by their own efforts to placate the gods and put an end to the rivalry now before them.

For his own part, he said, experience had taught him that Fortune is quick to change and can tilt great matters either way on the slightest occasion, handling human beings almost like little children.

## Book 15 Chapter 7: Conclusion of Hannibal's Speech

And yet, Hannibal said, it was chiefly for Scipio himself that he felt concern, because Scipio was still very young, because everything in Iberia and Libya had gone according to his wishes, and because he had not yet experienced the ebb of Fortune. Hannibal feared that for these reasons Scipio might not be persuaded by words that were nevertheless true.

But, he said, consider the reality from one example, not from some story of long ago, but from their own generation.

I am that Hannibal who, after the battle of Cannae, became master of almost all Italy and, after some time, came to Rome itself and encamped within forty stades of it, deliberating how I should deal with you and the soil of your fatherland.

And now I am here in Libya, meeting with you, a Roman, to discuss the safety of myself and the Carthaginians. Looking at this, do not be proud,

but deliberate like a man, with full awareness of the human condition. That means always choosing the greatest good and the least evil.

For what sensible man would willingly rush into the danger now set before you? If you win, you will add little to your own glory or to that of your country; but if you lose, you will by your own act wipe away all the noble and splendid things you have achieved before now.

So what is the purpose of all I am saying?

That everything over which we previously disputed should belong to the Romans — Sicily, Sardinia, and the territories in Iberia — and that the Carthaginians should never again wage war against Rome over these matters; and likewise that all the other islands lying between Italy and Libya should belong to Rome.

For I am convinced that these terms would be the safest for the future of Carthage, and the most honorable for you and for all the Romans.

## Book 15 Chapter 8: Scipio's reply

Such was Hannibal’s speech. Scipio answered that in neither the war over Sicily nor that over Iberia had the Romans been the aggressors; plainly the Carthaginians had been, and no one knew that better than Hannibal himself.

The gods themselves, he said, had borne witness to this by granting victory not to those who first laid unjust hands on others, but to those who defended themselves.

He too, no less than anyone else, kept before his eyes the uncertainties of Fortune and tried, so far as he could, to reckon within the limits of human life.

But if Hannibal had withdrawn from Italy before the Romans crossed into Libya and had then proposed these terms of settlement, Scipio said, he did not think Hannibal would have been disappointed in his hopes.

As it was, Hannibal had left Italy against his will, while the Romans had crossed into Libya and taken control of the open country; so the position of affairs had plainly changed greatly.

And above all, to what point had matters now come?

After Hannibal’s countrymen had been defeated and had begged for terms, written agreements had been drawn up, in which, besides the concessions Hannibal was now offering, the Carthaginians were to return prisoners without ransom, surrender their warships, pay five thousand talents, and give hostages for the performance of these terms.

These were the terms mutually agreed. Both sides had sent embassies to senate and people alike: the Romans declaring that they approved the treaty, the Carthaginians begging that it be granted to them.

The senate had agreed, and the people had ratified it. Yet after obtaining what they asked for, the Carthaginians broke the agreement and violated the truce toward Rome.

What, then, was left for him to do?

Should he, Scipio said, put himself in Hannibal’s place and remove the harshest of the imposed terms, so that by receiving a reward for lawlessness the Carthaginians might learn in future to break faith even with benefactors? Or was he to suppose that, once granted what they demanded, they would owe Rome gratitude?

Why, after receiving as suppliants exactly what they had asked for, simply because they caught some slight hope through Hannibal, they had immediately treated the Romans as enemies and opponents.

In such circumstances, he said, if even harsher terms were imposed, it might still be possible to refer the settlement back to the Roman people; but any proposal to remove what had already been laid down was not even fit for discussion.

So this was his conclusion: the Carthaginians must place themselves and their fatherland at Roman discretion, or else win by fighting.

## Book 15 Chapter 9: Dispositions For the Battle of Zama

After this exchange Hannibal and Scipio parted, their conference having ended without agreement.

At first light the next day both commanders led out their armies and began the contest — for the Carthaginians it was a fight for their own survival and for power in Libya, for the Romans a struggle for universal empire and supremacy.

And who, understanding the situation, would not be moved by the story?

One could hardly have found armies more fit for war, or commanders more successful and more practiced in every labor of battle, nor could Fortune have set before combatants greater prizes than those now at stake.

For the victors in this battle were destined to become masters not only of Libya or of Europe, but of all the other parts of the inhabited world that now lie within the scope of history. And that indeed came to pass not long afterward.

Scipio drew up his own forces in the following way.

First he posted the hastati, with intervals between the maniples; behind them the principes, placing their companies not opposite the gaps in the front line, as Roman custom usually required, but directly behind the first line and at some distance, because of the great number of elephants in the enemy army. Last of all he stationed the triarii.

On the wings he placed Gaius Laelius on the left with the Italian cavalry, and Massanissa on the right with all the Numidians under his command.

The gaps between the front maniples he filled with units of javelin-men, ordering them to begin the fighting.

If they were driven back by the elephants’ charge, some were to retreat through the straight lanes between the maniples to the rear of the whole force, while any who were caught up at close quarters were to slip aside into the lateral spaces by the standards.

## Book 15 Chapter 10: Scipio's Speech to his Men

After making these arrangements, Scipio moved along the line encouraging the troops, speaking briefly but in a way perfectly suited to the danger before them.

He called on them to remember their earlier struggles, to show themselves brave men worthy of themselves and of their country, and to keep before their eyes that, if they defeated the enemy, they would not only secure complete possession of affairs in Libya, but also win for themselves and for Rome the undisputed leadership and dominion of the rest of the world.

But if the danger turned out otherwise, then those who died nobly in battle would have the finest memorial, a death for their fatherland, while those who escaped by flight would live the rest of their days in the deepest shame and misery.

For there was no place in Libya that could provide safety for fugitives, and anyone who judged matters rightly could already see what would happen if they fell into Carthaginian hands. “May none of you,” he said, “learn that by experience.”

Since Fortune had set before them the greatest of prizes, whichever way the battle went, would they not be the basest and most senseless of men if, out of mere love of life, they let go the noblest of goods and deliberately chose the worst of evils?

So he urged them, with only two things set before them — victory or death — to go straight at the enemy.

For men who hold such a resolve, he said, necessarily defeat those drawn up against them, once they go into battle with no hope left except in fighting.

## Book 15 Chapter 11: Hannibal's Preparations and Speech

Such was Scipio’s exhortation. Hannibal, for his part, placed the elephants — more than eighty of them — in front of his entire army; after them he stationed the mercenaries, about twelve thousand in number, consisting of Ligurians, Celts, Balearians, and Mauretanians.

Behind these he drew up the native Libyans and Carthaginians, and behind all the rest, at a distance of more than a stade from the troops in front, the men who had come with him from Italy.

He secured his wings with cavalry, placing the allied Numidians on the left and the Carthaginian horse on the right.

He ordered each group’s officers to encourage their own men, telling them to rest their hopes of victory on Hannibal himself and on the forces he had brought with him.

The Carthaginian officers he instructed to set plainly before their men what would happen to their children and wives if the battle turned out badly. So they did as they had been told.

Hannibal himself rode along the men who had come with him and urged them at greater length to remember the seventeen years of comradeship they had shared, and to remember how many contests with the Romans they had fought.

In all those struggles, he said, they had remained undefeated and had never even left the Romans any hope of victory.

Above all, setting aside the lesser battles and their countless advantages, they must keep before their eyes the battle by the river Trebia against the father of the present Roman commander, then the battle in Etruria against Flaminius, and after that the battle at Cannae against Aemilius,

battles with which the present danger could not compare, whether in the number of enemy troops or in their quality.

As he said this, he told them to look up and inspect the enemy ranks. They would see that these men were not only fewer than those they had fought before, but not even a small fraction of them, and in fighting quality they did not deserve comparison.

Those earlier Roman armies, he said, had fought them fresh and undefeated, whereas these were either the sons or the surviving remnants of the men beaten in Italy and made to flee before him again and again.

Therefore they must not destroy either their own reputation or the fame of their commander, but by fighting bravely confirm the report handed down about them, that they were unconquered.

Such, or words very like these, were the exhortations made on both sides.

## Book 15 Chapter 12: The Battle of Zama Begins

Once everything on both sides was ready for battle, and the Numidian cavalry had already been skirmishing with one another for some time, Hannibal gave the order for the elephant-drivers to charge the enemy.

But when the trumpets and horns sounded all around them, some of the beasts were thrown into confusion and wheeled about against the Numidian allies supporting the Carthaginians; and by this Massanissa quickly stripped the Carthaginian left wing of its mounted support.

The rest of the elephants collided with the Roman javelin-men in the space between the battle lines and both inflicted and suffered heavy damage.

At last, terrified, some of them ran off down the lanes between the Roman formations, the Romans receiving them safely, just as their general had planned, while others, veering to the right and being showered with missiles from the cavalry, were finally driven out beyond the ground between the camps.

At that moment Laelius too, taking advantage of the confusion caused by the elephants, charged and forced the Carthaginian cavalry into headlong flight.

He pursued the fugitives vigorously, and Massanissa did the same.

Meanwhile the infantry lines on both sides advanced upon one another at a measured pace and with proud confidence — all except the troops who had come from Italy with Hannibal, who held their original position.

When the opposing lines came close, the Romans raised their customary battle cry, beat their swords against their shields, and charged.

But the Carthaginian mercenaries raised a confused and discordant clamor,

for, as the poet says, “their cry was not one and the same, nor was their speech one,”

since they were men of many peoples and many tongues, just as I listed a moment ago.

## Book 15 Chapter 13: Cowardice of the Carthaginians

Now that the struggle had become a hand-to-hand fight, man against man, the mercenaries at first had the advantage in skill and boldness and wounded many Romans.

But the Romans, trusting in the firmness of their formation and in their equipment, kept pressing steadily forward.

At the same time, the Romans were supported and encouraged by the troops behind them, whereas the Carthaginians neither came up to aid their mercenaries nor closed in behind them, but lost heart.

So in the end the foreign troops gave way, and thinking they had been openly abandoned by their own side, they turned on the men posted behind them as they retreated and began killing them.

This forced many Carthaginians to die bravely against their will, since, while being slaughtered by the mercenaries, they had to fight at once against their own men and against the Romans.

Fighting in this wild and disordered fashion, they killed quite a number on both sides, friend and enemy alike.

By charging in this way they threw the maniples of the hastati into confusion. But when the officers of the principes saw what had happened, they halted and brought up their own line.

Most of the mercenaries and Carthaginians were then cut down, some by one another and some by the hastati themselves.

As for those who survived and fled, Hannibal did not allow them to mix with his reserve. He ordered his officers to present their weapons and prevent the fugitives from entering.

So these men were forced to withdraw toward the wings and the open spaces there.

## Book 15 Chapter 14: Victory of Scipio and Flight of Hannibal

But the ground between the armies that still remained in position was full of blood, slaughter, and corpses, and this obstacle created a serious difficulty for the Roman commander during the rout.

For the slippery footing caused by the dead, lying in heaps and soaked with blood, and the confusion of weapons scattered everywhere among the bodies were bound to make orderly advance extremely difficult.

Even so, Scipio carried the wounded to the rear of the line, recalled the pursuing hastati by trumpet signal, and stationed them directly in front of the enemy center.

Then he closed up the principes and triarii on each wing and ordered them to advance through the corpses.

When they had come through and drawn level with the hastati, the lines clashed with the greatest fury and eagerness.

Since both sides were nearly equal in numbers, spirit, courage, and equipment, the battle remained undecided for a long time, men dying where they stood because of sheer determination.

At last Massanissa and Laelius, wheeling back from their pursuit of the cavalry, arrived at exactly the right moment.

When they fell on Hannibal’s troops from the rear, most of the Carthaginians were cut down in their ranks, and of those who turned to flee only a few escaped entirely, since the cavalry were upon them and the ground was flat.

More than fifteen hundred Romans fell, while over twenty thousand Carthaginians were killed, and not many fewer were taken prisoner.

## Book 15 Chapter 15: Hannibal Did All He Could

Such was the outcome of the battle that decided everything and, under the commanders already mentioned, assigned empire to the Romans.

After the battle Scipio followed up the pursuit, plundered the Carthaginian camp, and then returned again to his own.

Hannibal, meanwhile, withdrew continuously with a few horsemen and reached Hadrumetum safely, having done in the battle everything possible that a good general, and one with experience in many great affairs, ought to do.

First of all, by entering into conference he tried to settle the crisis by negotiation.

That is what one does who understands success, yet does not trust Fortune, and who foresees the unexpected turns to which battles may come.

Then, when he had accepted battle, he handled matters in such a way that, against Romans armed in similar fashion, no better plan of engagement could have been devised than the one Hannibal then arranged.

For since the Roman formation is hard to break, and since in general and in detail a Roman force can fight in every direction out of a single deployment, with the nearest standards always wheeling to the point of danger,

and since their equipment gives both protection and confidence, by reason of the great shield and the sword’s power to endure heavy blows, they are for these reasons difficult opponents and hard to master.

## Book 15 Chapter 16: Hannibal's Tactics Sound

Yet despite all this, Hannibal adapted himself to each of these difficulties as well as circumstances allowed, leaving nothing better to be desired.

His numerous elephants he collected and threw forward for the express purpose of throwing the enemy lines into confusion and breaking their order.

He placed the mercenaries in front and the Carthaginians behind them so that the enemy’s bodies might first be worn down by exertion, the edge of their weapons dulled by the number of men they killed, and the Carthaginians, standing in the middle, compelled to remain and fight, as the poet says, “even the unwilling man must battle under necessity.”

The most warlike and steadfast of his troops he posted at a distance, so that, seeing what happened from afar and preserving their bodies and spirits fresh, they might employ their courage at the proper moment.

And if, after doing everything possible to win, a man previously undefeated still failed, he deserves pardon.

For sometimes chance itself thwarts the designs of brave men, and sometimes, as the proverb says, a good man meets another stronger than himself. One might well say that this is what happened to Hannibal then.

## Book 15 Chapter 17: Scipio Unmoved by Carthaginian Laments

Displays of emotion that go beyond what is customary among a people, when they appear to spring genuinely from overwhelming disaster, call forth pity in those who see and hear them; the strangeness of the spectacle moves us all in some degree.

But when such behavior seems adopted theatrically and for effect, it produces not pity but anger and disgust. That was what happened in the case of the Carthaginian envoys.

Scipio began briefly by saying that for their own sake the Romans owed them no kindness at all, since they themselves admitted that they had first brought war upon Rome by enslaving Saguntum in violation of treaty, and had only lately broken sworn oaths and written agreements once again.

Any mildness now shown them, he said, was for the Romans’ own sake, out of regard for Fortune and for the human condition, and because they had decided to behave with magnanimity.

And this, he said, would become clear to the envoys too if they judged the present position rightly. They ought not to count it terrible if they were ordered to suffer, to do, or to surrender certain things; rather,

they should regard it as remarkable if any act of humanity at all were granted them, since Fortune, by reason of their own injustice, had taken away any claim they had to pity or indulgence and had made them subject to their enemies.

After saying this, he set out what leniencies were being granted and, in turn, what penalties they would have to endure.

## Book 15 Chapter 18: Terms Made After the Battle of Zama

These were the main points of the proposals. The Carthaginians were to keep the cities in Libya that they had possessed before making war on Rome, as well as the land they had formerly held, together with their livestock, slaves, and other property.

From that day onward the Carthaginians were to remain free from molestation, to use their own customs and laws, and to live without a garrison.

These were the favorable terms. The opposite clauses were that the Carthaginians were to make full restitution to the Romans for all injuries committed during the armistice, to return all prisoners and deserters from every past period, and to hand over all warships except ten triremes, along with all their elephants.

They were to wage war on no people outside Libya at all, and on none within Libya without Roman consent.

They were to restore to King Massanissa every house, tract of land, city, and whatever else had belonged either to him or to his ancestors within the boundaries that would be assigned to him.

They were to furnish the Roman army with provisions for three months and to pay it until a reply came from Rome regarding the treaty.

The Carthaginians were to pay ten thousand talents of silver in fifty years, paying two hundred Euboic talents each year.

And as security for good faith they were to give one hundred hostages selected by the Roman general from among the young men, none younger than fourteen and none older than thirty.

## Book 15 Chapter 19: Hannibal Persuades Carthage to Accept These Terms

Such was what the Roman commander said to the envoys. After hearing it, they hurried home and explained everything to those in Carthage.

At that point, it is said, when one of the senators was about to speak against accepting the terms and had just begun, Hannibal stepped forward and pulled the man down from the platform.

When the rest were angered because he had done something contrary to established custom, Hannibal rose again and said that he admitted he did not understand such usages, but they ought to excuse him if he acted against their habits, since he had left his country when he was only nine years old and had returned to it only after more than forty-five years.

Therefore, he said, they should not focus on whether he had failed in matters of custom, but far more on whether he truly felt for the condition of his fatherland. That, he said, was what had driven him into this breach of decorum.

For it seemed to him astonishing, indeed utterly irrational, if any man who was a Carthaginian and knew what policies they had pursued against Rome, both collectively for the city and individually for themselves, did not now bow before Fortune in gratitude when, having become subject to Rome, they were receiving such mild terms.

Only a few days before, if anyone had asked what they expected their country to suffer if the Romans prevailed, none of them could even have answered, so great and excessive were the evils that seemed likely to come upon them.

So now, he said, they should not even bring the matter into debate, but unanimously accept the proposed terms, offer sacrifice to the gods, and pray with one voice that the Roman people would confirm them.

Since he was seen to be advising wisely and in a way suited to the crisis, it was decided to make peace on the stated terms.

The council immediately sent envoys to agree formally to them.

## Book 15 Chapter 20: League Against Ptolemy Epiphanes

II. Affairs of Macedonia and Greece

Who would not marvel at this: while Ptolemy himself was alive and did not need their help, these kings were ready to assist him,

but when he died and left behind a mere infant, whose kingdom both of them were naturally bound to preserve, then they invited one another to divide the child’s dominion between themselves and remove the heir he had left.

And they did not, like tyrants, put forward even some small pretext to veil their shame; rather they acted openly and brutally, in a way so savage that it calls to mind the life attributed to fish, among whom the destruction of the smaller becomes food and sustenance for the larger, though they are of the same kind.

Who, looking into this treaty as into a mirror, would not think himself an eyewitness of these kings’ impiety toward the gods, their cruelty toward human beings, and their excessive greed?

And yet who would not reasonably cease blaming Fortune for the conduct of human affairs in this case, seeing that she afterward inflicted on them the punishment they deserved and offered to later generations the finest example of correction in the chastisement of those kings?

For while they were breaking faith with each other and carving up the child’s kingdom, Fortune brought the Romans upon them, and what those men had unlawfully planned against their neighbor she justly and fittingly brought about against them.

For both of them were soon defeated in war and not only prevented from seizing what belonged to another, but were themselves shut in under tribute and made to do Rome’s bidding.

In the end, within a very short time, Fortune restored Ptolemy’s kingdom, while the dynasties and successors of those two monarchs she either utterly overthrew and destroyed or else involved in almost the same disasters.

## Book 15 Chapter 21: Molpagoras of Cius

There was at Cius a man named Molpagoras, capable both in speech and in action, but in his political character a demagogue and a greedy schemer.

By flattering the crowd, handing over the prosperous citizens to the mob, putting some to death outright, driving others into exile, confiscating their property, and distributing it among the multitude, he quickly won for himself a kind of monarchic power.

So the people of Cius fell into disasters of this size not so much because of Fortune or the injustice of their neighbors as, for the greater part, because of their own folly and bad government.

For they were always promoting the worst men and punishing those who opposed them, so that they might divide one another’s property.

In this way they almost volunteered for their own ruin. Somehow all human beings plainly fall into such disasters and yet cannot stop being foolish, nor even learn the least measure of caution, unlike some irrational animals.

For if those creatures have once been harmed by bait or trap, or even see another in danger from one, you will not easily lure them again into anything of the same sort; they grow suspicious of the place itself and mistrust everything they see.

Human beings, by contrast, though they hear of cities utterly ruined by this policy and even see others suffering the same fate before their eyes, still rush mindlessly toward the bait whenever someone, with flattering words, sets before them the hope of repairing their own fortunes at the expense of others.

And they do so even though they know perfectly well that no one who swallows such bait has ever been saved, and that governments of this kind have universally brought destruction on all who adopted them.

## Book 15 Chapter 22: Philip Rouses the Enmity of the Greeks

When Philip had gained possession of the city, he was overjoyed, as if he had accomplished some noble and splendid deed, having zealously aided his brother-in-law, overawed all who resisted him, and gained an abundance of bodies and money by what he imagined was just warfare.

But he failed to see the opposite side of the matter, though it was plain enough: first, that he was helping his brother-in-law not when wronged, but when that man was treacherously attacking his neighbors in violation of faith;

second, that by unjustly surrounding a Greek city with the greatest misfortunes he was bound to confirm the reputation already spread abroad concerning his cruelty toward his friends, and by both actions would justly inherit throughout Greece a name for impiety;

third, that he had insulted the envoys from the cities already mentioned, who had come to rescue the people of Cius from the dangers around them, but after being put off and mocked by him day after day were forced to witness with their own eyes what they most wished not to see;

and beyond all this, that he had made the Rhodians so savage in their anger against him that they would no longer listen to a word in his favor.

## Book 15 Chapter 23: The Rhodians Object to Philip's Treatment of Cius

And in this matter Fortune plainly cooperated against Philip as well.

For while his ambassador was making his defense before the Rhodians in the theater, proclaiming Philip’s greatness of spirit and saying that, though he was in effect already master of Cius, he was granting this favor to the Rhodian people because he wished to refute the slanders of his opponents and make his own intentions clear to the city,

at that very moment a man who had just landed came to the prytaneum announcing the enslavement of the people of Cius and the cruelty Philip had shown in the affair.

So while the ambassador was still speaking, and the prytanis came forward and reported the news, the Rhodians could hardly believe it because the faithlessness was so extreme.

Thus Philip had betrayed not so much the people of Cius as himself, and had fallen into such ignorance — or rather such transgression — of what was fitting that he boasted and gloried in deeds for which he ought to have felt the deepest shame.

From that day onward the Rhodian people regarded Philip as an enemy and made their preparations accordingly.

By this same action he aroused similar hatred against himself among the Aetolians.

For he had only recently made peace with them and offered them the hand of friendship. No pretext for a breach had arisen; and the Aetolians, the people of Lysimachia,

the Chalcedonians, and the people of Cius had all been friends and allies. Yet shortly before this he had first detached Lysimachia from the Aetolian alliance and brought it over to himself, then treated Chalcedon in the same way, and finally enslaved the people of Cius, even though an Aetolian officer was residing there and directing public affairs.

Prusias, because his design had been accomplished, was delighted on the one hand; but because another man carried off the real prize while he himself inherited only the bare, desolate site of a city, he was displeased and yet able to do nothing.

## Book 15 Chapter 24: Philip at Thasos

Philip, on his voyage back, committed one breach of faith after another, and among them he put in at midday to the city of the Thasians and enslaved it, though it was friendly to him.

The Thasians told Metrodorus, Philip’s general, that they would surrender the city if he would preserve them free from garrison, tribute, and quartering of troops, and allow them to use their own laws.

The king, he said, agreed that the Thasians should remain free from garrison, free from tribute, free from billeting, and should use their own laws. When everyone signaled approval of these terms with a loud shout, they admitted Philip into the city.

Perhaps all kings, at the beginning of their reigns, set the name of liberty before everyone and call those who share their hopes friends and allies; but once they come to real action, they immediately treat those who trusted them not like allies but like subjects.

So such people are deceived in the honorable position they expected, though they generally do not miss the immediate advantage they sought.

But when a king is attempting the greatest enterprises, embracing the whole inhabited world in his ambitions, and still has all his hopes intact, how can it seem anything but irrational and mad to proclaim his own faithlessness and instability at the very first and smallest opportunities that present themselves?

## Book 15 Chapter 24a: Egypt

III. Affairs of Egypt

Since in each year we recount the events that happened in the various parts of the inhabited world in their proper sequence, it is plain that in some cases the end must be presented before the beginning, whenever that section first falls into place in the division of the whole work and, in the progress of the narrative, the event containing the conclusion naturally comes before the one containing the beginning and first design.

## Book 15 Chapter 25: Ptolemy Epiphanes Succeeds To the Crown

Sosibius, the false guardian of Ptolemy, had the reputation of being a shrewd and long-enduring instrument of evil at court.

He had first arranged the murder of Lysimachus, son of Arsinoe daughter of Lysimachus and Ptolemy; next that of Magas, son of Ptolemy and Berenice daughter of Magas; third, that of Berenice, mother of Ptolemy Philopator; fourth, that of Cleomenes the Spartan; and fifth, that of Arsinoe daughter of Berenice.

Three or four days later, after building a platform in the largest colonnaded court of the palace, Agathocles and Sosibius summoned the bodyguards and household attendants, together with the officers of infantry and cavalry.

When they had assembled, Agathocles and Sosibius mounted the platform, announced the deaths of the king and queen, and proclaimed the customary mourning to the crowd.

After this they placed the diadem on the child’s head and proclaimed him king, and then read out a forged will in which the dead king had supposedly left Agathocles and Sosibius as guardians of the boy.

They urged the officers to support the child and preserve his rule. Then they brought in two silver urns, pretending that one contained the king’s bones and the other those of Arsinoe.

In reality one did contain the king’s remains, but the other was filled with spices. After these arrangements they at once proceeded with the funeral. At that moment the truth about Arsinoe became plain to everyone.

For once her death was established, people began to inquire into the manner of it. Since no other explanation was available, and the true rumor had already spread though still disputed, what had really happened now fixed itself in every mind. So a great disturbance arose among the crowd.

No one paid any attention to the king, but with regard to Arsinoe some recalled her orphaned condition, others the outrage and humiliation she had suffered throughout her life, and when to these was added the misfortune of her death, the city was filled with groaning, tears, and unceasing lamentation.

Yet to those who judged rightly, these things were less signs of affection for Arsinoe than of hatred toward Agathocles and his circle.

When Agathocles had placed the urns in the royal burial chambers and ordered mourning clothes laid aside, his first move was to issue the troops two months’ pay, convinced that the hatred of the masses could be dulled by appealing to their advantage. He next administered the customary oath taken at the proclamation of kings.

He also sent away Philammon, who had overseen the murder of Arsinoe, making him governor of the Libyan districts around Cyrene, and he placed the child in the hands of Oenanthe and Agathocleia.

After this he sent Pelops son of Pelops to Asia, to Antiochus the king, to urge him to preserve the friendship and not violate the treaty made with the child’s father; and he sent Ptolemy son of Sosibius to Philip both to settle the matter of an intermarriage alliance and to ask for help if Antiochus should attempt any more open breach of faith.

He also appointed Ptolemy son of Agesarchus as envoy to the Romans, not because he meant him to press the embassy in earnest, but so that, once he reached Greece and met friends and relatives there, he would stay away.

For his aim was to remove all prominent men from his path.

He also sent Scopas the Aetolian to Greece to recruit mercenaries, providing a large sum of gold for the advance payments.

He had two purposes in this. One was to use the men thus recruited in the war against Antiochus. The other was to send the existing mercenaries out to country forts and settlements, and by means of the newcomers refill and remake the household regiments, the palace guards, and likewise the rest of the city watch.

He thought that troops recruited and paid by himself would feel no sympathy with what had happened before, because they knew nothing of it, and that, having all their hopes both of safety and advancement resting in him, they would be ready assistants and collaborators in whatever he ordered.

All this happened before the negotiations with Philip, as I have said; but since those matters had to be taken first in the order of the narrative, it was necessary to handle these events in this way, by first describing the audiences, the diplomatic dealings, and the dispatch of the ambassadors.

Agathocles, then, once he had removed the most distinguished men and to a great extent checked the anger of the crowd by distributing pay, promptly returned to his former habits. He filled the places of his friends

by drawing from the attendants and other servants the most worthless and shameless men.

He himself spent most of day and night in drunkenness and the excesses that follow it, sparing neither mature women nor brides nor unmarried girls, and he did all this with the most offensive display.

So there was widespread and manifold resentment; yet no relief or help appeared, and instead outrage, arrogance, and carelessness were continually added to the burden.

Thus the old hatred among the people flared up again, and everyone renewed in memory the misfortunes that had already befallen the kingdom through these very people.

But since they had no respectable figure to stand in front and on whom they might lean while pressing their anger against Agathocles and Agathocleia, they stayed quiet, watching for one remaining hope in Tlepolemus and fixing their attention on him.

As long as the king lived, Tlepolemus attended to his own affairs. But when the king died, he quickly won over the troops and once more became commander in the region of Pelusium.

At first he reported what was being done in the interest of the king, believing that there would be some governing council to hold the guardianship of the child and oversight of the whole state.

But when he saw that the men fit to serve as guardians had been removed and that Agathocles was boldly seizing control of everything, he quickly changed his purpose. Fearing the danger that threatened him because of the enmity between them, he gathered troops around himself and sought money, so that he might not lie easy to the hand of any enemy.

At the same time he did not despair of gaining the guardianship of the child and the chief direction of affairs for himself, thinking that by his own judgment he was far more worthy than Agathocles in every respect, and hearing besides that both the troops under his command and those in Alexandria placed their hopes in him as the man who would put down Agathocles’ insolence.

With dispositions such as these on his side, the quarrel soon grew, since both factions helped to inflame it.

For Tlepolemus, eager to win over the generals, the taxiarchs, and the officers under them, carefully arranged drinking parties; and at these gatherings, partly from the flattery of his companions and partly from his own impulse — since he was young and the conversation was carried on over wine — he cast jibes at the family of Agathocles, at first riddling and obscure, then ambiguous, and at last open and filled with the bitterest abuse.

He mocked the writer of lampoons, the sambuca-girl, the lady’s maid, and that little page who had done and suffered everything at drinking parties when as a boy he poured wine for the king.

And because those present were always laughing and adding something to the mockery, the matter quickly reached Agathocles and his circle.

Once the hostility had become open, Agathocles at once began circulating slanders against Tlepolemus, claiming that he was alienating himself from the king and inviting Antiochus to seize control of affairs.

He found many plausible arguments for this, twisting some from actual events and inventing and dressing up others from the beginning.

He did this because he wished to provoke the crowd against Tlepolemus. But the result was the opposite. For since the people had long fixed their hopes on Tlepolemus, they were delighted to see the quarrel growing hot.

The beginning of the popular movement, however, arose from certain such causes as these. Nicon, a kinsman of Agathocles, had while the king was still alive been placed in command of the fleet...

## Book 15 Chapter 26: Another Murder Committed by Agathocles

Agathocles first assembled the Macedonians, and before them he entered with the king and Agathocleia.

At first he acted the part of a man unable to say what he wished because of the flood of tears that overcame him.

But after repeatedly wiping his face with his cloak and mastering the display, he lifted up the child and said, “Take this boy, whom his father, when dying, placed in this woman’s arms” — pointing to his sister — “and entrusted, Macedonians, to your keeping.

Her goodwill contributes only a slight advantage toward his safety; but his whole situation now lies with you and in your hands.

For Tlepolemus has long been clearly seen, by those who observe matters rightly, to aim at greater things than suit his station, and now he has fixed the very day and moment on which he means to take up the diadem.

Do not trust me about this,” he said, “but men who know the truth and are now present from the facts themselves.”

With that he brought in Critolaus, who declared that he had personally seen altars being prepared and sacrificial victims made ready among the populace for the proclamation of the diadem.

But when the Macedonians heard this, far from pitying him, they simply paid no attention to what he said. Muttering and whispering among themselves, they made such a confused uproar that Agathocles himself did not even understand how the assembly came to an end.

The same thing happened in his addresses to the other groups as well.

Meanwhile many people were sailing down from the upper camps, and some urged their kinsmen, others their friends, to help in the present crisis and not stand by while they were openly outraged by men so unworthy.

What especially stirred the masses to punish those in power was the knowledge that the coming struggle lay in their own hands, because they controlled all the supplies being brought into Alexandria.

## Book 15 Chapter 26a

Agathocles also killed Deinon son of Deinon, and this deed, as the proverb says, was the most just of all his injustices.

For when the letters concerning the murder of Arsinoe came into his hands, he had the chance to reveal the plot and save the kingdom, but instead he cooperated with Philammon and so became responsible for all the evils that followed. Later, after the murder had been carried out, he kept recalling it, pitying the victims and regretting that he had let such a moment pass; and when this became known to Agathocles, he at once met the punishment that suited him and lost his life.

## Book 15 Chapter 27: Growing Hatred of Agathocles

Something done by Agathocles and his own circle also helped to intensify the anger both of the people and of Tlepolemus.

They seized Danae, the mother-in-law of Tlepolemus, from the temple of Demeter, dragged her unveiled through the middle of the city, and threw her into prison, wishing to make their quarrel with Tlepolemus unmistakably public.

The populace, outraged by this, no longer kept their talk private or confined to secret circles. Some by night wrote on the walls in every quarter, while others by day gathered in groups and openly expressed their hatred toward those in power.

Agathocles and his circle, seeing what was happening and holding sinister expectations about their own future, sometimes planned flight; but because nothing had been prepared for that course, thanks to their own incompetence, they abandoned the idea.

At other times they drew up lists of conspirators and partners for a daring stroke, intending at once to cut down some enemies, arrest others, and after that seize tyrannical power.

While they were considering these plans, a report reached them against a certain Moeragenes, one of the bodyguards, that he was revealing everything to Tlepolemus and cooperating with him because of his connection with Adaeus, then commander at Bubastus.

Agathocles immediately ordered Nicostratus, the secretary in charge of correspondence, to arrest Moeragenes and examine him thoroughly, putting every form of torture before him.

So Moeragenes was at once arrested by Nicostratus and taken to a secluded part of the palace, where he was first questioned directly about the charges.

When he admitted nothing, he was stripped, and some were making ready the instruments of torture while others, with whips in hand, were taking off their cloaks.

At just that moment one of the attendants ran up to Nicostratus, whispered something in his ear, and hurried away again. Nicostratus followed close behind him,

saying nothing but repeatedly slapping his thigh as he went.

## Book 15 Chapter 28: Moeragenes Escapes

What happened to Moeragenes then was astonishing and beyond belief.

The floggers were standing there all but ready to bring down their whips, while others at his feet were preparing the instruments of compulsion.

But once Nicostratus had gone away, they all stood gaping and staring at one another, constantly expecting the man to come back.

As time passed, those standing by gradually slipped away one by one, until Moeragenes was left alone. Then, passing through the palace, he unexpectedly escaped half naked into a tent of the Macedonians near the court.

Finding them by chance at breakfast and gathered together, he told them what had happened to him and the strange way he had been saved.

At first they doubted him, but when they saw him naked they were forced to believe.

Taking advantage of this reversal, Moeragenes begged the Macedonians with tears to secure not only his own safety, but also that of the king and, above all, their own.

For, he said, destruction was plain to all unless they seized the moment when the hatred of the crowd was at its height and everyone stood ready to punish Agathocles.

And that moment, he said, was now in full strength.

## Book 15 Chapter 29: A Revolt Decided Upon By the Army

Moeragenes said that what was chiefly needed was only someone to take the lead. When the Macedonians heard this, they were stirred to anger, and in the end they were persuaded by him. They immediately went first to the Macedonian tents and then to those of the other soldiers.

All these camps lay together, facing one side of the city.

Since the impulse had long been ready in the minds of the crowd and needed only someone to call it forth and dare the first move, the affair, once begun, blazed up like fire.

For before four hours had passed, every class, military and civilian alike, had agreed on the attack.

And at this crisis chance too contributed greatly toward the outcome.

For Agathocles received both a letter and the report of spies: the letter, written by Tlepolemus to the troops, declared that he would arrive soon, and the spies reported that he was already there.

This drove Agathocles so completely out of his senses that he gave up both action and even deliberation about the danger confronting him, but at his usual hour went off to drinking and there carried through the gathering in his customary fashion.

His mother Oenanthe, in deep distress, went to the Thesmophorium, the sanctuary being open because of an annual sacrifice.

At first she knelt and made magical appeals to the goddesses; then she sat down by the altar and kept still.

Most of the women, enjoying the sight of her distress and misery, kept silent. But some female relatives of Polycrates and certain other noblewomen, still unaware of the full crisis, approached and tried to comfort her.

She cried out in a loud voice, “Do not come near me, you beasts! I know you well: your hearts are against us, and you pray to the goddesses for the worst things against us.

But I still trust that, if the gods will it, you shall taste your own children yet.”

After saying this she ordered the women carrying rods to drive them off and beat any who refused to obey.

Taking this as their excuse, all the women withdrew, lifting up their hands to the gods and praying that she herself might experience those very things she was threatening to inflict on others.

## Book 15 Chapter 30: A Mob Assembles

By now the men had decided on revolution, and when in every household the anger of the women was added to the general resentment, the hatred flared up doubly.

As soon as night came on, the whole city was full of noise, lights, and people running about.

Some were gathering in the stadium with shouting, some were calling one another to join them, and some were darting about and hiding in unsuspected houses and places.

Already the open spaces around the palace, the stadium, the broad street, and the area by the Dionysian theater were full of every sort of crowd.

When Agathocles learned what was happening, he roused himself out of drunkenness — he had only just dismissed his drinking party — and taking with him all his relatives except Philo, he went to the king.

After briefly lamenting his own misfortune to the boy and taking him by the hand, he went up into the covered passage that lay between the Maeander and the wrestling-school and led to the entrance of the theater.

After securing the first two doors, he retreated to the third with two or three bodyguards, the king, and his own family.

These doors were made of lattice-work so that one could see through them, though they were fastened with double bars.

By this time the crowd had assembled from the whole city, so that not only the open ground but even the steps and rooftops were packed with people, and there arose a mixed uproar of shouting and cries, since women and children were mingled with the men.

For in disturbances of this kind, both in Carthage and in Alexandria, the children play no smaller a part than the men.

## Book 15 Chapter 31: The King Surrendered To the Soldiers

As day was already beginning to show, the shouting was still confused, but one demand stood out clearly from it: they were calling for the king.

The Macedonians were the first to act. Rising up, they seized the gatehouse used for public business in the royal palace.

A little later, when they discovered where in the court the king was, they went round to the passage, forced open the first doors, and when they came to the second demanded the child with loud cries.

Agathocles and his party, seeing now how matters stood for them, begged the bodyguards to go as envoys on their behalf to the Macedonians and declare that they resigned the guardianship, all other authority and honors, and even all their revenues.

They asked only that life itself be granted them, together with the bare necessities of food, so that by returning to their original station they might be incapable of harming anyone in future, even if they wished to.

None of the bodyguards obeyed except Aristomenes, the very man who after some time came to hold chief power in the state.

This man was an Acarnanian, and when later in life he gained control of affairs, he seemed to have governed both king and kingdom in the noblest and most honorable way; but to the same degree he had earlier flattered Agathocles in the days of his prosperity.

For he was the first, when Agathocles dined at his house, to present him alone among the guests with a golden crown, an honor custom allowed only to kings themselves.

He was also the first who dared to wear Agathocles’ likeness on his ring; and when a daughter was born to him, he named her Agathocleia.

But enough of that. After receiving these instructions, Aristomenes went out through a small wicket and came to the Macedonians.

He spoke briefly and explained Agathocles’ intention. At first the Macedonians wanted to stab him on the spot, but some held up their hands over him and begged the crowd to spare him, and so he returned with the order that either he must come back bringing the king with him or else not come out again himself.

Having sent Aristomenes back with these words, the Macedonians came up to the second doors and forced them open as well.

When Agathocles and his party saw from the Macedonians’ actions as much as from their answer how violent they were, they first stretched out their hands through the door, while Agathocleia thrust out the breasts which, she said, had nursed the king, and with every sort of pleading begged the Macedonians to grant them bare life.

## Book 15 Chapter 32: The King Conducted to the Stadium

But when, for all their many lamentations over their own fate, they accomplished nothing, at last they sent out the child with the bodyguards.

The Macedonians took the king, quickly set him on horseback, and led him to the stadium.

As soon as he appeared, there arose a great shout and clapping. They stopped the horse, lifted the boy down, led him forward, and seated him in the royal place.

Among the crowd there was at once both joy and grief: they were delighted that the child had been brought, but displeased that the guilty parties had not been arrested and made to suffer the punishment they deserved.

So they kept shouting continuously, demanding that the authors of all the evils be brought out and made an example.

As the day wore on and the crowd found no one on whom it could vent its rage, Sosibius — the son of Sosibius, and at that time one of the bodyguards, a man especially attentive to the king and the state —

seeing both that the people’s fury was unchangeable and that the child was suffering from the unfamiliar faces around him and from the uproar of the crowd, asked the king whether he would hand over to the people those who had wronged him or his mother.

When the boy nodded, Sosibius told some of the bodyguards to announce the king’s decision, while he himself lifted the child up and took him to his own nearby house to be cared for.

As the king’s command was announced, the whole place burst with clapping and shouting.

At that moment Agathocles and Agathocleia separated and withdrew to their respective lodgings.

Then some of the soldiers — some volunteering, others pushed on by the crowd — rushed off to search for them.

## Book 15 Chapter 33: The Massacre of Agathocles and Family

The beginning of bloodshed and slaughter came about by the following chance occurrence.

One of Agathocles’ attendants and flatterers, a man named Philo, came staggering out into the stadium still drunk.

Seeing the rush of the crowd, he said to those standing by that, just as before, they would regret it again if Agathocles appeared.

Some of those who heard him abused him, others shoved him about, and when he tried to defend himself, some tore off his cloak while others thrust their spears into him and stabbed him.

As soon as he was dragged insultingly into the middle, still writhing, and the crowd had tasted blood, they all began eagerly waiting for the arrival of the others.

Not long after, Agathocles himself came first, led in bound. The moment he entered, some rushed at him and stabbed him on the spot — an act done more by friends than by enemies, since they spared him the punishment he truly deserved. After him came Nicon,

then Agathocleia naked with her sisters, and after them all the rest of the family.

Last of all they dragged Oenanthe from the Thesmophorium into the stadium, bringing her in naked on horseback.

When they were all handed over to the crowd together, some bit them, some stabbed them, some gouged out their eyes, and whenever one fell, they tore the limbs apart, until they had mangled every one of them.

For there is something terrible in the savagery of the people of Egypt when their passions are stirred.

At that same time certain girls who had grown up with Arsinoe learned that Philammon, the man who had supervised the queen’s murder, had arrived three days earlier from Cyrene, and so they rushed to his house.

Forcing their way in, they beat Philammon to death with stones and clubs, strangled his little son, and then dragged Philammon’s wife naked into the street and killed her too.

Such was the end of Agathocles, Agathocleia, and their relatives.

## Book 15 Chapter 34: Why Agathocles Died As He Did

I am not unaware of the marvels and embellishments with which some writers have treated these events in order to astonish their hearers, giving more attention to ornamental comment than to the facts themselves and what truly matters in them.

Some refer everything to Fortune and place before the eyes the uncertainty and slipperiness of her workings; others dwell on the unexpectedness of what happened and try to supply the events with causes and probabilities.

But I did not intend to handle the matter in that way, because Agathocles was neither distinguished for military daring and power,

nor successful and admirable in administration, nor finally remarkable for courtly cleverness and villainous intrigue — the sort of ability by which Sosibius and many others managed to survive under one king after another. In Agathocles’ case the opposite of all this was true.

For he had indeed gained an astonishing advancement because of Philopator’s incapacity to rule.

But after gaining that position, and after receiving the most favorable opportunity to preserve his power following the king’s death, he lost both his authority and his life through his own cowardice and indolence, being condemned in a very short time.

## Book 15 Chapter 35: Agathocles Not a Good Example of Mutability of Fortune

Therefore one ought not to attach an elaborate moralizing discourse to men of this kind, as I said, in the way one might with Agathocles and Dionysius of Sicily and certain other men whose public careers won a real name.

For one of those men began from a common and humble station, and Agathocles — as Timaeus says mockingly — was a potter who left wheel, clay, and smoke behind and came as a young man to Syracuse.

At first, in their own generations, both became tyrants of Syracuse, a city that at that time had attained the highest distinction and greatest wealth.

Afterward they were thought sovereigns of all Sicily and masters of certain parts of Italy as well.

Agathocles even made an expedition against Libya and at last died still in possession of those high fortunes.

That is why they say that Publius Scipio, the first conqueror of the Carthaginians, when asked whom he considered the most practical men of affairs and the boldest with sound judgment, answered: the Sicilians Agathocles and Dionysius.

With men like those, it is fitting to draw readers to attention, perhaps also to speak of Fortune and of human affairs, and to add an instructive reflection. But with the men we have been discussing, that is wholly out of place.

## Book 15 Chapter 36: Agathocles Was Not An Important Person

For these reasons we have rejected any expanded treatment of Agathocles.

And not least because all startling reversals have a single force at first encounter that is worth notice, but afterward the hearing and sight of them become not only useless but positively wearisome.

For those who present anything to ear or eye ought to aim at two ends, benefit and pleasure; and in history, where this obligation especially applies, excessive dwelling on shocking disasters misses both.

For who would wish to imitate extraordinary catastrophes? And again, no one enjoys continually seeing and hearing things that are unnatural and beyond common human expectation.

We are indeed eager once, and at first, to see or hear such things, in order to learn that what seemed impossible is actually possible.

But once we believe it, no one enjoys lingering over the unnatural, and no one would wish to encounter the same thing again and again.

So what is told ought to be either admirable or pleasant; but an excessive account of disasters beyond these belongs more properly to tragedy than to history.

Still, perhaps one should pardon those who do not attend either to nature or to the general course of events throughout the inhabited world.

For they suppose that the greatest and most astonishing events in history are precisely those they themselves happen to meet with or hear about from others, and so they fix all their attention on these.

Accordingly they do not notice that they are spending too much speech on things that are neither novel, since others have already recounted them, nor able to give either profit or pleasure. But let this be enough on that point.

## Book 15 Chapter 37: Antiochus

IV. Affairs of Asia

King Antiochus at the beginning of his reign seemed a man of vast designs, boldness, and energetic execution.

But as he advanced in age he proved far inferior both to his earlier self and to the expectation others had formed of him.
