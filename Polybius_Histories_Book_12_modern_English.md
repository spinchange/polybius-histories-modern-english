## Book 12 Chapter 2: The Lotus

Polybius of Megalopolis, having been an eyewitness, reports much the same things as Herodotus and his followers about the so-called lotus in Libya, writing as follows in the twelfth book of his *Histories*:

The lotus is a tree that is not large, but rough and thorny; it has a green leaf resembling that of the rhamnus (buckthorn), somewhat deeper in color and broader.

The fruit, at first, is like ripe white myrtle-berries both in color and in size,

but as it grows it turns crimson in color, and in size it comes to resemble round olives, and it has a very small stone.

When it has ripened, they gather it: the portion meant for the household slaves they pound up with groats and pack into jars, while the portion meant for the free men they likewise store away after removing the stone, and on this they feed.

The food is much like a fig or a date, but superior in fragrance.

A wine is also made from it by soaking and crushing it in water—sweet and pleasant to the taste, like good mead—which they drink without diluting it with water.

It cannot keep for more than ten days; and so they make it only in small quantities, as the need arises. They also make vinegar from the fruit.

## Book 12 Chapter 3: Timaeus Wrong about Libya

II. The Errors of Timaeus concerning Africa and Corsica

Anyone would marvel at the excellence of the country's soil,

but of Timaeus one would have to say that he has not only failed to investigate the facts about Libya, but is also childish and utterly incapable of reasoning, and still bound fast to those old traditional tales we have inherited—that Libya is wholly sandy, parched, and barren.

The same applies to its animals. For the abundance of horses, cattle, and sheep, and along with these of goats, throughout the country is so great that I doubt whether its match could be found anywhere else in the inhabited world,

because many of the tribes in Libya do not rely on cultivated crops, but draw their livelihood from their flocks and live among their flocks.

And indeed, who has not recorded the number and strength of its elephants, lions, and leopards, and besides these the beauty of its antelopes and the size of its ostriches? Of these there is nothing whatever in Europe, whereas Libya is full of the creatures just mentioned.

About all this Timaeus, having recorded nothing, gives an account that—as though deliberately—is the very opposite of what truly exists.

And just as he has improvised about the affairs of Libya, so too he has done about the island called Cyrnus (Corsica).

For in mentioning that island in his second book, he says that there are in it many wild goats, sheep, and wild oxen, and besides these deer, hares, wolves, and some of the other animals, and that the people busy themselves in hunting these and pass their whole way of life in such pursuits.

But on the island in question there is not so much as a wild goat or ox—nor even a hare, a wolf, a deer, or any other animal of the kind—except foxes, rabbits, and wild sheep.

The rabbit, seen from a distance, looks like a small hare, but when one takes it in hand it shows a great difference both in appearance and in taste; and it lives, for the

## Book 12 Chapter 4: The Errors of Timaeus

greater part, underground. All the animals on the island, however, do seem to be wild, for the following reason.

The herdsmen cannot follow alongside their flocks at pasture, because the island is wooded, precipitous, and rugged; but whenever they wish to gather them, they take their stand at convenient spots and call the animals together with a trumpet, and every one of them runs unfailingly to its own trumpet.

Consequently, when people sail up to the island and see goats or cattle grazing untended, and then try to seize them, the animals will not let them approach, since they are unaccustomed to them, but flee.

But when the herdsman, seeing the men disembark, sounds his trumpet, the animals at once rush headlong and run together to the trumpet. This gives the impression of wildness—about which Timaeus, having inquired poorly and perfunctorily, made a slapdash report.

Their obedience to the trumpet is nothing to wonder at: for in Italy too those who raise swine manage the pasturing in the same way.

The swineherds do not follow at the heels of the beasts, as among the Greeks, but go on ahead sounding the horn at intervals, while the beasts follow behind and run together toward the sound;

and so great a familiarity develops in the animals toward their own horn that those who hear of it for the first time are amazed and can scarcely believe it.

For owing to the abundance of labor and the other resources available, the droves of swine in Italy turn out to be enormous—especially along the coast, among the Etruscans and the Gauls—so that a single breeding sow rears a thousand pigs, and sometimes even more.

For this reason they make their drives out from the night-pens according to litters and ages.

Hence, when several droves are brought forward to the same place, they cannot keep them sorted by litter, but they get mixed up with one another both during the drives out and at pasture, and likewise during the drives home.

From these circumstances the device of the horn has been contrived by them for separating the animals, whenever they get mixed together, without toil or trouble.

For when one of the herdsmen goes off toward one quarter sounding his horn, and another turns aside toward another, the beasts separate of their own accord and follow their own horns with such eagerness that it is impossible by any means to force or to check their rush.

But among the Greeks, in the oak-forests, when the herds get mixed together while chasing the mast, the man who has more hands and seizes the opportunity gathers up his neighbor's beasts along with his own and drives them off.

And sometimes a thief, lying in wait, drives them off, without the herdsman in charge even realizing how he lost them—because the animals stray far from their drivers in their scramble for the mast, just when it is beginning to fall. But enough of this.

## Book 12 Chapter 4a

III. On the Other Errors of Timaeus

Having torn Timaeus apart on many points, Polybius goes on to say: Who could still grant indulgence for such mistakes, especially to Timaeus, who fastens on others over hangnail-sized trifles like these?

Among these charges he accuses Theopompus on the ground that, although Dionysius made his crossing back from Sicily to Corinth in a warship, Theopompus says that Dionysius arrived in a round-bottomed merchant vessel.

And again he falsely charges Ephorus with ignorance, claiming that Ephorus says that Dionysius the Elder took over the government when he was twenty-three years old, held power for forty-two years, and ended his life having reached the age of sixty-three.

Now no one, surely, would say that this slip belongs to the historian; by common consent it belongs to the copyist.

For either Ephorus must have surpassed even Coroebus and Margites in stupidity, if he was incapable of reckoning that forty-two added to twenty-three makes sixty-five;

or, since this could never be believed of Ephorus, it is plain that the error *** is the copyist's, while no one would approve of Timaeus's fondness for fault-finding and accusation.

## Book 12 Chapter 4b

Moreover, in his account of Pyrrhus he says again that the Romans, even now, keep up a memorial of the destruction at Ilium: on a certain day they spear to death a warhorse in front of the city, in the place called the Campus, because the capture of Troy came about through the horse known as the Wooden Horse — a thing more childish than anything.

For at that rate one would have to say that all barbarians are descendants of the Trojans;

since nearly all of them, or if not all, at least the majority, whenever they are about to begin a war or to stake everything in a decisive battle against some enemy, sacrifice a horse beforehand and slaughter it for omens, reading the future

## Book 12 Chapter 4c

from the way the animal falls. As for Timaeus, in this matter of irrationality he seems to me to display not only inexperience but, even more, the great pretentiousness of a late and shallow learner, since, just because they sacrifice a horse, he immediately supposed that they do so because Troy is thought to have been captured by means of a horse.

But that he has given a poor account both of the affairs of Libya and of those of Sardinia, and most of all of those concerning Italy,

is evident from these instances; and in general that the part of his work dealing with inquiry and the gathering of evidence has been utterly slovenly with him — though this is the most essential part of history.

For since events occur simultaneously in many places, and it is impossible for the same man to be present in several places at the same time, and likewise impossible for one man to be an eyewitness of all the regions of the inhabited world and of their distinctive features,

what remains is to make inquiry from as many sources as possible, to trust those who are worthy of credence, and to be a sound judge of the reports that come in.

## Book 12 Chapter 4d

It is in this very area, where he makes the greatest pretensions, that Timaeus seems to me to fall furthest short of the truth.

For so far is he from investigating the truth accurately through others that, even concerning the things of which he has been an eyewitness and the places he has himself visited, not even about these does he give us any sound account.

This will become clear if we show, in his treatment of Sicily, that he is ignorant of the very things on which he pronounces.

For there will hardly be need of many more words about his lying, if he is found to be ignorant and wide of the truth even in the regions where he was born and bred — and among these, in the most conspicuous of them.

He says, then, that the spring Arethusa in Syracuse draws its sources from the river Alpheus in the Peloponnese, which flows through Arcadia and past Olympia;

for that river, he claims, sinks beneath the earth and, after being carried four thousand stades under the Sicilian sea, comes up again in Syracuse.

And this, he says, was made clear when, at a certain time, torrential rains broke out during the season of the Olympic games and the river flooded the area around the sacred precinct,

and Arethusa threw up a quantity of dung from the oxen sacrificed at the festival, and cast up a golden bowl, which people recognized as belonging to the festival and retrieved.

## Book 12 Chapter 5: Aristotle's Account of Locri is Correct

It so happens that I have several times visited the city of the Locrians and rendered them necessary services.

For it was through me that they obtained release from the expedition to Iberia, and likewise from the one to Dalmatia, both of which, under the terms of their treaty, they were obliged to furnish to the Romans by sea.

Having thereby been freed from considerable hardship, danger, and expense, they repaid me with every kind of honor and kindness. For this reason I have all the more cause to speak well of the Locrians rather than the opposite.

Nevertheless, I have not hesitated to say and to write that the account of their colonization handed down by Aristotle happens to be truer than the one told by Timaeus.

For I know from the people themselves that they agree that the tradition about the colonization which has been passed down to them from their fathers is the one Aristotle has given, not Timaeus. And they offered proofs of this such as the following.

First, that among them all the distinctions inherited through ancestors derive from the women, not the men—so that, for example, those reckoned noble among them are immediately those said to be descended from the hundred households.

These hundred households are the ones that were singled out by the Locrians before the colony set out, the households from which the Locrians, in accordance with the oracle, were to choose by lot the maidens to be sent to Ilium.

Some of these women, they say, set out together with the colony, and it is their descendants who are still now reckoned noble and called "those of the hundred households."

Again, concerning the woman they call the "cup-bearer," a tradition of this kind had been handed down to them:

that at the time when they drove out the Sicels who held this region of Italy—a people among whom the boy of the most renowned and noble of the chief men led the way in the sacrifices—they themselves, having taken over many of the Sicel customs as well (since they had no ancestral ones of their own), preserved this one too from those people,

but corrected this very point: they did not make the cup-bearer a boy of their own, but a maiden, on account of the nobility that descended through the women.

## Book 12 Chapter 6: The Epizephyrian Locrians

As for treaties, with the Locrians in Greece there were none, nor was it said among them that any had ever existed; but with the Sicels they all had one preserved in their tradition.

Concerning this they said that, at the time when, on their first arrival, they found the Sicels occupying this land in which they now dwell, and these Sicels, struck with fear of them, received them out of dread,

they made an agreement of this kind: that they would truly be friendly toward them and hold the country in common, "for as long as they set foot upon this ground and bear their heads upon their shoulders."

While oaths of this sort were being taken, they say that the Locrians put earth inside the soles of their shoes and concealed heads of garlic on their shoulders, and so swore the oaths;

and then, having shaken the earth out of their shoes and thrown away the heads of garlic, they drove the Sicels out of the country not long afterward, when the opportunity arose.

This, then, is the story told among the Locrians.

## Book 12 Chapter 6a

From these considerations one would, on reflection, side with Aristotle rather than with Timaeus; and indeed what follows in Timaeus is utterly absurd.

For to suppose, as he suggests, that it was not likely that the servants of those who had fought as allies of the Spartans should transfer their masters' goodwill to their masters' friends — that is naïve;

for not only the friendships, but also the ties of hospitality and the kinships of their masters, are what former slaves, when they have unexpectedly prospered and time has gone by, try to claim for themselves and to renew, even more than their own natural connections,

since by this very means they are eager to wipe out the diminished standing and the disrepute that formerly attached to them, preferring to appear

## Book 12 Chapter 6b

descendants of their masters rather than freedmen. And this is most likely to have happened in the case of the Locrians; for, having moved far away from those who knew the facts and having taken time as their accomplice, they were not so foolish as to pursue practices by which they would only renew the memory of their own shortcomings, rather than, on the contrary, the practices by which they would cover them up.

That is why they reasonably gave the city its name from the women, and claimed kinship through the women, and furthermore kept renewing the ancestral friendships and alliances that came down through the women.

Consequently, the fact that the Athenians ravaged their territory is no proof at all that Aristotle is lying;

for, since it is reasonable, on the grounds already stated — even if they were ten times over slaves — that those who set out from the Locrians and settled in Italy should have claimed the friendship of the Spartans, it becomes equally reasonable that the Athenians should have been hostile toward all the aforesaid peoples, judging them not so much by their descent as by their political conduct.

"But by Zeus," it may be objected, "how is it that the Spartans themselves used to send back their men in their prime to their homeland for the sake of begetting children, yet would not allow the Locrians to do anything similar?" Now each of these points differs greatly, not only in plausibility,

but also in truth.

For the Spartans were not going to forbid the Locrians, while doing the same thing themselves — that would be absurd — nor, even if the Spartans had ordered it, would the Locrians necessarily have done the like.

For among the Spartans it was both an ancestral and a customary thing for one woman to have three husbands, or four, and sometimes even more, when they were brothers, and for their children to be held in common; and it was considered honorable and customary, once a man had begotten enough children, to give his wife in marriage to one of his friends.

Therefore the Locrians, being bound neither by the curses nor by the oaths which the Spartans had sworn — not to return home before they had taken Messene by storm — reasonably had no share in the collective expedition,

but, making their returns home individually and only rarely, they gave their women the chance to associate more familiarly with the slaves than with their original husbands, and the unmarried girls still more so — which became the cause of the migration. —

## Book 12 Chapter 7: Timaeus and the Character of a Historian

Timaeus records many falsehoods, and he seems to do so not at all from inexperience in such matters, but because he is blinded by contentiousness: once he sets out to blame someone, or on the contrary to praise him, he forgets everything else and oversteps the mark by a great deal.

But let what we have said on Aristotle's behalf suffice—how, and by attending to what evidence, he produced such an account of the Locrians.

What I am about to say about Timaeus and his whole composition, and about the duty of those engaged in writing history generally, will take this kind of treatment.

That both of them framed their attempt according to probable reasoning, and that there are more grounds for credence in Aristotle's account, I think anyone would agree from what has been said; yet it is impossible in these matters to determine anything as true and settled once and for all.

Even so, let us grant that Timaeus gives the more probable account. Is it for that reason necessary that those who in their histories tell the less probable story should have to listen to every harsh word and outcry and all but stand trial for their lives? Surely not.

For we have said that those who write falsehoods through ignorance deserve a kindly correction and pardon, but those who do so by deliberate choice deserve relentless condemnation.

## Book 12 Chapter 8: Timaeus Criticises Aristotle

It must therefore either be shown that Aristotle, in the account just discussed, spoke about the Locrians for the sake of favor, profit, or personal quarrel; or, if no one dares to claim this, then it must be admitted that those who treat their neighbors with such hostility and bitterness as Timaeus has used against Aristotle are themselves in the wrong and out of their senses.

For he says that Aristotle is reckless, irresponsible, and rash, and that, on top of this, he has shown insolent audacity toward the city of the Locrians by saying that their colony was made up of runaway slaves, household servants, adulterers, and kidnappers.

And he says that Aristotle makes these claims so authoritatively that one would think he was one of the generals who had just defeated the Persians at the Cilician Gates in a pitched battle by his own might,

rather than a late-learning, odious sophist who had only recently shut up his much-vaunted apothecary's shop—and who, besides, had pushed his way into every court and royal tent, and was a glutton and a gourmet, in everything carried away by his belly.

It seems to me that such language would scarcely be tolerable even from a charlatan and a reckless man hurling words about in a law court—and not even then would he seem moderate.

But a writer of public affairs and a true champion of history would never so much as conceive such things in his own mind, much less dare to put them in writing.

## Book 12 Chapter 9: Timaeus's Statement of Method

Let us now examine Timaeus's own method as well, and set side by side for comparison the statements he has made about this same colony, so that we may learn which of the two better deserves such censure. In the same book, then, he says—

no longer using the same kind of merely probable reasoning in his refutations, but claiming that he himself had genuinely gone in person to the Locrians in Greece to investigate the matters concerning the colony.

These Locrians, he says, first showed him written agreements, still preserved even today, made with the original settlers who had been sent out—agreements at the head of which the relationship was set down in these terms: "as parents to their children."

Besides these, there were decrees granting mutual rights of citizenship between the two peoples. And in general, when they heard Aristotle's account of the colony, they were astonished at the writer's effrontery.

Then, crossing over again to the Locrians in Italy, he says he found that their laws and customs likewise accorded not with the unscrupulous ways of household slaves but with a colony of free men:

for among them, certainly, penalties have been laid down against kidnappers, just as against adulterers and runaway slaves—none of which would be the case if they were aware of being themselves descended from such people.

## Book 12 Chapter 10: The Investigations of Timaeus

Now the first difficulty one might raise is this: to which of the Locrians did he go, and whom did he question, about these matters?

For if it had been the case that the Locrians in Greece, like those in Italy, all lived in a single city, then perhaps there would be no need for puzzlement, and the matter would be easy to settle.

But since there are two peoples of Locrians, to which of them did he go? And to which cities of the one or the other? And in whose keeping did he find the agreements on record? For none of this is made clear to us.

And yet that this is a specialty of Timaeus's—that it is in this above all that he has set himself in rivalry with other writers, and that this is broadly the very ground of his reputation *** —I mean his parade of precision in chronology and in records, and his care for that department of research—this, I think, we all recognize.

It is therefore worth wondering how he failed to make clear to us the name of the city in whose keeping he found the document, the place in which the agreement happens to be inscribed, or the magistrates who showed him the record and with whom he held his conversation—so that no one might have any ground for doubt at all, but that, with the place and city fixed, those who disputed the point might be able to ascertain the truth.

By passing over all this, he plainly shows that he is conscious of having lied on purpose. For that Timaeus, had he laid hold of such evidence, would have let none of it slip but would have clung to it, as the saying goes, with both hands—this is evident from the following.

For he is the man who rested his case, by name, on the authority of Echecrates, with whom he says he held his conversation about the Locrians in Italy, and from whom he says he learned these things;

and who took the further trouble, so as not to appear to have heard them from some chance informant, of adding that this man's father had earlier been thought worthy of an embassy by Dionysius.

Would such a man, then, surely, had he laid hold of a public record or an ancestrally transmitted inscribed pillar—

## Book 12 Chapter 11: Timaeus's Use of Chronological Records

—have passed it over in silence? For this is the very man who draws up his comparative tables, tracing the line of the ephors back to its origins and setting them against the kings at Lacedaemon and the archons at Athens, and matching the priestesses at Argos against the Olympic victors, and who convicts the cities of error in these records when they show a discrepancy of as little as three months—this is he.

And indeed it is Timaeus who hunted out the inscribed pillars in the rear chambers of the temples and the records of guest-friendship on the doorposts of the shrines.

He is a man whom we cannot believe either to have been ignorant of any such thing that existed, or, having found it, to have left it out; nor can pardon be granted him on any grounds if he has lied:

for, having been a harsh and relentless censurer of his neighbors, he may reasonably expect to meet with relentless accusation from his neighbors in return. Nevertheless, having been plainly convicted of falsehood in these matters,

he crosses over to the Locrians in Italy and first says that the constitution and the other humane institutions are shared by both Locrian peoples *** and that Aristotle and Theophrastus have lied against the city.

Now I am well aware that here too I shall be compelled to digress from my proper task, in laying down and affirming the truth about these matters;

yet it was for this very reason that I held over my discussion of Timaeus to a single section, so that I might not again and again be forced to neglect more pressing business.—

That Timaeus says the greatest fault in history is falsehood: hence he also advises those whom he may convict of false statements in their writings to seek some other name for their books, and to call them anything whatever rather than history.—

## Book 12 Chapter 12: Timaeus On Divination

For just as with a carpenter's rule—even if it be shorter in length or narrower in width, yet so long as it possesses the essential property of a rule, he says it must still be called a rule, but when it falls away from straightness and from kinship with the straight line, it must be called anything rather than a rule—

in just the same way, he says, with written works: however much they may go astray in their diction, or in their handling, or in any other of their particular parts, yet if they hold fast to the truth, the books may lay claim to the name of history; but when they fall away from truth, they ought no longer to be called history.

Now, that truth must be regarded as the guiding principle of such works, I myself agree—and somewhere in my own work I have said as much, putting it thus: that just as, when the eyes are removed from a living body, the whole is rendered useless, so, if you take away truth from history, what is left of it becomes a worthless tale.—

We said, however, that there are two kinds of falsehood:

one arising from ignorance, the other from deliberate intention;

and that, of these, those who stray from the truth through ignorance deserve pardon, while toward those who do so by deliberate choice one should be unforgiving.—

These points being agreed between us,

I hold that in this very matter of falsehood there is a great difference between what comes about through ignorance and what through deliberate intention—the former admitting of pardon and kindly correction, the latter justly meeting with unforgiving condemnation;

and it is to this latter kind, above all, that one would find Timaeus himself liable. That he is indeed such a man, it is now possible to consider.—

## Book 12 Chapter 12a

Of those who break their agreements we say, by way of this proverb, "the Locrians and their compacts." Someone has discovered that this is acknowledged both among the historians and among other people,

namely that, at the time of the invasion of the Heraclidae, the Locrians had agreed with the Peloponnesians to raise hostile beacon-fires if it should turn out that the Heraclidae were making their crossing not by way of the Isthmus but by way of Rhium, so that the Peloponnesians, getting advance warning, might guard against their attack;

but the Locrians did not do so — quite the contrary, they raised friendly beacons when the Heraclidae arrived. As a result, the Heraclidae made their crossing in safety, while the Peloponnesians, caught off their guard, unwittingly admitted the enemy into their own land, having been betrayed by the Locrians' breach of faith.

For he says that Callisthenes was a flatterer for writing such things, and that he was very far from philosophy, attending instead to vulgar dances and to women in Corybantic frenzy; and that he justly met with punishment at Alexander's hands for having corrupted that man's soul as far as he was able;

## Book 12 Chapter 13: Timaeus On Demochares and Agathocles

Timaeus claims that Demochares prostituted the upper parts of his body, that he was not fit to blow upon the sacred fire, and that in his practices he had outdone the writings of Botrys and Philaenis and all the other authors of shameless filth.

Yet such abuse and such insinuations are not the sort of thing a cultivated man would have set down — indeed, not even one of those who ply their trade with their bodies from a brothel rooftop would have done so.

And in order to seem credible in his foul language and his utter shamelessness, Timaeus has also fabricated lies against the man, dragging in besides some anonymous comic poet as his witness.

On what grounds do I make this inference? First, from the fact that Demochares was both born well and raised well, being a nephew of Demosthenes;

second, from the fact that he was deemed worthy by the Athenians not only of a generalship but of the other honors as well — none of which would have come his way had he been struggling with such disgraces.

For this reason it seems to me that Timaeus is not so much accusing Demochares as he is accusing the Athenians, if they really did promote such a man and entrust their country and their own lives to one like him.

But none of this is true. For Archedicus the comic poet would not have been the only one to say these things about Demochares,

as Timaeus claims; rather, many of Antipater's friends would also have said them — for against Antipater he spoke with great frankness, saying much that could wound not only Antipater himself but also his successors and friends — and so too would many of his political opponents, among whom was Demetrius of Phalerum.

Against Demetrius that man (Demochares) leveled no ordinary accusation in his histories, declaring that he had been the sort of leader of his country, and that he prided himself in his administration on the sort of things, that even a vulgar tax-farmer might boast of.

For Demetrius, he says, used to boast that many goods were sold cheaply throughout the city and that there was abundance of the necessities of life for everyone — on these things he prided himself;

and Demochares relates that a snail moving by itself led Demetrius's procession, spitting out spittle, and that along with it asses were paraded through the theater — the point being that the country, having surrendered to others all the fair distinctions of Greece, was doing whatever Cassander commanded — and that Demetrius was not ashamed of these things.

But all the same, neither Demetrius nor anyone else ever said

## Book 12 Chapter 14: No Place in History for Abusive Language

anything of the kind about Demochares. And so I, holding the testimony of his own country to be more reliable than the bitterness of Timaeus, confidently declare that the life of Demochares is liable to none of these accusations.

And even if some such disgrace had truly attached to Demochares, what occasion or what circumstance compelled Timaeus to enter these matters into his history?

For just as sensible men, when they decide to take revenge on their enemies, do not consider first what the other man deserves to suffer, but rather what it befits themselves to do —

so too with abuse: one should not regard first what it is fitting for our enemies to hear, but should reckon as most essential what it is proper for us to say.

As for those who measure everything by their own passions and ambitions, one is bound to be suspicious of all they say and to distrust everything spoken by them beyond what is right.

And so in the present case we may reasonably be thought to set aside what Timaeus has said against Demochares,

while he himself would not reasonably obtain pardon or credence from anyone, because he so plainly falls away from what is proper in his abuse, owing to his inborn bitterness.

## Book 12 Chapter 15: Timaeus Blinded by Personal Malignity

For neither do I approve of his abuse against Agathocles, even if Agathocles was the most impious of all men. I mean the passages

in which, at the close of his whole history, he says that Agathocles in his youth was a common prostitute, available to the most unrestrained, a jackdaw, a buzzard, offering his backside to the front of all who wished.

In addition to this, he says that when Agathocles died, his wife, weeping over him, lamented in this way: "What did I not do for you? What did you not do for me?"

In the face of these passages too one might not only repeat what was said about Demochares, but also marvel at the extravagance of his bitterness.

For that Agathocles must by nature have possessed great advantages is clear from the very things that Timaeus himself declares.

For if he came to Syracuse fleeing the potter's wheel, the smoke, and the clay, at about eighteen years of age,

and after some time, setting out from such a starting point, became master of all Sicily, brought the Carthaginians into the gravest dangers, and finally, having grown old in power, ended his life bearing the title of king —

does it not follow that Agathocles must have been some great and marvelous figure, and that he possessed many resources and capacities for practical affairs?

Concerning these things the historian ought to relate to posterity not only what tends to slander and accusation, but also what redounds to the man's praise; for this is the proper task of history.

But Timaeus, blinded by his own bitterness, has reported his faults to us spitefully and with exaggeration, while he has omitted his successes altogether,

not realizing that falsehood is no less present in those who in their histories pass over in silence what actually happened than in those who misstate it.

As for me, I have refrained from adding further measure for the sake of dwelling on his hatefulness, but I have not left out what was germane to my own purpose. —

## Book 12 Chapter 16: The Laws of Zaleucus

Two young men were in dispute over a certain slave. It happened that the slave had been in the possession of one of them for a longer time;

but the other, having come to the farm two days earlier when the master was not present, had carried off the slave to his own house by force,

and then the first man, on learning of it, came to the house, took the slave, and was leading him away before the magistrate, declaring that he ought to have possession by providing sureties.

For the law of Zaleucus, he said, ordained that, pending the trial, the disputed property should remain in the keeping of the party from whose possession the seizure had been made.

But the other man, according to the same law, claimed that the seizure had been made from himself — for it was from his house that the slave was being taken away to the magistrate —

so that the presiding magistrates, being at a loss about the matter, called in and shared the case with the Cosmopolis.

He interpreted the law, saying that the seizure is always reckoned to be made from those in whose possession the disputed property had last rested undisputed for some period of time;

but that if someone takes it by force from another and carries it off to himself, and then the prior owner makes his seizure from this man, that seizure is not valid.

When the young man took this badly and denied that this was the lawgiver's intention, they say the Cosmopolis challenged him, if he wished to say anything about the meaning according to the law of Zaleucus.

This meant arguing before the seated court of the thousand, with nooses hung in place, about the lawgiver's intention:

whichever of the two should be shown to be interpreting the meaning for the worse was to be destroyed by hanging in the sight of the thousand.

When the Cosmopolis proposed these terms, they say the young man replied that the agreement was not equal: for to the one only two or three years of life were left —

since it happened that the Cosmopolis was not far short of ninety years old — while for himself, in all reasonable likelihood, the greater part of his life still remained.

So the young man, having made this witty turn, dissolved the seriousness of the matter, and the magistrates judged the question of the seizure in accordance with the opinion of the Cosmopolis.

## Book 12 Chapter 17: A Criticism on Ephorus and Callisthenes

So that I may not seem to be running down such distinguished writers without warrant, I will recall a single battle — one that happens to be among the most celebrated ever fought, not very remote from us in time, and, most importantly, one at which Callisthenes himself was present.

I mean the engagement in Cilicia between Alexander and Darius. Callisthenes says that Alexander had by then already made his way through the narrow passes and through what are called the Cilician Gates, while Darius, taking the route through the so-called Amanid Gates, came down with his army into Cilicia.

On learning from the local people that Alexander was advancing in the direction of Syria, Darius followed after him, and on coming up close to the passes he encamped beside the river Pinarus.

The width of the ground, he says, was no more than fourteen stades from the sea up to the foot of the mountains.

Across this stretch the aforementioned river ran at an angle: right where it issued from the mountains its banks were broken with gullies, and in its course through the level country down to the sea it had steep and difficult bluffs.

Having laid down this description, he says that when Alexander's men, having turned about, began drawing near and advancing back against them, Darius and his officers decided to draw up the whole phalanx within the encampment itself, just as it had been from the start, and to use the river as a defensive barrier, since it flowed right past the camp.

After this, he says, they stationed the cavalry along the sea, the mercenaries next to them right along the river, and the peltasts beyond these, linking up with the mountains.

## Book 12 Chapter 18: The Battle of Issus

But how he could have drawn these troops up in front of the phalanx, when the river was flowing right beside the encampment, is hard to understand — especially given that they were present in such great numbers.

For there were thirty thousand cavalry, as Callisthenes himself states, and thirty thousand mercenaries; and it is easy to work out how much ground these would have needed.

At the very most, cavalry is drawn up eight deep for any real practical purpose, and between each squadron there has to be an interval equal to its frontage, so that they can be useful in wheeling and in countermarching.

On this reckoning a stade holds eight hundred horsemen, ten stades hold eight thousand, and four stades hold three thousand two hundred; so that the fourteen-stade space would be filled by eleven thousand two hundred.

But if he were to deploy the whole thirty thousand, the cavalry alone would fall only a little short of forming three phalanxes one behind another.

Into what space, then, was the mass of mercenaries drawn up? Unless, by Zeus, behind the cavalry — but he does not say that; rather, he says that these mercenaries clashed with the Macedonians during the advance.

From this one is forced to take it that the cavalry's line occupied half the ground, the half by the sea, and the mercenaries the other half, the half toward the mountains.

And from these figures it is easy to reckon how deep the cavalry stood, and how great a distance the river must have been from the encampment.

After this, he says that as the enemy drew near, Darius himself, who was positioned at the center of the line, called the mercenaries over to himself from the wing.

But how this can be meant is a puzzle: the junction of the mercenaries and the cavalry must have been at the center of the ground, so that, since Darius was right there among the mercenaries, where and to what point and how could he have been calling the mercenaries over?

Finally, he says that the cavalry from the right wing led an advance and charged into Alexander's force, and that these received them gallantly, charged back, and made a fierce fight of it.

But that there was a river in between — and a river of just the kind he had himself described a moment before — he forgot.

## Book 12 Chapter 19: Callisthenes Vague on Alexander's Movements

His account of Alexander's movements is much the same. He says that Alexander made his crossing into Asia with forty thousand infantry and four thousand five hundred cavalry,

and that as he was about to invade Cilicia a further force came from Macedonia: five thousand infantry and eight hundred cavalry.

Even if one were to subtract from these three thousand infantry and three hundred cavalry — making the most generous allowance for losses incurred along the way in the various operations — there would still be left forty-two thousand infantry and five thousand cavalry.

On these assumptions, he says that Alexander learned of Darius's arrival in Cilicia when he was a hundred stades away from him, having already passed through the narrows;

and that he therefore turned about and made his way back through the narrows, leading first the phalanx, then the cavalry, and last of all the baggage train. As soon as the head of the column emerged into open ground,

he gave the order for everyone to extend the line, bringing up the phalanx alongside, and made its depth thirty-two; then, after that, again into sixteen; and finally, as he neared the enemy, into eight.

But these are even greater absurdities than the ones already mentioned. A stade, on the marching intervals, holds — when the men are sixteen deep — sixteen hundred men, each man occupying six feet.

It is plain, then, that ten stades will hold sixteen thousand, and twenty stades twice that number.

From this it is readily seen that at the moment Alexander made his force sixteen deep, the width of ground had to be twenty stades, and there would still be left over all the cavalry and ten thousand of the infantry.

## Book 12 Chapter 20: Inaccuracies about Tactics

After this he says that Alexander led his force forward in line abreast, when he was about forty stades from the enemy.

It is hard to imagine an absurdity greater than this. For where could anyone find ground of such a kind — and especially in Cilicia — as to lead a sarissa-bearing phalanx in line abreast over a front of twenty stades and a depth of forty?

So many are the obstacles to such a formation and operation that one could not easily even count them. But one of the things mentioned by Callisthenes himself is enough to settle the matter:

he says that the winter torrents pouring down from the mountains make so many washed-out gullies across the plain that, as he reports, most of the Persians too perished in such hollows during their flight.

But, by Zeus — it may be said — Alexander wanted to be ready for the enemy's appearance.

Yet what is less ready than a phalanx broken up and strung out along its front? For how much easier it is to deploy from a suitable marching order than to bring a force that is broken up and pulled apart along its front onto a single straight line and form it up for battle in wooded and broken country!

It was therefore far better — not just by a little — to advance in a double or quadruple phalanx of suitable form, for which it is not impossible to find marching ground, and which it is easy to deploy quickly, since one can learn of the enemy's presence well in advance through the advance scouts.

But this man, apart from everything else, did not even post the cavalry in front while leading his force in line abreast over level ground; instead he set them on an equal footing with the infantry.

## Book 12 Chapter 21: Worst Misstatement of Callisthenes

But the greatest error of all is yet to come. He says that, once Alexander was already close to the enemy, he formed his men up eight deep.

From this it follows necessarily that the length of the phalanx had to extend over forty stades.

And even if they had closed their shields together completely, in the poet's phrase so as to press right up against one another, the position would still have required twenty stades.

Yet he himself says that the available ground fell short of fourteen stades. *

** And of this, a portion next to the sea was taken up by half the cavalry on the right wing; *** and further, the whole line had to stand a sufficient distance away from the mountains so as not to be exposed to the enemy who held the slopes at their feet.

For we know how he makes the wing bend back at an angle to face them.

Even so, let us here leave out the ten thousand infantry, who are more than his own scheme allows. The upshot is that, on Callisthenes's own showing, at most eleven stades are left for the length of the phalanx, within which the thirty-two thousand men must necessarily be packed shield to shield thirty deep.

Yet he says the battle was fought with the men drawn up eight deep.

Mistakes of this kind do not even admit of any defense, for what is physically impossible in such matters carries its own proof on its face.

And so, once a writer has laid down both the intervals occupied by each man and the fixed size of the whole ground, together with the number of the men, the falsehood becomes indefensible.

## Book 12 Chapter 22: Further Absurdities

It would take too long to mention all his other absurdities along with these, so I shall confine myself to a very few.

He says that Alexander took pains, in drawing up his order of battle, to set himself directly opposite Darius so as to engage him in person; and that likewise, at the outset, Darius too wished to be opposite Alexander, but afterwards changed his mind.

But as to how these two recognized each other, or at what point in their own forces each had taken up his position, or to what point Darius in turn shifted himself, absolutely nothing is said.

And how did a body of phalanx troops climb up onto the bank of the river, when it was steep and overgrown with brushwood? This too is contrary to reason.

Now such an absurdity ought not to be charged against Alexander, since it is agreed by everyone that he had experience and practice in warfare from boyhood,

but rather against the historian, who through his own inexperience cannot even distinguish, in such matters, between what is possible and what is not.

Let this, then, be our verdict concerning Ephorus and Callisthenes.

## Book 12 Chapter 23: Timaeus Exalts Timoleon To Excess

VI. On the historian Timaeus

Timaeus mounts a most violent attack against Ephorus, though he is himself guilty of two faults:

first, that he bitterly accuses others of the very things to which he is himself liable; and second, that he is thoroughly corrupt in his own mind, setting down such pronouncements in his memoirs and breeding such opinions in those who read them.

But if we are to hold that Callisthenes was justly punished when he met his death, what ought Timaeus to suffer? For the divine power would have far better cause to be indignant with this man than with Callisthenes.

Callisthenes, after all, only wished to deify Alexander, whereas Timaeus makes Timoleon greater than the most illustrious gods.

Callisthenes was exalting a man of such a kind that all agree he was, in spirit, of a greatness beyond the human measure,

while Timaeus exalts Timoleon—who not only is not thought to have achieved anything great, but never even attempted it, having completed in his whole life a single line, and one not even worth much, in a manner of speaking, set against the vast extent of the world: I mean the line from his homeland to Syracuse.

But it seems to me that Timaeus was convinced that, if Timoleon—having pursued glory within Sicily alone, as though in a little saucer—should be made to appear comparable with the most illustrious of the heroes, then he himself too, though he had busied himself only with Italy and Sicily, might fairly be deemed worthy of comparison with those who composed their histories of the whole world and of events at large.

So much, then, concerning Aristotle, Theophrastus, and Callisthenes, and further Ephorus and Demochares: this is enough by way of answer to the attacks of Timaeus, and equally to those who, without any critical care, have been persuaded that this writer tells the truth.

## Book 12 Chapter 24: Timoleon. Phalaris

One may well be perplexed at Timaeus's line of argument. For he says that poets and prose writers reveal their own natures in their works through the excesses on which they dwell too much.

He says, for instance, that the poet, by his frequent carving of meat throughout his poetry, shows himself as it were a glutton; and that Aristotle, by dressing up rich dishes again and again in his treatises, is a lover of fine food and a gourmand.

In the same way he speaks *** of Dionysius the tyrant, who was forever fussing over the arrangement of his couches and elaborating the peculiarities and rich variety of his hangings.

*** one must form the corresponding judgment *** and be dissatisfied *** with respect to his own bent of character.

For this man, in accusing his neighbors, displays great force and audacity, but in his own statements he is full of dreams, portents, and unconvincing fables, and, in a word, of base superstition and womanish marvel-mongering.

And yet that it does happen, through inexperience and poor judgment, that many men sometimes are in a sense not present though present, and do not see though they are looking—this has become clear both from what has just been said and from what has befallen Timaeus.

## Book 12 Chapter 25: General Remarks on Timaeus as an Historian

Concerning the bronze bull that was made by Phalaris at Acragas, into which he used to put men and then, by kindling a fire beneath, exact

such a punishment from his subjects that, as the bronze was heated white-hot, the man, roasted and scorched on every side, was destroyed; and in the extremity of his agony, whenever he cried out, the sound that issued from the device struck the ears of those listening like the bellowing of a bull.

This bull, when the Carthaginians gained the upper hand, was carried off from Acragas to Carthage, and the little door between the shoulders—through which those condemned to punishment used to be let down—still remained; and although no other reason why such a bull should have been built at Carthage could be discovered at all,

Timaeus nevertheless set out both to demolish the common report and to brand as false the statements of the poets and historians, asserting that the bull at Carthage had not come from Acragas and that no such thing had ever existed in that city;

and he has devoted a great many arguments to this point. — What term and what word must one apply to Timaeus? It seems to me that his kind deserves all the most bitter names, the very ones he himself has used against those around him.

That he is quarrelsome, a liar, and reckless has been shown well enough, I think, from what has already been said; but that he is unphilosophical and, in a word, an ill-bred writer will become clear from what is about to be said.

For in his twenty-first book, and at the very end of it, in the exhortation of Timoleon he says this: that the earth, lying beneath the heavens, is divided into three parts, one called Asia, another Libya, and another Europe.

That Timaeus could have said such a thing no one would believe—no, not even of that proverbial Margites.

For who is so ignorant, I do not say among those who have applied themselves to written records, *** [the text breaks off]

## Book 12 Chapter 25a

What Polybius of Megalopolis says about Timaeus. Just as the proverb has it that a single drop from the largest vessel is enough to tell us the character of the whole contents, so we must judge in the matter now before us.

For once one or two falsehoods are found in a man's writings, and these turn out to have been deliberate, it is clear that nothing said by such a writer can any longer be regarded as certain or reliable.

But in order to convince even those who are more inclined to take his part, I must speak about his method and the pains he takes with his public speeches and exhortations, and further with the speeches of ambassadors—in short, with everything of that kind, which are, as it were, the summary headings of events and hold the whole history together.

For that Timaeus has set these down in his account contrary to the truth, and has done so on purpose, what reader can fail to notice?

He has not written what was actually said, nor reported it as it really was said. Instead, having first decided how it ought to have been said, he reels off all the supposed speeches and the circumstances attending the events as a man might do who, in a rhetorical exercise, were attempting to argue a set theme *** as though he were making a display of his own ability, rather than giving a true account of what was said. —

## Book 12 Chapter 25b

It is the special property of history first to learn the words that were actually spoken, whatever they may have been, and second to find out the reason why the action or speech in question either failed or succeeded.

For the bare statement of what happened, while it may entertain us, is of no profit; but when the reason is added, the study of history becomes fruitful.

For when we transfer parallels from the past to our own circumstances, we gain starting points and grounds for forming judgments about what is to come, so that we may at one time take precaution and at another, imitating what has gone before, set about facing present dangers with greater confidence.

But the man who passes over in silence both the words that were spoken and the reason for them, and instead gives false arguments and long-winded speeches in their place, destroys the very thing that is proper to history. This is exactly what Timaeus does, and we all know that his books are full of this kind of thing.

## Book 12 Chapter 25c

One might perhaps be puzzled how a man of the character we are describing has won such acceptance and credit with some readers.

The reason for this is that, since his work overflows with censure and abuse of others, he is judged not by his own work nor by his own statements, but by his attacks on his neighbors; and to this end he seems to me to have brought a quite exceptional industriousness and a natural talent.

Something very much like this happened in the case of Strato the natural philosopher too. For he likewise, whenever he sets out to pick apart and refute the opinions of others, is admirable; but whenever he advances something of his own and expounds one of his own ideas, he appears to those who know the subject far sillier and duller than himself.

And it seems to me that what happens with writers is exactly like what happens in our life as a whole.

For here too it is easy to find fault with one's neighbors, but hard to keep oneself free from error; and one might say, almost as a rule, that those who are quickest to censure their neighbors are the very ones who make the most mistakes in their own lives.

## Book 12 Chapter 25d

Besides what has already been described, something else applies in the case of Timaeus. Having settled in Athens for nearly fifty years and devoted himself to the writings of earlier authors, he supposed that he possessed the greatest resources for writing history—mistakenly, in my view.

For history has something in common with medicine, in that each of them, in its broadest divisions, falls into three parts; and it follows that the dispositions of those who take them up are likewise comparable.

Take medicine straightaway: one part of it is the theoretical, the next the dietetic, and the third the surgical and pharmaceutical, of the kind *** entirely. ... by misrepresenting the profession *

** Now the theoretical branch, which has its chief origin from Alexandria, among those called there the followers of Herophilus and Callimachus, occupies one part of medicine; but by its outward appearance and its grand professions it draws to itself such a reputation that no one else seems to have a grasp of the subject.

Yet when you bring these men to the test of reality and put a sick patient in their hands, they prove to be as far from being of use as those who have never read a medical treatise at all. Some patients, having entrusted themselves to such men on account of their fluency in argument, though there was nothing seriously wrong with them, have often put themselves in extreme danger.

For they are truly like men who navigate from a book. And yet these men, traveling about the cities with much show, when they have gathered crowds *** by reputation, bring those who have given true proof of themselves in actual practice into the utmost difficulty and contempt with their audience, since the plausibility of words often overcomes the verdict of experience on the actual facts.

But the third kind—the man who brings to each profession a genuine, practiced competence—is not only rare, but is also often overshadowed by glibness and audacity, owing to the lack of judgment in the many.

## Book 12 Chapter 25e

In the very same way, practical history too is threefold. One of its parts concerns the diligent study of documents and the comparison of the material drawn from them; another concerns the survey of cities and places, of rivers and harbors, and in general of the distinctive features and distances by land and by sea; and the third concerns political affairs.

And in the same manner many set their hearts on history because of the reputation it has previously enjoyed, yet most of those who take up the task bring to it nothing that does them credit—nothing but facility, audacity, and unscrupulous workmanship.

Like the quack drug-sellers, they court popularity and always say what will please the moment, in order to make a living by this means; and they are not worth saying more about.

But some of those who seem to approach history on reasonable grounds, like the theoretical physicians, spend their time in libraries and acquire in general a wide range of experience from the documents, and so persuade themselves that they are competent for the task, and seem to outsiders to be adequately equipped—yet this is only one part, as it seems to me, of practical history.

For it is true that the examination of earlier records is useful for knowing the views of the ancients and the conceptions they once held about situations, places, peoples, and constitutions and actions, and further for understanding the circumstances and fortunes that each people experienced in earlier times.

For the past fittingly draws our attention toward the future, provided one inquires truthfully into what is past in each case.

But to be persuaded—as Timaeus is—that, starting from this capacity alone, one is equipped to write well the history of later events is utterly foolish, and much as if a man who had looked at the works of the ancient painters should think himself a competent painter and a master of the art.

## Book 12 Chapter 25f

What I am saying will become still clearer from what follows—for instance, straightaway from what happens to Ephorus in certain passages of his history. For in military matters he seems to me to have some notion of operations at sea, but to be entirely without experience of land engagements.

Accordingly, when one fixes attention on the sea-battles off Cyprus and off Cnidus, which the King's generals fought against Evagoras of Salamis and again against the Spartans, it is reasonable to admire the writer both for his ability and for his expertise, and to carry away much that is useful for similar situations.

But when he describes the battle of Leuctra between the Thebans and the Spartans, or again that of Mantinea between these same peoples, in which Epaminondas lost his life, then, if one pays close attention to the details and studies the formations and maneuvers made at the very crises of the action, he appears ridiculous and altogether inexperienced and ignorant of such matters.

Now the engagement at Leuctra, which was straightforward and fought with the army in a single body, does not make the writer's inexperience very conspicuous; but the one at Mantinea has, in his presentation, a varied and generalship-like appearance, yet is untenable and entirely beyond the writer's grasp. This will become clear

if one accurately lays out the ground and measures against it the movements he describes.

The same thing happens with Theopompus too, and most of all with Timaeus, who is our present subject.

For wherever such writers treat their subject in summary outline, they pass undetected; but wherever they choose to elaborate and demonstrate some point in detail, they show themselves to be exactly such men as Ephorus. —

## Book 12 Chapter 25g

That it is impossible to write well about events in war for the man who has no experience of military operations, or about affairs in the various states for the man who has had no practical experience of such actions and circumstances.

It follows that, when nothing is written either with experience or vividly by these bookish authors, the work proves to be of no practical use to its readers. For if one takes out of history the element that can benefit us, what is left of it becomes utterly worthless and useless.

Furthermore, when men set out to write in detail about cities and places while being untrained in such experience, it clearly must follow that the like result occurs: they leave out many things worthy of mention, and make much of many things not worth it. This happens most of all to Timaeus, owing to his want of direct observation.

 —

## Book 12 Chapter 25h

Timaeus says, in his thirty-fourth book: "Having spent fifty unbroken years living as a foreigner in Athens, I admittedly had no experience of warfare, nor, moreover, of the firsthand inspection of places."

Consequently, whenever in his history he comes upon one of these subjects, he is ignorant of much and falsifies much; and even when he occasionally touches the truth, he is like those painters who make their sketches from stuffed dummies.

For with them too the outer outline is sometimes preserved, but the vividness and the energy of living creatures is absent—which is precisely what is proper to the art of painting. And the same thing happens with Timaeus and in general with those who set out from this bookish habit.

For the vivid impression of events is absent from them, because this comes only from the writer's own firsthand experience; and so those who have not themselves gone through the events do not implant in their hearers any genuine enthusiasm.

It is for this reason that our predecessors thought such vivid touches ought to be present in their accounts; so that, when the discussion was about political affairs, they would add that of necessity the writer had taken part in public life and had experience of what happens in that sphere; and when it was about military affairs, that he had likewise served in campaigns and faced danger; and again, when about private life, that he had raised children and lived with a wife. And the like for the other departments of life.

This is reasonably to be found only among those writers who have themselves gone through the events and have made this department of history their own. To be, as it were, an active doer and performer of everything is perhaps difficult; but of the greatest and most general matters it is essential.

## Book 12 Chapter 25i

That what I say is not impossible, the poet is sufficient proof to win belief, for in him one may see this vividness present in abundance.

From all this everyone would reasonably agree that the diligent study of documents is the third part of history and holds the third rank.

That what I am now saying is true would become most evident in the case of the deliberative and hortatory speeches, and further the speeches of ambassadors, which Timaeus employs.

For few occasions admit of presenting all the arguments available, while most admit only a few of those at hand—and of these, some are used by the men of today, others by those of former times; and some the Aetolians put forward, some the Peloponnesians, and some the Athenians.

Now to run through all the available arguments idly and out of season, applied to everything and to everyone—which is what Timaeus does, contriving arguments for every subject—is utterly untruthful, juvenile, and fit only for the lecture-room; and indeed this has already been the cause of failure and contempt for many. But to choose always the fitting and timely arguments—that is essential.

And since the application is variable—both how many and what kind of the available arguments one should use—a rather different sort of practice and rule is needed if we are not to harm but to benefit our readers.

Now the right occasion is in all cases hard to teach by rule, yet it is not impossible to be brought within the range of intelligent guess through observations drawn from firsthand experience and practice; and in the present case what I am saying may best be inferred from the following.

For if writers, having indicated the occasions and the impulses and dispositions of those deliberating, and then having set out the speeches actually delivered, were to make clear to us the reasons why the speakers either hit the mark or missed it, we would arrive at some true understanding of the matter, and would be able—at once by discriminating and by transferring the parallel to similar cases—always to hit the mark in the questions before us.

But the trouble is, I think, that to explain causes is hard, while to coin speeches in books is easy; and to say a few words to the point, and to find the rule for this, is within reach of few, whereas to spin out a great deal of empty matter lying ready to hand is common and easy.

## Book 12 Chapter 25k

In order that we may confirm our verdict on Timaeus in these matters too, just as we have done concerning his ignorance and his deliberate falsification of the record, I will set out briefly a few of his speeches that are acknowledged to be his, citing them by name.

 — Of those who held power in Sicily after Gelon the elder, we have received as the most statesmanlike men Hermocrates, Timoleon, and Pyrrhus of Epirus—men to whom one would least of all wish to attach juvenile, lecture-room speeches.

Now Timaeus says, in his twenty-first book, that at the time when Eurymedon arrived in Sicily and was urging the cities to join the war against the Syracusans, the people of Gela, worn out by the war, then sent envoys to the people of Camarina to propose a truce;

and when these readily accepted, each side afterward sent embassies to their own allies, urging them to dispatch trustworthy men who should come to Gela to deliberate about a settlement and the common interests.

When the delegates had assembled and a debate had been laid before them, he brings on Hermocrates using speeches of some such tenor.

For this man, having praised the people of Gela and of Camarina—first because they were the ones who had made the truce, second because they had been responsible for the discussions about a settlement coming about, and third because they had taken care that it should not be the masses who deliberated about the settlements, but the leading citizens, who knew clearly what difference war makes from peace—

and then, after taking up two or three practical arguments, he says they had fixed their attention and learned how great a difference war makes from peace—though only a little earlier he had said that on this very account Hermocrates was grateful to the people of Gela that the discussions were not taking place before the multitude, but in a council that well understood such reversals of fortune.

From all this Timaeus would seem to fall short not only of practical ability, but also to come off no little the worse in the very arguments used in the schools of rhetoric.

For everyone surely thinks that proofs ought to be brought forward of things unknown and things disbelieved by the hearers, whereas about matters already known it is the most pointless and most childish thing of all to keep contriving arguments... than what is already known.

But this man, apart from his whole blunder in directing the greater part of his speech toward things that need no argument at all, has also used assumptions of such a kind

that no one could believe Hermocrates used them—the man who fought alongside the Spartans in the sea-battle at Aegospotami, and who with all their crews subdued the forces of the Athenians and their generals in Sicily—nor indeed any boy...

## Book 12 Chapter 26: Sophistical Commonplaces

...any chance youth? For he, first of all, thinks he ought to remind the delegates that those who sleep at dawn are roused in war by trumpets, but in peacetime by the birds.

After this he says that Heracles established the Olympic games and the truce that goes with them as a demonstration of his own principle, and that, although he harmed all those against whom he made war, he did so under necessity and by another's command, and was never willingly the cause of harm to any man.

Next to these, he brings in from the poet (Homer) Zeus, displeased with Ares and saying:

"Most hateful to me are you of the gods who hold Olympus; for strife is ever dear to you, and wars and battles."

And likewise the wisest of the heroes saying:

"Clanless, lawless, hearthless is that man who longs for chilling civil war."

And that Euripides agrees with the poet,

in the lines where he says:

"O Peace, rich in deep wealth, fairest of the blessed gods, I long for you—how long you delay! I fear that old age may overtake me before I look upon your lovely season, your songs of beautiful dances, and your garland-loving revels."

And in addition to these he says that war is most like disease and peace like health: for the one restores even the sick, while in the other even the healthy perish.

And that in peace the older are buried by the younger, as nature requires, whereas in war the opposite happens;

and, most important of all, that in war there is no safety even as far as the city walls, while in peace it extends to the very borders of the country—and other things much like these.

I wonder, then, what other words or expressions could have been used by a youth who has only just taken up the schools and the busywork drawn from written notes, and who wishes, by the rules of the art, to frame his composition from the attributes appropriate to the characters; for it seems to me he would use no others than precisely those which Timaeus says Hermocrates used.

## Book 12 Chapter 26a

Then again, what are we to make of the passage where Timoleon, in the same book, is urging the Greeks on to face the danger of war against the Carthaginians? When they were all but on the very point of coming to grips with an enemy many times their number, he first calls on them not to look at the multitude of their opponents but at their cowardice.

For although the whole of Libya is continuously inhabited and teeming with people, nevertheless in our proverbs, whenever we want to convey the idea of desolation, we say "more deserted than Libya"—aiming the expression not at any emptiness of population, but at the cowardice of the people who live there.

And in general, he says, who could be afraid of men who, though nature has given to human beings, as their own special endowment beyond the rest of the animals—I mean their hands—nevertheless go through their whole lives carrying these hands tucked uselessly inside their tunics?

But the crowning point, he says, is that under their tunics they actually wear loincloths, so that not even when they fall in battle should they be exposed to the enemy...

## Book 12 Chapter 26b

When Gelon offered to come to the aid of the Greeks with twenty thousand infantry and two hundred decked warships, on condition that they cede to him the command either on land or at sea, the story goes that the Greek leaders presiding at Corinth gave Gelon's envoys a most statesmanlike answer.

They told Gelon to come with his forces as an ally, but said that the command would, of necessity, be conferred by circumstances on the best men.

This is the language not of men taking refuge in the hopes held out by the Syracusans, but of men who trusted in themselves and were challenging anyone who cared to enter the contest of courage and compete for the crown of valor.

And yet, on each of the points just mentioned, Timaeus spins out speeches of such length and shows such eagerness to make Sicily grander than all of Greece together, the deeds done there more brilliant and finer than those of the rest of the world, its wise men the wisest of all who have excelled in wisdom, and its men of action—those from Syracuse—the most masterful and most godlike,

that he leaves no room for further exaggeration even to the schoolboys in the lecture-halls and the philosophers' walks who go in for paradoxical exercises, whenever they set out to deliver a eulogy of Thersites, or a denunciation of Penelope, or something else of that kind.

## Book 12 Chapter 26c

The upshot is that, through his excessive love of paradox, he ends up bringing the very men and deeds he wants to champion not into favorable comparison but into ridicule; and he falls into almost the same plight as those students in the Academy who have trained themselves in the most facile kind of argument.

For some of these men, wanting to drive those they debate with into perplexity—both about things that seem plainly knowable and about things that are genuinely unknowable—resort to such paradoxes and have such a ready stock of plausibilities that they make one wonder whether it is possible for people in Athens to smell eggs being boiled in Ephesus, and to doubt whether perhaps, at the very moment they are discussing these matters in the Academy, they are not awake at all but lying at home asleep, framing these arguments in a dream.

As a result, through their excessive love of paradox, they have brought the whole school into disrepute, so that even the questions they raise legitimately have come to be disbelieved by people.

And quite apart from their own misjudgment, they have bred in the young such a passion for this sort of thing that it comes about that they give not even a passing thought to ethical and practical arguments—the very ones from which philosophers derive real benefit—but instead waste their lives chasing empty glory in useless and paradoxical feats of argumentation.

## Book 12 Chapter 26d

The same thing has happened to Timaeus too in the field of history, and to his admirers. Being a lover of paradox and combative about whatever thesis he sets up, he has overawed the general public with his arguments and forced them to pay attention to him through the appearance of telling the truth; some he has even drawn over to his side and seems to win round with apparent proof.

He has produced this impression above all by his pronouncements about colonies, the founding of cities, and the kinships between peoples.

In these matters he makes such a display, through his precision and through the bitterness of the refutations he aims at his rivals, that all the other writers seem to have slept through their subject and dealt with the world offhand, while he alone appears to have investigated the exact truth and sorted out the histories in each case—matters in which much is stated soundly, but much also falsely.

Nevertheless, those who have spent a long time steeped in his early books—the ones containing his treatments of the subjects just mentioned—first give full credence to the whole extravagant scale of his claims; but then, when someone shows them that on the very points where Timaeus is most savage toward his rivals he is himself guilty of the same fault (just as we have lately shown him going wrong over the Locrians and the matters that follow),

they turn quarrelsome, contentious, and hard to budge. And one might almost say that those who have applied themselves most diligently to his books carry away just this as the profit of their reading.

As for those who have devoted their attention to his public speeches, and to his discursive set-pieces in general, they turn out puerile, pedantic, and utterly out of touch with the truth, for the reasons just given.

## Book 12 Chapter 27: An Historian Needs Practical Experience

For there being by nature two instruments, as it were, by which we learn and investigate everything—hearing and sight—and sight being not a little the truer, according to Heraclitus (for the eyes are more accurate witnesses than the ears)—of these Timaeus set out to pursue his inquiry by the pleasanter,

but the inferior, of the two roads.

For he abstained entirely from learning through sight, and laid claim to learning through hearing. And this latter being itself of two kinds—one through written records *** —he handled the part concerning personal interrogation carelessly, as we have shown above.

The reason he made this choice is easy to grasp: matters drawn from books can be investigated without danger or hardship, if one only takes the single precaution of securing either a city that possesses an abundance of records or a library somewhere close at hand.

After that, one need only recline and search out the question at issue and compare the errors of earlier writers without any hardship at all.

Personal inquiry, on the other hand, demands much toil and expense, yet it contributes greatly and is the most important part of history.

This is clear from the very writers who compose histories. For Ephorus says that, if it were possible for them to be present at all the events, that would be far the best of all kinds of experience;

and Theopompus says that the best in military matters is the man who has taken part in the most battles, and the most capable in speech is the one who has shared in the most political contests.

And the same thing holds in the case of medicine and of steering a ship.

And even more vividly than these writers the poet (Homer) has spoken about this point. For he, wishing to show us what kind of man the man of action ought to be, takes up the figure of Odysseus and speaks somehow as follows:

"Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns, who wandered very far,"

and then continuing,

"and of many men he saw the cities and learned their mind, and many sorrows he suffered in his heart upon the sea,"

and again,

"passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves."

## Book 12 Chapter 27a

It remains, then, that the factual part of his history is built up out of every kind of error, most of which we have already gone through.

We shall now state the cause of the error—one that will not seem credible to most people, but will be found to be the truest of all the charges against Timaeus.

For he does appear to have equipped himself with both a practical command of each subject and a habit of painstaking inquiry, and, in a word, to have come to the writing of history industriously;

and yet in certain respects no one among the writers of repute seems to have been more inexperienced or less painstaking than he. What I mean will become clear from the following.

## Book 12 Chapter 28: Mere Inquiry Is Insufficient

It seems to me that the very dignity of history calls for such a man.

Now Plato says that human affairs will go well only when either philosophers become kings or kings take up philosophy;

and I too would say that history will be in a good state only when either men of action undertake to write histories—

not, as they do now, as a secondary matter, but considering this too to be among their most necessary and finest pursuits, and devoting themselves undistracted *** to this branch of work throughout their lives—

or when those who set out to write come to regard the experience gained from affairs themselves as indispensable for history. Until that happens there will be no end to the ignorance of historians.

Of all this Timaeus took not the slightest forethought,

but having lived out his life in a single place as a resident alien, and having, almost as if on purpose, renounced both active engagement in military and political affairs and the firsthand experience that comes from travel and observation, he somehow or other carries off the reputation of one who lays claim to the foremost rank among writers.

And that he is of this character it is easy to demonstrate from Timaeus' own admissions.

For in the preface to his sixth book he says that some people suppose that the genre of display oratory requires greater natural talent, industry, and preparation than that of history, and that these opinions had earlier been brought against Ephorus;

and since that writer had been unable to answer adequately those who said such things, he himself attempts to set history side by side with display oratory in a comparison—doing the most absurd thing of all,

first in misrepresenting that writer. For Ephorus, admirable throughout his whole work in his style, in his treatment, and in the conception of his material, is at his most masterly in his digressions and in the maxims he contributes of his own—in short, whenever he sets out some supplementary observation;

and, as it happens, it is on this very subject—the comparison of historians with speech-writers—that he has spoken most agreeably and most persuasively.

But Timaeus, so as not to seem to be following Ephorus, on top of misrepresenting him has at the same time condemned the rest as well: for, expounding at length, obscurely, and in every way worse the matters that had been properly handled by others, [he leaves none of them...] *** [the text breaks off]

## Book 12 Chapter 28a

...of living men he supposed he would be able to observe this. All the same, in his eagerness to exalt the writing of history, he says first that the difference between history and rhetorical display-pieces is as great as the difference between buildings really constructed and furnished and the scenes and settings that merely appear in stage-paintings.

Second, he says that the mere gathering of the materials for history is a greater task than the whole business of composing display-pieces.

He himself, at any rate, claims to have gone to such expense and trouble in collecting the records of the Cyrnians (Corsicans) and in researching the customs of the Ligurians and the Celts, and along with these the Iberians, that he could scarcely have believed it possible himself, nor would others be believed if they were to describe these efforts.

One would gladly put this question to the historian: which does he suppose requires greater expense and hardship—to sit in a city collecting records and researching the customs of the Ligurians and the Celts, or to put most of the nations and places to the test and become an eyewitness oneself?

And again, which is the harder: to inquire about pitched battles, sieges, and naval engagements from those who happened to be present in the moments of danger, or to gain firsthand experience of the perils and of all that goes with them in the actual operations themselves?

For my own part, I do not think that real buildings differ from the scenes in stage-paintings, or history from display-speeches, by as much as, in every kind of composition, an account based on personal action and personal experience differs from one written up from hearsay and report.

Being utterly inexperienced in this, he naturally assumed that what is the smallest and easiest task of all is the greatest and most difficult for those engaged in writing history—I mean the collecting of records and the questioning of people who know each set of facts.

And yet in just this part the inexperienced are bound to go badly astray; for how can a man question people properly about a pitched battle, a siege, or a naval engagement, and how can he follow the details of those describing them, when he has no conception of the things in question?

For the inquirer contributes no less to the account than those who report it; the very prompting about the circumstances that attend the events serves to lead the narrator by the hand to each of the things that happened.

In all this the inexperienced man is neither capable of cross-examining those who were present, nor, even when he is there alongside them, of understanding what is going on; rather, even when he is present, in a way, though present, he is not really present at all.
