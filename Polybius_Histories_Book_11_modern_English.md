# Polybius — The Histories, Book XI

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 11 Chapter 1: Death of Hasdrubal

Hasdrubal's arrival in Italy proved much easier and quicker than had been expected.

So Rome was never at any other time more tense or more terrified, as it waited to see what would happen.

None of this pleased Hasdrubal; but since circumstances no longer allowed any withdrawal, because he saw the enemy already drawn up and advancing, he was forced to form his Iberians and the Gauls who were with him in battle order.

He placed the ten elephants in front, deepened his lines, compressed his whole force into a narrow space, and stationed himself in the center of the formation under the protection of the elephants, advancing against the enemy's left with the settled resolve that in the present danger he must either win or die.

Livius advanced against the enemy in earnest and fought bravely once the armies had joined.

Claudius, who had been posted on the right wing, could neither move forward nor outflank the opposition because of the difficult ground in front, and it was in reliance on this ground that Hasdrubal had made his attack on the Roman left.

Unable to act and troubled by his inaction, he learned from the situation itself what he ought to do.

So he took the troops from his own right wing, marched them behind the battle line, passed beyond the Roman left, and attacked the Carthaginians near the elephants on the flank.

Up to this point victory hung in the balance. Both sides fought with equal desperation, because the Romans knew that if they failed they had no hope of safety, while the Iberians and Carthaginians were in the same case; and the elephants served both sides alike in the battle.

Caught in the middle and pelted with missiles, they threw both the Roman and the Iberian lines into confusion.

But as soon as Claudius' men fell on the enemy from the rear, the battle was no longer equal, since the Iberians were being pressed from the front by some and from behind by others.

As a result, most of the Iberians were cut down where they stood in the very crisis of the fight.

Six of the elephants fell together with their riders, and the other four, after breaking through the ranks, were later captured by themselves, stripped of their Indian drivers.

## Book 11 Chapter 1a

Some may perhaps ask why in this book I have not prefaced the narrative with summaries, as earlier writers did, but have instead set out an introduction to the events for each Olympiad.

In my judgment the use of summaries is indeed valuable. They help readers understand, attract and encourage those who come upon the work, and make it easy to find whatever one is looking for.

But since I see that this kind of prefatory summary is often neglected and loses its force for many reasons, I was led to adopt this other method.

For the introductory survey is not merely as useful as a summary but can do even more, and it also has a safer place because it is woven into the history itself.

Therefore I decided to use this method throughout the whole work, except in the first six books. In those I used summaries, because that sort of introduction did not fit them very well.

## Book 11 Chapter 2: Hasdrubal's Conduct in His Last Battle

Hasdrubal showed himself a brave man both before this and in his final hour, and he ended his life fighting hand to hand. That deserves notice and should not be passed over in silence.

That he was Hannibal's own brother, and that when Hannibal went to Italy he entrusted the affairs in Iberia to him, I have already explained earlier.

I have also shown how he fought many struggles against the Romans and wrestled with many varied difficulties because of the generals sent out from Carthage to Iberia, and how through all these reverses and setbacks he continued to bear himself nobly and with courage worthy of the son of Barca. But now I will speak of his last contests,

for in these above all he seems to me to deserve close attention and even imitation.

For one may observe that most generals and kings, when they undertake decisive struggles, keep before their eyes only the glory and advantage to be won by success, and often pause to consider how they will use each success if events turn out as they wish.

But they do not set before themselves the consequences of failure, nor do they consider what each must do in a reversal. Yet the first is easy, while the second requires much foresight.

Therefore most men, through weakness of spirit and poor judgment in such moments, make their defeats shameful, even when their soldiers have often fought bravely; they disgrace what they had accomplished before and leave behind a life marked by reproach.

Since many leaders fail precisely in this point, and since the difference between one man and another here is very great, anyone who wishes can learn it readily; for past history has provided many examples.

Hasdrubal, by contrast, so long as any reasonable hope remained that he could do something worthy of his earlier achievements, took no greater thought in danger than for his own preservation.

But when Fortune had shut him in at the last extremity and stripped away every hope for the future, then, though he omitted nothing either in preparation or in battle that might secure victory, he took just as much thought for how, if he failed, he might confront the event in a way worthy of all that he had done before and endure nothing unworthy of his former life.

Let these remarks stand for those engaged in action, so that they may neither, by reckless courting of danger, ruin the hopes of those who trusted them, nor, by clinging to life beyond what is honorable, make their disasters shameful.

## Book 11 Chapter 3: The Romans Celebrate a Victory

After winning the battle, the Romans at once plundered the enemy camp, and they slaughtered many of the Celts in their sleeping-places, drunk and helpless like sacrificial victims.

They also gathered the rest of the captured booty, from which more than three hundred talents were brought into the public treasury.

Of the Carthaginians and Celts together, not fewer than ten thousand fell in the battle, while about two thousand Romans died. Some distinguished Carthaginians were taken alive, but the rest were destroyed.

When the report reached Rome, at first they could not believe it, so strongly did they want it to be true.

But when more messengers arrived, making clear not only that the event had happened but the details of it as well, then the city was filled with overwhelming joy, every sacred precinct was adorned, and every temple was crowded with cakes and sacrificial victims.

In general they were brought to such confidence and courage that all thought Hannibal, whom they had feared more than anything before, could no longer even remain in Italy.

Yet another declared that such words had only the appearance of truth, while the reality was otherwise—indeed the opposite.

## Book 11 Chapter 4: A Plea For Union In Greece

"I think the facts themselves have made it clear, men of Aetolia, that neither King Ptolemy nor the city of Rhodes nor the peoples of Byzantium, Chios, and Mytilene treat a reconciliation with you as a minor matter.

For this is not the first time or the second that we have spoken to you about peace. Ever since you began this war, we have sat by you, seized every opportunity, and never stopped reminding you of these things,

having in view for the present the destruction of both yourselves and the Macedonians, and for the future the safety of our own countries and of the rest of the Greeks.

For just as with a fire, once someone has set the material alight, what follows is no longer under his control, but the blaze takes whatever course chance gives it, governed mostly by the winds and by the destruction of the fuel beneath it, and often in an irrational way rushes back upon the very man who first kindled it,

so too war, once some people have set it blazing, sometimes destroys them first and sometimes sweeps onward unjustly ruining all that lies in its path, always finding new fuel and being fanned, as if by winds, by the ignorance of neighboring peoples.

Therefore, men of Aetolia, since the islanders as a whole and the Greeks who dwell in Asia have sent us here to beg you to put an end to the war and choose peace, because what is happening concerns us as well, come to your senses, feel shame, and listen to those who urge you.

If by some chance you were fighting a war that was unprofitable, as most wars are, but still glorious both in its original purpose and in the reputation likely to follow from it, perhaps someone might forgive your eagerness.

But if it is the most disgraceful of all wars, full of dishonor and slander, does the matter not require the greatest reflection?

I will speak openly about what I think, and if you are wise you will bear it calmly.

For it is far better to be reproved at the right moment and be saved than, by hearing pleasing words, soon to perish yourselves and drag the rest of the Greeks down with you."

## Book 11 Chapter 5: Dangers of the Treaty With Rome

"Set your own blindness before your eyes. You claim to be making war on Philip for the sake of the Greeks, so that, being safe, they may not have to do what he commands; but in reality you are making war for the enslavement and ruin of Greece.

That is what your treaty with the Romans says—a treaty which formerly existed only on paper, but now is seen taking shape in events themselves.

At first the written terms merely brought shame on you; now by your actions that shame is made visible to everyone.

Philip is now only a name and a pretext for the war, for he is suffering no great harm. He has as allies most of the Peloponnesians, the Boeotians, Euboeans, Phocians, Locrians, Thessalians, and Epirotes; and it is against these peoples that you have made your treaty,

on the condition that their persons and movable goods belong to the Romans, while their cities and land belong to the Aetolians.

If you yourselves captured a city, you would not tolerate insulting free people or burning their towns, because you regard such conduct as cruel and barbaric.

Yet you have made treaties by which you have handed over all the other Greeks to barbarians for the most shameful outrages and lawless acts.

Formerly this was not understood. But now, because of the people of Oreus and the wretched Aeginetans, you have become plain to all, as if Fortune had deliberately set your folly up on a public stage.

Such are the origins of the war and such the things already happening. And if everything now turns out exactly as you wish, what sort of end should one expect? Surely the beginning of great evils for all the Greeks?"

## Book 11 Chapter 6: Philip In Aetolia Again

"For anyone can see that if the Romans put an end to the war in Italy—and that moment is close at hand now that Hannibal has been shut up within a very narrow corner of Bruttium—

they will next hurl their full force at Greece, claiming in words that they are helping the Aetolians against Philip, but in truth intending to bring everything there under their own power. I think this is perfectly plain.

And whether, once masters, they govern well or badly, the credit and title will belong to them if they act well, while if they act badly, the profits from those they ruin and the authority over those they spare will likewise belong to them.

Then you will call the gods to witness, when neither any god will still wish nor any human being be able to help you.

Perhaps from the beginning you ought to have foreseen all this,

for that would have been fitting for you. But since many future events escape human foresight, now at least, after seeing from these things what happens, you ought to take better counsel about the future.

In any case, for our part, we have left undone nothing that true friends ought to say or do in the present, and we have spoken our mind frankly about what is coming.

We ask and urge you not to begrudge either yourselves or the rest of the Greeks their freedom and safety."

This speech seemed to produce some change of mind among the majority. After him came the ambassadors from Philip, who passed over the arguments point by point and said they had come with two instructions.

If the Aetolians chose peace, they were to accept it at once; but if not, they were to call the gods and the ambassadors then present from the other Greek states to witness, and to depart after declaring that for whatever should happen afterward to the Greeks the Aetolians, not Philip, must be held responsible.

## Book 11 Chapter 7: Philip Vandalizes Thermus

Philip bitterly lamented that he had very nearly failed to get Attalus into his hands.

Then, after marching to Lake Trichonis and arriving at Thermus, where there was a sanctuary of Apollo, he defaced all the offerings that he had left untouched before, showing bad judgment in the former case and bad temper in the latter.

For when a man is angry with human beings and in his anger becomes impious toward the divine, that is a sign of utter irrationality.

## Book 11 Chapter 8: Faults of the Achaean Officers

Since there are three methods by which all sensible men who apply themselves to generalship try to attain it—first by memoirs and the preparation derived from them,

second by systematic instruction and the teaching handed down by experienced men, and third by practice and familiarity in real operations—

the Achaean commanders were in general ignorant of all three.

Most of them were infected with an unhappy rivalry produced by the boastfulness and bad taste of others.

They made a great point of their entourages and their clothing, and among most of them there was an extravagance that outstripped their means of living. But they paid not the slightest attention to their weapons.

For most people do not even try to imitate the actual achievements of successful men, but by admiring their side habits they merely put their own lack of judgment on display to their own harm.

## Book 11 Chapter 9: Philopoemen in the Peloponnese, B. C. 207

He said that brilliance in armor contributes greatly to striking fear into one's opponents, and that having weapons properly fitted is of great practical use.

The best course would be this: give your weapons the care you now lavish on your clothing, and transfer to your clothing the neglect you now show toward your arms.

If you do this, you will both improve your private way of life and be able, by common agreement, to preserve the public interest.

So, he said, when a man goes out to drill or to campaign and is fastening on his greaves, he ought to consider more carefully that they fit well and shine brightly than that his shoes and sandals do.

And when he takes up shield, cuirass, and helmet, he should make sure that these are cleaner and finer than his cloak and tunic.

For wherever things meant merely for display are preferred to things meant for use, what will happen in danger is obvious from the start.

In general he insisted that adornment in clothing belongs to women, and not even to respectable women, whereas splendor and dignity in armor belong to good men who choose to save themselves and their cities honorably.

Everyone present approved what he said so strongly and admired the sense of his exhortation that, as soon as they left the council chamber, they immediately pointed out those who were overdressed and forced some of them to withdraw from the marketplace.

And later, in drills and campaigns alike, they watched themselves still more carefully in the matters he had mentioned.

## Book 11 Chapter 10: Philopoemen and Machanidas

So it is that a single timely word spoken by a man who deserves trust often not only turns people away from the worst courses but also urges them on to the finest.

And when the man who exhorts others also presents his own life as consistent with his words, then that advice necessarily carries the highest conviction. This was especially true in the case of that man.

For in dress and food he was plain and simple, and the same was true of his care for the body and of his dealings with others: he was easy to approach and free from pretension.

Above all, he made the greatest effort throughout his life to tell the truth. So even when he spoke briefly and without ornament, he left a powerful conviction in his hearers.

By presenting his own life as an example in every matter, he made his listeners need very few words.

Accordingly he often swept away long speeches, which his political opponents thought well argued, with only a few remarks, simply because of the trust he inspired and the way facts themselves supported him.

So on this occasion too, when the deliberation had ended, all went back to their cities having formed an exceptional opinion both of his words and of the man himself, believing that with him in command no serious disaster could befall them.

Philopoemen at once began visiting the cities, making the tour energetically and with earnest purpose.

Then he gathered the young men, trained them, organized them, and after no more than eight months spent in this preparation and practice, assembled his forces at Mantinea to contend with the tyrant for the freedom of all the Peloponnesians.

## Book 11 Chapter 11: Arrangement of Forces at Mantinea

Machanidas had grown confident and thought the Achaean advance turned out exactly as he could have wished. As soon as he learned that they had assembled at Mantinea,

he addressed the Spartans at Tegea as the situation required, and on the very next day, just at dawn, advanced against Mantinea, himself leading the right wing of the phalanx.

He marched his mercenaries on either side of the van in parallel columns, and behind them came teams carrying a large quantity of engines and catapult missiles.

At the same time Philopoemen led his army out of Mantinea, having divided it into three parts: by the road from the sanctuary of Poseidon he took the Illyrians and armored troops, together with all the mercenaries and light-armed men; by the next gate, toward the west, the phalanx; and by the next after that the citizen cavalry.

He first seized with his light troops the hill in front of the city, which rises considerably above the road called Xenis and the sanctuary just mentioned. Next he stationed the armored troops, extending the line southward, and beside them he placed the Illyrians.

After these, continuing in the same straight line, he set the phalanx in companies with intervals between them along the ditch that runs toward the Poseidium through the middle of the Mantinean plain and joins the mountains that border the territory of the Elisphasians.

Next to them on the right wing he stationed the Achaean cavalry under Aristaenetus of Dyme, while he himself held the entire mercenary force on the left in successive ranks.

## Book 11 Chapter 12: Battle of Mantinea

As soon as the enemy force had come plainly into view, he rode along the companies of the phalanx, urging them on briefly but vividly with reference to the danger before them.

Most of what he said could not be heard, because the good will and trust of the troops drove the multitude into such eagerness and zeal that their answering encouragement resembled inspired frenzy, as they shouted for him to lead them on and be confident.

Still, whenever he found the chance, he tried to make at least this point clear: that the present struggle stood on one side for shameful and degrading slavery, and on the other for glorious freedom that would be remembered forever.

Machanidas at first made a show of charging in column against the enemy's right wing; but when he drew near, he deployed his force by the right, stretched out the line, made his own right equal in length to the Achaean left, and stationed the catapults at intervals in front of the whole army.

Philopoemen saw from this maneuver that Machanidas intended by catapult fire to wound the men in the companies of the phalanx and throw the whole formation into disorder.

So he gave him no time and no breathing-space, but vigorously opened the battle through the Tarentines in the region near the Poseidium, where the ground was level and well suited for cavalry action.

Machanidas, seeing what was happening, was forced to do the same and launch his own Tarentines into action.

## Book 11 Chapter 13: Efficiency of Mercenaries

At first the collision of these troops alone was spirited; but as the light-armed men gradually came to the aid of those under pressure, it very quickly happened that the mercenaries on both sides became thoroughly mixed together.

As the fighting among them became dense and man-to-man on every side, the struggle remained evenly balanced for a long time, so much so that the rest of the forces, watching to see in which direction the dust would turn, were unable to join battle because both sides held their original ground for so long.

But as time went on, the tyrant's mercenaries began to prevail because of their numbers and because long practice had made them handier in action.

This was natural and is indeed what usually happens.

For although the citizens in democracies are generally more eager in military struggles than the civic subjects of tyrants, the mercenaries under monarchs are naturally superior to and more effective than the mercenaries hired by democracies.

Just as in the former case one side fights for freedom and the other for slavery, so among mercenaries one side exerts itself for certain reward while the other knows that victory will obviously bring loss.

For when a democracy has put down those who attacked it, it no longer maintains its freedom by means of mercenaries; but the more a tyranny reaches after greater power, the more mercenaries it needs.

By wronging more people it gains more men who plot against it. In general the security of monarchs rests on the loyalty and force of foreign troops.

## Book 11 Chapter 14: Machanidas Changes his Plan

That is why on this occasion the mercenary force under Machanidas fought with such fury and violence that even the Illyrians and armored troops stationed in reserve behind the Achaean mercenaries could not withstand their attack, but all were driven out and fled headlong toward Mantinea, which was seven stades away.

At that moment what had been doubtful to some became plain and universally acknowledged: in warfare many outcomes are decided by the experience—or again the inexperience—of commanders.

For perhaps it is a great thing to follow up an initial advantage properly, but it is a far greater thing, after failing in one's first attempt, to remain master of oneself, notice the lack of judgment of the apparent victors, and then strike at their mistakes.

Indeed one may often see those who seem already to have gained the advantage soon afterward ruined in the whole affair, while those who seemed at first to have stumbled later, thanks to their own quick intelligence, recover and win a surprising success.

That, in fact, now seemed to have happened clearly in the case of both commanders.

For when the entire mercenary force had broken the Achaeans and the left wing had collapsed, Machanidas abandoned the position that lay before him and gave up the plan of outflanking the enemy on one side while meeting them in front on the other.

Instead, in a loss of self-control and with the impulsiveness of a young man, he poured forward with his own mercenaries and pressed hard after the fugitives, as though fear itself were not enough to drive men who had once given way all the way to the gates.

## Book 11 Chapter 15: Defeat of the Lacedaemonians

The Achaean general, for as long as he could, kept trying to hold the mercenaries in place, calling the officers by name and urging them on.

But when he saw them being overwhelmed, he neither fled in panic nor withdrew in despair. Instead he drew back under cover of the wing of the phalanx, and when the pursuers had gone past and the ground where the struggle had been was left vacant, he immediately ordered the front companies of the phalanx to wheel with locked shields and advance at the double while keeping their ranks.

By swiftly occupying the abandoned position he both cut off the pursuers and at the same time found himself beyond the enemy's wing.

He urged the phalangites to be bold and stand fast until he gave the signal for the general advance.

And he ordered Polybius the Megalopolitan to gather the remaining Illyrians, armored troops, and mercenaries who had not joined in the flight, to form a reserve on the flank of the phalanx, and to watch for the return of those coming back from the pursuit.

The Spartans, however, without waiting for orders and elated in spirit by the success of the light troops, lowered their sarissas and rushed on the enemy.

But when in the course of this advance they reached the edge of the ditch, the moment no longer allowed them to change their minds and turn back with enemies already close at hand; and besides, despising the ditch because the descent into it had long been gradual and there was no water at the bottom and no wild growth in it, they charged through it without proper thought.

## Book 11 Chapter 16: Philopoemen Seizes an Opportunity

At that very moment when the long-anticipated opportunity appeared against the enemy, Philopoemen ordered all the phalangites to level their sarissas and charge.

The Achaeans advanced with one spirit and with a terrifying shout. The Spartan ranks, already broken during the descent into the ditch, lost courage as they tried to climb out and found the enemy above them.

Many turned to flight, while the greater number perished in the ditch itself, some at the hands of the Achaeans and others at the hands of their own men.

This result did not come by mere chance or from the terrain alone, but from the intelligence of the commander, who had deliberately taken position behind the ditch.

For Philopoemen had not done so to avoid battle, as some supposed, but because he had calculated each point with exactness and true generalship. He reasoned that when Machanidas arrived, if he advanced his force without foreseeing the ditch, then his phalanx would suffer exactly what in fact now happened to it.

But if, noticing the awkwardness of the ditch, he changed his mind, seemed to lose heart, and broke off while extended in line so as to give himself a long withdrawal on the march, then victory would still fall to Philopoemen and the opposite to his enemy, even without a full battle.

This has already happened to many commanders who drew up for battle and then judged themselves not fit to fight their opponents,

either because of the ground, or because of inferior numbers, or for other reasons, and who while withdrawing in extended order hoped either to gain an advantage by means of their rear guard or at least to disengage safely from the enemy.

In just such situations it is the commanders who are most often exposed to the gravest danger.

## Book 11 Chapter 17: Army of Machanidas Put to Flight

Philopoemen, however, was not mistaken in his foresight of what would happen, for the Spartans were routed with a severe defeat.

Seeing that the phalanx was winning and that affairs in general were moving splendidly in his favor, he now pressed on to complete what remained of the whole design: to make sure that Machanidas did not escape.

Knowing that, in the course of the pursuit, Machanidas had been cut off with his own mercenaries in the area between the ditch and the city, he waited for his appearance.

Machanidas, seeing as he broke off from the pursuit that his army was fleeing and realizing that he had rushed too far forward and lost all hope for the campaign, immediately tried to gather the mercenaries around him into a compact body and break through the scattered pursuers.

Some at first stayed with him, seeing his plan and believing that this was their only hope of safety.

But when they arrived and saw that the Achaeans were holding the bridge over the ditch, they at once lost heart, drifted away from him, and each man began to seek his own safety.

Then the tyrant, despairing of the way over the bridge, rode along beside the ditch, searching vigorously for some crossing-place.

## Book 11 Chapter 18: Fall of Machanidas

Philopoemen recognized Machanidas by his purple cloak and the trappings of his horse. He left Anaxidamus and his men behind, urging them to guard the passage carefully and spare none of the mercenaries, since these were always the men who strengthened the tyrannies in Sparta.

He himself took with him Polyaenus of Cyparissia and Simias, who were then acting as his shield-bearers, and rode on the opposite side of the ditch parallel to the tyrant and those with him.

At that moment only two men were still with Machanidas: Arexidamus and one mercenary.

As soon as Machanidas found a place in the ditch where crossing seemed possible, put spurs to his horse, and forced it over, Philopoemen wheeled sharply against him, struck him a decisive blow with the spear, and then dealt another with the butt-spike, killing the tyrant outright in hand-to-hand combat.

Those riding with Philopoemen did much the same to Arexidamus. The third man, despairing of the crossing, escaped danger at the moment the others were killed.

When both had fallen, Simias and his party at once stripped the bodies, and taking with the armor the tyrant's head as well, hurried off to the pursuers.

They were eager to show the troops the destruction of the enemy commander, so that once convinced they might press the pursuit of their opponents still more boldly and confidently all the way to Tegea.

This indeed contributed greatly to the charge of the troops; for not least because of it they captured Tegea by assault, and on the following days camped by the Eurotas as undisputed masters of the countryside.

After being unable for many years to drive their enemies out of their own land, they now themselves ravaged all Laconia without fear.

They had lost only a small number of their own men in the battle, but had killed not fewer than four thousand Spartans, captured even more than that alive, and taken possession of all the baggage and arms.

## Book 11 Chapter 18a

As Polybius says, what use is it for readers to go through wars and battles, the enslavement of cities, and sieges, if they do not understand the causes by which in each case some succeeded and others failed?

For the outcomes of actions merely entertain those who hear of them, whereas the analysis of the plans formed beforehand, when properly examined, benefits those who love learning.

And above all, a detailed presentation of the handling of each event helps to correct the judgment of attentive readers.

## Book 11 Chapter 19: The Hannibalian War Continued

Who would not pause to admire the man's leadership, courage, and power in the field, if he reflects on the length of this period,

and considers the battles both general and particular, the sieges, the changes of cities, the shifting circumstances, and the sweep of the whole design and action,

within which Hannibal, while waging war against the Romans in Italy for sixteen continuous years, never once dismissed his forces from the open field, but kept such great multitudes under his control, like a skilled helmsman, free from mutiny either against himself or among one another, though the army he used was not merely of different nations but not even of the same stock.

For he had Libyans, Iberians, Ligurians, Celts, Phoenicians, Italians, and Greeks, people who by nature shared no law, no custom, no language, and nothing else in common with each other.

And yet the commander's intelligence made such vast and varied differences obey a single order and submit to one purpose, though the circumstances were far from simple and were highly changeable, with Fortune often blowing splendidly in their favor and at other times the reverse.

From this anyone may justly admire the commander's ability in this respect and confidently say that, if he had begun elsewhere in the world and only attacked the Romans last, none of the objectives he set before himself would have escaped him.

As it was, he began with those whom he ought to have attacked last, and with them he both began and ended his career.

## Book 11 Chapter 20: Scipio in Spain, After the Battle of the Metaurus

The forces under Hasdrubal, having gathered the army from the cities where they had spent the winter, advanced and encamped not far from the city called Ilipa, placing their camp at the foot of the hills with open ground in front well suited for battle.

They had seventy thousand infantry, four thousand cavalry, and more than thirty elephants—thirty-two in all.

Publius sent Marcus Junius to Colichas to take over the forces prepared by him, namely three thousand infantry and five hundred cavalry. He himself kept gathering the rest of the allied contingents as he advanced toward his objective.

When he came near Castulo and the region about Baecula, and there joined Marcus and the troops from Colichas, he fell into great uncertainty about the situation.

For without the allies his Roman troops were not strong enough to risk a pitched battle, but to risk everything while depending on allied troops for the final outcome seemed dangerous and very rash.

Still, though perplexed and hemmed in by circumstances, he was driven to make use of the Iberians in such a way that they would create an appearance to the enemy,

while the real contest would be carried by his own camp forces. With this plan in mind he marched out with his whole army, having forty-five thousand infantry and about three thousand cavalry.

When he came near the Carthaginians and was within sight, he encamped on some low ridges directly opposite the enemy.

## Book 11 Chapter 21: Futile Attack by Mago

Mago, thinking the moment favorable for an attack while the Romans were making camp, took most of his own cavalry and Masannasan with the Numidians and rode against the encampment, convinced that he would catch Publius off guard.

But Scipio had long foreseen what was coming and had stationed cavalry equal in number to the Carthaginian horse under cover of a hill.

When they charged unexpectedly, many of the enemy wheeled round at once in panic because of the surprise and fell away, while the rest engaged the enemy and fought bravely.

But the Carthaginians were at a disadvantage because of the superior agility of the Roman horsemen coming downhill, and after a short resistance, with many losses, they gave way.

At first they conducted their retreat in order, but when the Romans pressed them they broke up their squadrons and fled under their own camp.

As a result the Romans became more confident for the coming battle, while the Carthaginians experienced the opposite.

Even so, during the next few days both sides drew out their forces on the plain between the camps, skirmished with cavalry and light troops, tested one another, and then moved to decide the whole matter.

## Book 11 Chapter 22: Defeat of Hasdrubal Son of Gesco

On this occasion Publius seems to have used two stratagems.

For he observed that Hasdrubal was late in bringing out his army and regularly placed the Libyans in the center with the elephants in front of both wings, while he himself was accustomed to take the field somewhat later,

putting the Romans in the center opposite the Libyans and placing the Iberians on the wings. But on the day when he decided to bring matters to a decision, by reversing these arrangements he gave great help toward victory to his own forces and placed the enemy at no small disadvantage.

At first light he sent his attendants out and ordered all the military tribunes and soldiers to eat breakfast, arm themselves, and assemble outside the palisade.

When this had been done, and all obeyed eagerly because they guessed what was coming, he first sent forward the cavalry and light-armed troops with instructions to approach the enemy camp and begin skirmishing boldly.

He himself followed with the infantry just as the sun was rising, and when he reached the middle of the plain he drew them up in the reverse of his usual order, placing the Iberians in the center and the Roman troops on the wings.

The Carthaginians, with cavalry suddenly pressing close to the camp and the rest of the army already deploying in sight, scarcely had time to arm.

Therefore Hasdrubal and his officers were compelled, while their men were still unfed and unprepared, to send their cavalry and light troops out at once into the plain against the opposing cavalry, and to form up their infantry not far from the foothills on the level ground, as was their custom.

For some time the Romans remained still. But as the day advanced, and the struggle among the light troops remained indecisive because the men under pressure kept retreating to their own phalanxes and then turning again to fight,

Publius at that point withdrew the skirmishers through the intervals between the standards, divided them behind the battle line between the two wings, first placing the javelin-fighters and then the cavalry after them, and began the advance at first head-on.

When he had come to within about four stades of the enemy, he ordered the Iberians to keep advancing in the same formation.

## Book 11 Chapter 23: Scipio Outflanks the Enemy

He ordered the right wing to wheel outward to the right and the left wing the opposite way. He himself took from the right wing the three leading squadrons of cavalry, while Lucius Marcius and Marcus Junius took the corresponding force from the left, and in front of these they placed the usual javelin-fighters and three companies of infantry—this formation of infantry the Romans call a cohort.

The men on one side wheeled shieldwise and those on the other spearwise, then advanced straight at the enemy with great energy, while the units behind successively moved up and followed the turning movement.

When these troops had come to within no great distance of the enemy, while the Iberians in the frontal line still remained a considerable way off because they were advancing at a walking pace, the Roman forces struck both enemy wings at once exactly as Scipio had intended from the start.

The later movements, by which the troops following behind came up and formed on the same straight line with those in front and joined battle with the enemy, were opposite to one another in their arrangement, the right wing being the reverse of the left, and within each wing the infantry of the cavalry.

For on the right wing the cavalry and light troops came up spearwise and tried to outflank the enemy, while the infantry came into line the other way.

On the left wing, by contrast, the companies advanced spearwise, while the cavalry with the javelin-men came up from the rein side.

Thus by this movement the right of the cavalry and light troops on both wings effectively became their left.

The general paid little attention to that lesser point and concentrated on the greater one, namely the outflanking movement, and he reasoned correctly.

For a commander must understand what is happening, but he must use whatever movements suit the occasion.

## Book 11 Chapter 24: Carthaginians Driven From Spain

Once these troops had closed, the elephants, being pelted with javelins and thrown into confusion on all sides by the skirmishers and cavalry, suffered badly and did no less harm to friends than to enemies, since in their blind rushes they kept trampling and killing whoever came under them on either side.

As for the infantry, the Carthaginian wings were being broken, while the center, where the Libyans stood and which was the most useful part of the army, remained inactive to the end.

For they could neither leave their own post to go aid the wings because of the Iberian advance, nor, by staying where they were, could they do anything effective, since the enemy opposite them did not come to close quarters.

Even so, for a time the wings fought bravely, because on both sides the struggle concerned everything.

But by now the midday heat was at its height, and the Carthaginians were giving way because they had not taken the field when they themselves intended and had been prevented from making the proper preparations.

The Romans, by contrast, had the advantage both in strength and in morale, especially because through the general's foresight their most effective troops had been matched against the weakest of the enemy.

At first Hasdrubal's men, pressed step by step, retired in order. Then they gave way in a body and withdrew toward the foothills, and when the Romans pressed still harder they fled headlong into the camp.

If some god had not aided their escape, they would have been driven out of the camp at once.

But an extraordinary storm gathered in the sky, and when a violent and unbroken downpour burst upon them, the Romans only with difficulty got back to their own camp.

Many Romans lost their lives in the fire while trying to recover the silver and gold that had melted and run together.

## Book 11 Chapter 24a

When everyone was congratulating Publius after he had driven the Carthaginians out of Iberia, and was urging him to rest and take his ease on the ground that he had brought the war to an end, he said that he counted them fortunate for having such hopes,

but that he himself was now more than ever considering how he should begin the war against the Carthaginians.

For until now, he said, the Carthaginians had been making war on the Romans, but now Fortune had handed the Romans the chance to carry the war over against the Carthaginians.

And when Publius spoke with Sophax, being naturally gifted in this respect, he dealt with him so graciously and skillfully that Hasdrubal said to Sophax on the following days that Publius had seemed more formidable to him in conversation than in arms.

## Book 11 Chapter 25: Scipio Suppresses A Mutiny in Spain

When a mutiny broke out among some of the Roman camp, Publius, though he already had plenty of experience in affairs, nevertheless found himself in greater perplexity and difficulty than ever before. And this was only natural.

For just as in the case of bodies, external causes of harm—such as cold, heat, fatigue, or wounds—can be guarded against before they occur and are easy to treat once they do occur, while the swellings and diseases that arise from the body itself are both hard to foresee and hard to cure,

so we must think about constitutions and armies in the same way.

Against attacks and wars from outside, the means of preparation and defense are easily at hand for those who pay attention,

but against rivalries, factional uprisings, and disturbances arising within, the remedy is difficult and demands great dexterity and exceptional quickness of mind.

Still, there is one rule that, in my opinion, suits armies, cities, and bodies alike.

It is this: never allow any of these to remain idle and inactive for long, least of all in times of prosperity and when supplies are abundant.

But Publius, being, as I said from the beginning, exceptionally careful, sharp-minded, and energetic, gathered the tribunes and proposed some such solution to the present troubles as follows.

He said that they must promise the soldiers payment of their arrears, and in order to make the promise credible they must now openly and zealously collect the contributions already assessed on the cities for the maintenance of the whole army, as though the money were being gathered for the settlement of the pay.

The same tribunes were then to go back and ask and urge the mutineers to lay aside their folly and come to him to receive their rations and pay, whether they wished to do so in groups or all together.

Once that was done, he said, they could then decide at the proper moment what action ought to be taken.

## Book 11 Chapter 26: Scipio's Plan to Punish the Rebels

With this in mind they turned their attention to the matter of money.

When the tribunes had explained what had been decided, Publius asked the council what ought to be done next.

They resolved to announce a day on which the mutineers must appear, to grant terms to the mass of the men, but to punish the guilty leaders severely; these numbered thirty-five.

When the appointed day arrived and the rebels came in for the settlement and the payment of their arrears,

Publius secretly instructed the tribunes who had gone as envoys to meet the mutineers, and for each man to single out five of the ringleaders, greet them kindly as soon as they met, and invite them to their own quarters if possible, or at least to dinner or some similar gathering.

To the army with him he gave orders to prepare rations for a longer expedition within three days, as though they were to march with Marcus against Andobales.

This news made the mutineers more confident, for they supposed that power would lie chiefly in their own hands once they joined the general, since the other camps would then be separated from him.

## Book 11 Chapter 27: Suppression of the Mutiny

As the mutineers drew near the city, orders were given to the rest of the soldiers to march out at dawn on the next day with full equipment.

But the tribunes and prefects were instructed that, after this first movement, they should lay down the baggage, keep the soldiers in arms at the gate, divide them among all the gates, and take care that none of the mutineers got out.

Those detailed to meet them accordingly joined the newcomers and, following their instructions, courteously led away those who were responsible.

These officers had also been told that, once the men had eaten dinner, they were to seize the thirty-five, bind them, and keep them under guard, allowing no one from inside to go out except the messenger who was to report to the general from each group that the deed had been done.

When the tribunes had carried out their orders, the general at daybreak on the following day saw that the new arrivals were gathered in the marketplace and summoned the assembly.

As all ran together in the usual way at the signal, their minds were suspended in uncertainty, wondering what sort of appearance the general would present and what they would hear from him about the situation.

Publius then sent orders to the tribunes at the gates to bring the soldiers up under arms and surround the assembly, while he himself came forward and at his very first appearance threw them all into confusion.

For most of them still supposed him to be ill, and when, contrary to expectation, they suddenly saw him appearing strong and vigorous, they were struck with terror.

## Book 11 Chapter 28: Scipio Harangues the Mutinous Troops

He began his speech in something like these terms. He said he was amazed by what grievance had displeased them or what hopes had elated them so far as to attempt secession.

For there are three causes, he said, for which men dare to revolt against their country and their leaders: either they blame and resent those in authority, or they are dissatisfied with their present circumstances, or else, by Zeus, they reach after greater and fairer hopes.

"Now I ask which of these applies to you. Plainly, I suppose, you are angry with me because your rations and pay were not being given you.

But that was not a charge against me, for during my command none of your pay was lacking.

If the fault came from Rome, because old arrears were not now being settled—

was it right to bring this complaint by becoming deserters from your fatherland and enemies of the power that reared you, or to come to me in person, state the matter, and ask your friends to help and support you?

I think the latter was better. For men serving some master for wages may perhaps sometimes be excused for revolting against those who pay them, but men fighting for themselves and for their own wives and children cannot be excused at all.

It is much the same as if a man, claiming that his own father had wronged him in money matters, should come with arms to kill the one from whom he received life itself.

Or perhaps I laid upon you more hardships and dangers than on the others, while distributing the advantages and rewards more generously to others?

But you do not dare to say that, and even if you dared, you would not be able to prove it.

What then is the grievance for which you are dissatisfied with us and have now made this secession? That is what I want to learn, though I think none of you will either speak it or even conceive it."

## Book 11 Chapter 29: Scipio's Speech Continued

"No, not one of you. Nor can it be that you are troubled by the state of affairs. When has fortune flowed more favorably? When has Rome won greater advantages? When have those serving in the field had larger hopes than now?

But perhaps one of the faint-hearted will say that among our enemies the benefits appeared greater and their hopes larger and more secure. Among which enemies, then?

Among Andobales and Mandonius? And which of you does not know that these men first broke faith with the Carthaginians and revolted to us, and now, after once again violating their oaths and pledges, have made themselves our enemies?

A fine thing indeed—to become enemies of your own fatherland by trusting such men as these.

Nor did you place your hopes in yourselves, as though you would become masters of Iberia. For even if you had stood in line with Andobales, you were not strong enough to risk battle against us, much less if you had stood alone.

What then did you rely on? Was it perhaps the experience and courage of the leaders you have now chosen, or even the rods and axes carried before them? About these men it is not honorable even to say more.

No, my men, none of these things can serve you, nor can you state the slightest just complaint either against us or against your country.

Therefore I will make your defense before Rome and before myself on the basis of what all mankind agrees is just.

And that is this: every crowd is easily misled and easily led into anything. That is why the multitude and the sea are always liable to the same condition.

For just as the sea in its own nature is harmless to those who use it and remains at rest, but when violent winds fall upon it it seems to sailors to take on the character of the winds that whip it round,

so too the crowd always appears and becomes, for those dealing with it, whatever sort of leaders and advisers it happens to have.

Therefore I and all the officers of the camp now make peace with you and give our pledge that we will bear no grudge.

But toward those who were responsible we remain utterly unreconciled and hold that they must be punished as their offenses against their country and against us deserve."

## Book 11 Chapter 30: Execution of the Ringleaders

Just as he was saying these things, the soldiers standing in arms around the assembly, acting on orders, clashed their swords against their shields, and at the same moment the men responsible for the mutiny were brought in naked and in chains.

Such terror seized the crowd from the fear all around them and the horrors before their eyes that, while some were being scourged and others beheaded, not one man changed expression or uttered a sound, but all stood gaping and stunned by what was happening.

So the ringleaders of the evil, after suffering torture, were dragged dead through the middle. The rest then received from the general and the other officers a public pledge

that no one would bear a grudge against anyone, and they themselves came forward one by one and swore before the tribunes that they would obey the commands of their officers and harbor no thought hostile to Rome.

Thus Publius, having rightly corrected the beginnings of a great danger, restored his forces once more to their original condition.

## Book 11 Chapter 31: Scipio Proposes to Fight Andobales

Publius at once assembled the troops at Carthage into a meeting and spoke about the boldness of Andobales and his treachery toward them.

By developing these points at length, he stirred the mass of the soldiers to eagerness for an attack on the dynasts mentioned.

Then he reviewed their earlier campaigns against Iberians and Carthaginians fighting together under Carthaginian commanders,

and said that, since they had always defeated them, it was not fitting for them now to hesitate at the thought of fighting Iberians alone under Andobales.

Accordingly he said he would not even accept any Iberian ally at all, but would bring the struggle on by means of Romans alone,

so that it might become plain to all that it was not by using Iberians against the Carthaginians, as some people say, that they had driven the Carthaginians out of Iberia, but that Roman valor and their own courage had defeated both Carthaginians and Celtiberians.

Having said this, he urged them to be of one mind and of good courage, and to go into this struggle as boldly as they would go against anyone else. As for victory, he said that with the gods' help he had taken all proper thought for securing it.

This inspired such eagerness and confidence in the troops that, simply from their appearance, they looked like men who already saw the enemy before them and were just about to engage.

## Book 11 Chapter 32: Scipio Defeats Andobales

After saying this, he dismissed the assembly. On the next day he broke camp and advanced; reaching the Ebro on the tenth day and crossing it, he encamped on the fourth day after that near the enemy, with a certain valley lying between his camp and theirs.

On the next day he drove some of the livestock that followed the army into the valley, after giving Gaius instructions to have the cavalry ready and ordering certain tribunes to prepare the javelin-fighters.

The Iberians quickly rushed upon the animals, and Scipio released some of the javelin-men. As these engaged, and more men came to the support of either side, a large skirmish of infantry developed in the valley.

When the moment supplied a good opening for attack, Gaius, having the cavalry ready as ordered, charged the skirmishers and cut them off from the foothills, so that most of them were scattered through the valley and destroyed by the cavalry.

This enraged the barbarians; and fearing that, because of their earlier defeat, they might seem utterly demoralized, they marched out at daybreak and drew up their whole force for battle.

Publius was ready for the engagement, but when he saw that the Iberians were descending irrationally into the valley and posting not only their cavalry but even their infantry on the level ground, he waited,

wanting as many of them as possible to make use of this position. He trusted his own cavalry, and still more his infantry, because in regular, close hand-to-hand fighting both the equipment and the men on his side were far superior to the Iberians.

## Book 11 Chapter 33: Scipio's Return To Rome

When it seemed to him that the proper moment had come, he drew up a counterforce against the enemy stationed on the foothills, but against those who had descended into the valley he led out the main body from the camp and attacked the enemy infantry with four cohorts.

At the same time Gaius Laelius, with the cavalry, moved forward over the hills that slope from the camp toward the valley, charged the Iberian horse in the rear, and occupied them in fighting him.

The enemy infantry, therefore, deprived of the support of the cavalry in reliance on which they had come down into the valley, were pressed and overwhelmed in the battle; and the cavalry suffered much the same.

Hemmed in within a narrow space and at a disadvantage, they were destroyed more by each other than by the enemy, as their own infantry pressed on them from the flank, the hostile infantry faced them in front, and the cavalry surrounded them from the rear.

In a battle that developed in this way, nearly all who had descended into the valley were destroyed, while those on the foothills escaped.

These were the light-armed troops, amounting to a third of the whole army, and with them Andobales too escaped alive and fled to some fortified place.

Publius, having brought his work in Iberia to completion, arrived at Tarraco in the highest spirits, bringing to his country a most splendid victory and the material for a splendid triumph.

Then, eager not to miss the settlement of the consuls at Rome, he put all Iberian affairs in order, handed the army over to Junius and Marcius, and himself sailed with Gaius and his other friends for Rome.

## Book 11 Chapter 34: Antiochus Moves from Bactria Through Interior Asia

For Euthydemus himself was a Magnesian, and in his reply he said that Antiochus was wrong to be so eager to drive him from his kingdom.

For he had not himself revolted from the king; rather, after others had revolted, he had overthrown their descendants and in that way gained control of the Bactrian realm.

After saying more to the same effect, he urged Teleas to mediate the settlement in a friendly spirit, and to appeal to Antiochus not to grudge him the royal title and position,

since, if he did not grant what was asked, neither of them would be secure.

For there were no small numbers of nomads nearby, who were a danger to both, and if either side admitted them, the country would plainly be turned barbarian.

Having said this, he sent Teleas to Antiochus.

The king had long been looking for some resolution of affairs, and when he learned these things from Teleas he readily listened to proposals for peace for the reasons already given.

After Teleas had gone back and forth many times between the two, Euthydemus finally sent his son Demetrius to confirm the agreements. The king received him,

judged from his appearance, his conversation, and his bearing that the young man was worthy of royal power, and first promised to give him one of his own daughters; next he granted the royal title to his father.

After drawing up written agreements about the remaining matters and concluding a sworn alliance, he broke camp, liberally provisioning his army and taking along the elephants belonging to Euthydemus.

He crossed the Caucasus and descended into India, renewed his friendship with Sophagasenus, king of the Indians,

and after receiving more elephants, bringing the total to one hundred and fifty, he again supplied his army there. He himself marched off with the force, but left Androsthenes of Cyzicus behind to bring back the treasure that the king had agreed to give him.

Then, passing through Arachosia and crossing the river Erymanthus, he came by way of Drangene into Carmania, where, since winter was already closing in, he went into winter quarters.

So this was the end of Antiochus' campaign into the upper regions, by which he not only brought the upper satraps into obedience to his authority but also the coastal cities and the dynasts on this side of Taurus,

and in general secured his kingdom by overwhelming all his subjects with the impression made by his courage and endurance.

For by this expedition he proved himself worthy of kingship not only to the peoples of Asia but also to those of Europe.
