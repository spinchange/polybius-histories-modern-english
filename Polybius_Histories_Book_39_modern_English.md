# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXXIX

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 39 Chapter 1: Aulus Postumius and Cato’s Rebuke

Aulus Postumius became worthy of special notice in the following respects.

He came from a distinguished house and family, but by nature he was exceptionally talkative, garrulous, and ostentatious.

From childhood he had eagerly pursued Greek education and speech, and he knew so much of these things and was so saturated in them that, because of him, even the love of Greek culture itself became an offense to older and more serious Romans.

In the end he even attempted to write poetry and a work of practical history, in which, by way of preface, he asked readers to excuse him if, being a Roman, he could not fully command the Greek language and its stylistic handling.

Marcus Porcius Cato seems to have responded to this in characteristically apt fashion, saying that he wondered what justification there could be for such an apology.

For if the Amphictyonic Council had ordered him to write history, then perhaps he would have had reason to make such an excuse.

But when a man voluntarily enrolls himself, without any compulsion, and then asks pardon if he speaks barbarously, that is evidence of complete absurdity.

It is just as useless as if someone enrolled himself for the boxing or pankration contests, entered the stadium, and then, when he had to fight, asked the spectators to excuse him if he could neither endure the strain nor bear the blows.

Obviously such a man would deservedly be laughed at and get what was coming to him; and the same ought to be true of historians of that sort, so that they would not dare to overstep what is proper.

He was likewise eager in the rest of his life for the worst Greek habits: for he was pleasure-loving and work-shy.

This will be clear from the facts themselves: for he was the first man to be present in Greece at the time when the battle in Phocis took place, but he excused himself on grounds of ill health and withdrew to Thebes in order not to share the danger; yet after the battle was over he was the first to write to the Senate about the victory, adding detailed explanations as though he himself had taken part in the fighting.

## Book 39 Chapter 2: Soldiers’ Contempt for Corinth’s Artworks

Polybius, speaking with pity of what happened at the capture of the city, adds an account of the soldiers’ contempt for works of art and for the dedicated offerings.

For he says that he was present and saw paintings thrown on the ground, and the soldiers playing draughts on them.

Among them he names Aristides’ painting of Dionysus, of which some say the saying “Nothing to the Dionysus” was spoken, and the Heracles tormented by the robe of Deianeira.

## Book 39 Chapter 3: Polybius Saves Philopoemen’s Honors

Because of the goodwill the people had long felt toward Philopoemen, they did not take down the statues of him that stood in certain cities. So true is it, in my view, that everything done sincerely produces in those who have been well treated a goodwill that is hard to erase.

Therefore one might justly apply the common saying: he has missed, as the proverb goes, not by a door but by a whole street.

There were many statues of him and great honors that the cities had decreed, but during the disasters that befell Greece at Corinth a Roman attempted to remove them all and to prosecute him, charging, as though he were still alive, that he had been an enemy of the Romans and ill-disposed toward them. But after speeches had been made and Polybius had answered the false accuser, neither Mummius nor the envoys would consent to destroy the honors of a distinguished man.

[...] he undertook to explain at greater length, in keeping with what we said about the man at the beginning.

The points were these: that he had often disagreed with the Romans over their commands, but disagreed only so far as to inform and persuade them on the matters in dispute, and that he did not do even this without good reason.

He said that the man had given a genuine proof of his policy, and had done a service rescued, as the saying goes, out of the fire, at the time of the wars with Philip and with Antiochus.

For at that time, though he had the greatest weight of any of the Greeks, both through his own power and through that of the Achaeans, he had kept faith with the friendship of Rome most truly; he had joined the Achaeans in the decree by which, four months before the Romans crossed over, they declared war from their own territory on Antiochus and the Aetolians, when almost all the other Greeks had become estranged from friendship with Rome.

The ten commissioners, having heard this and approving the speaker’s attitude, granted that his honors should remain in all the cities.

Taking this opportunity, Polybius asked the general for the statues, although they had already been carried off from the Peloponnese to Acarnania—I mean those of Achaeus, Aratus, and Philopoemen.

The people, admiring his conduct in this, set up a stone statue of him.

## Book 39 Chapter 4: Polybius Refuses Diaeus’ Confiscated Property

After the arrangement that the ten commissioners had made in Achaea, those same commissioners instructed the treasurer who was to sell Diaeus’ property that whatever Polybius chose from among the possessions should be taken out and given to him as a gift, and that the rest should be sold to the buyers.

But the man mentioned above kept himself so far from accepting any such thing that he even urged his friends not to desire anything at all from what the treasurer was selling.

For the treasurer was going through the cities selling the property of all who had been associated with Diaeus and had also been condemned, except for those who had children or parents.

Some of these men paid no attention, but those who followed his advice won the very best reputation among the citizens.

## Book 39 Chapter 5: Polybius Settles the New Constitution

When these arrangements had been completed in six months, the ten commissioners, with the spring season now beginning, sailed back to Italy, having left behind for all the Greeks a fine proof of Roman intent.

As they were taking their leave, they instructed Polybius to travel through the cities and clear up whatever points the people found uncertain, until they had become accustomed to the constitution and the laws.

And after some time he did in fact do this, so that the people were brought, by argument, to accept the constitution that had been given to them, and no doubt arose about anything in the laws, either in private or in public.

For this reason the cities in general, from the outset accepting and honoring the man, and in later times especially when the things just mentioned were being done, honored him in every way with the greatest honors, both while he was alive and after his death.

Everyone judged that this was the right thing to do; for if he had not carried out and written down the laws concerning public jurisdiction, everything would have been left without order and full of confusion.

For this reason we must regard this among all the things mentioned above as Polybius’ finest achievement.

## Book 39 Chapter 6: The Roman General’s Restraint in Greece

As for the Roman general, after the council of the ten had departed from Achaea, he repaired the place at the Isthmus and adorned the temples at Olympia and Delphi, and on the following days he went from city to city, receiving honor in each and obtaining the respect appropriate to him.

And it was natural that he should be honored both publicly and privately.

For he behaved with restraint and purity, and in all matters he acted gently, although he had great opportunity and great power among the Greeks.

Indeed, even in those matters where he seemed to me to have overlooked something that ought to have been done, I did not think that he did it on his own account, but rather because of the friends who were with him.

This was most clearly shown in the case of the Chalcidian horsemen, whom he put to death.

## Book 39 Chapter 7: Character of King Ptolemy

When Ptolemy, king of Egypt, who had just become king of Syria as well, was struck in the course of the war, he died; and some judged him worthy of great praise and remembrance, while others judged the opposite.

For he was gentle and kind, perhaps more than any of the kings who had gone before him.

The greatest proof of this was the following: first, he never prosecuted any of his own friends for any charge, and I think that not even any of the other Alexandrians died because of him.

Later, when he seemed to have been driven from power by his brother, he first, at a favorable moment in Alexandria itself, treated the wrong done him as a matter for forgetting and returned no injury for injury.

And when his brother later plotted against Cyprus, and he became master of him both in body and spirit at Lapethos, he went so far from punishing him as an enemy that he even added gifts beyond those already promised in the treaty and undertook to give him his daughter as well.

Yet in moments of success and achievement he became unstrung in spirit, and a kind of Egyptian softness and laziness settled around him; and in such moods he fell into misfortunes.

## Book 39 Chapter 8: Polybius Recapitulates His Whole Plan

Polybius says near the end of his work that after we had brought these matters to a conclusion, we returned from Rome, as though we had already completed the chief points of what went before, and were worthy of the gratitude due to Roman goodwill.

Therefore we pray to all the gods that the remaining part of our life may continue in such things and depend upon such things, for we see that Fortune is truly jealous of human beings, and most especially in that part of life in which a man seems most blessed and most successful.

And so these things came to pass in that way.

Now that we have reached the end of the entire work, we wish, by recalling its beginning and the preface with which we set down the history, to summarize the whole subject, joining beginning and end together in both general terms and in detail.

Accordingly, at the beginning we said that we would make our starting point from where Timaeus left off.

Then, running briefly through the events in Italy, Sicily, and Libya—since he too had written history only about those regions—we came to the times when Hannibal took command of the Carthaginian forces, Philip son of Demetrius took the Macedonian kingdom, and Cleomenes the Spartan fled from Greece; and along with these came Antiochus’ succession to the Syrian kingdom and Ptolemy, called Philopator, to the Egyptian.

We further promised that, starting again from that point, when the 139th Olympiad was in progress, we would treat the common affairs of the inhabited world, setting them out by Olympiads, dividing them by year, and comparing the corresponding events until the capture of Carthage and the battle between the Achaeans and the Romans at the Isthmus, and then the restoration that followed for the Greeks.

From all this we said that the finest and most useful result for students would be to learn how, and under what kind of constitution, almost the entire inhabited world came under a single power, that of the Romans—something that had never happened before.

Now that all this has been completed for us, what remains is to make clear the times covered by the history, the number of books, and the extent of the whole work.
