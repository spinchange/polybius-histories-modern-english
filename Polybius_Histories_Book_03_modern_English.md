# Polybius — The Histories, Book III

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 3 Chapter 1: Summary of the Work

In the first book of my whole composition—which is the third counting backward from the present one—I made clear that I would take as the starting points of my work the Social War, the Hannibalic War, and, alongside these, the war over Coele-Syria.

In that same book I also explained the reasons why, going back in time to the period before these events, I composed the books that precede this one.

Now, after a few preliminary remarks about my own undertaking, I shall attempt to give a demonstrated account of the wars mentioned above, of the causes from which they arose, and of the reasons why they grew to such proportions.

Since the whole subject I have set my hand to write about is in fact a single action and a single spectacle—namely, how, when, and why all the known parts of the inhabited world fell under the dominion of Rome—

and since this event has a recognizable beginning, a fixed span of time, and an acknowledged conclusion, I think it useful to give in summary form a preliminary account of the greatest of its parts, all those that lie between the beginning and the end.

For I believe that in this way I shall best furnish to those eager to learn an adequate grasp of the whole design.

The mind takes in much when it moves from the whole toward knowledge of the particulars, and much again when it moves from the particulars toward understanding of the whole; and since I judge that the best comprehension and view comes from combining both, I shall make my preliminary outline of my own work follow accordingly.

I have already set out the general impression of my subject and its overall scope.

As for the particular events that occurred within it, the starting points are the wars mentioned above; the culmination and conclusion is the overthrow of the kingdom in Macedonia; and the time between the beginning and the end is fifty-three years.

Within this span are contained deeds so many and so great that no previous age has embraced their equal in an equal interval of time.

Beginning my account of these from the hundred and fortieth Olympiad, I shall make my approach to the exposition in the following manner.

## Book 3 Chapter 2: Plan: Causes of Wars

I shall make my approach to the narrative as follows. First, having pointed out the causes from which the previously mentioned war between the Carthaginians and the Romans—the one called the Hannibalic War—arose,

I shall tell how the Carthaginians invaded Italy, broke up the dominion of Rome there, and brought the Romans into great fear for themselves and for the very soil of their homeland, while they themselves conceived great and unexpected hopes, expecting even to take Rome itself by assault.

Next, I shall try to make clear how, during the same period, Philip of Macedon—after fighting his war with the Aetolians to its end and afterward settling affairs among the Greeks—set out to share these same hopes with the Carthaginians,

how Antiochus and Ptolemy Philopator first disputed and finally went to war with each other over Coele-Syria,

and how the Rhodians and Prusias took up war against the Byzantines and forced them to stop levying tolls on those sailing into the Pontus.

Pausing my account at this point, I shall put together a discussion of the Roman constitution, in which I shall show in due sequence that the distinctive character of their political system contributed enormously to their success—not only in winning back their dominion over the Italians and Sicilians, and in further acquiring the rule over the Iberians and the Celts, but finally also, after they had defeated the Carthaginians in war, in coming to conceive the idea of universal empire.

Together with this, by way of digression, I shall describe the fall of the dynasty of Hiero of Syracuse.

To these I shall append the disturbances in Egypt: how, after King Ptolemy departed this life, Antiochus and Philip agreed together to divide up the realm of the child he had left behind, and began their treacherous dealings and laid their hands on it—Philip on the regions around the Aegean, Caria, and Samos, and Antiochus on Coele-Syria and Phoenicia.

## Book 3 Chapter 3: Plan: Events in Greece

After this, having summarized the operations of the Romans and Carthaginians in Iberia, Libya, and Sicily, I shall, along with the shift in events, move my narrative entirely to the regions of Greece.

Having recounted the naval battles of Attalus and the Rhodians against Philip, and also the war between Rome and Philip—how it was conducted, by whom, and what end it had—

I shall join to this in unbroken sequence the account of the resentment of the Aetolians, in the course of which they drew in Antiochus from Asia and kindled the war against the Achaeans and the Romans.

After explaining its causes and the crossing of Antiochus into Europe, I shall make clear, first, how he was driven out of Greece; second, how, after being defeated, he withdrew from all the territory on this side of the Taurus;

and third, how the Romans, after crushing the insolence of the Galatians, secured for themselves undisputed mastery over Asia and freed those dwelling on this side of the Taurus from their fear of the barbarians and from the lawlessness of the Galatians.

After this, having set before the reader's eyes the misfortunes of the Aetolians and the Cephallenians, I shall take up the wars that Eumenes waged against Prusias and the Galatians, and likewise the one waged together with Ariarathes against Pharnaces.

Following these, after recalling the concord and resettlement of the Peloponnesians, and also the growth of the Rhodian state, I shall draw together my whole narrative and the events within it,

and finally relate the campaign of Antiochus, called Epiphanes, against Egypt, the Persian War, and the overthrow of the kingdom in Macedonia.

Through all of this it will at the same time be observed how the Romans, by their handling of each situation, made the whole inhabited world subject to themselves.

## Book 3 Chapter 4: Extension of the First Plan of the Work

Now, if it were possible to form an adequate judgment about which men and political systems are blameworthy and which, on the contrary, praiseworthy simply on the basis of their successes or their failures, then I ought to stop here and bring both my narrative and my work to a close with the last events I have mentioned, in keeping with my original intention.

For the fifty-three years came to their end at this point, and the growth and advance of Roman power had reached completion;

and besides, it seemed an accepted fact, forced upon everyone, that from then on all that remained was to listen to the Romans and to obey their commands.

But since judgments based purely on the contests themselves are not conclusive, whether about the victors or about the defeated,

because to many what seemed the greatest of successes has, when not used as it should be, brought the greatest disasters, while not a few who have met the most shattering reverses, when they bore them nobly, have often turned them to their advantage—

I ought therefore to add to the events already described an account of the policy of the victors: what it was afterward, and how they presided over the whole; and also the reactions and judgments of the others—how many and what kind there were concerning the leading power; and besides these I must set out the impulses and ambitions that prevailed and held sway among each people, both in their private lives and in their public affairs.

For it is clear that from these things it will become evident to people now living whether the rule of Rome is to be avoided or, on the contrary, to be welcomed, and to later generations whether their empire is to be considered praiseworthy and enviable or blameworthy.

It is precisely in this part that the usefulness of my history, both for the present and for the future, will chiefly lie.

For it must not be supposed that the end of a course of action—either for those who direct affairs or for those who pronounce judgment on them—is simply to conquer and bring everyone under one's own power.

No one with any sense makes war on his neighbors merely for the sake of defeating those who oppose him, nor sails the seas merely to make the crossing, nor takes up skills and crafts merely for the sake of the knowledge itself.

Everyone does everything for the sake of the resulting pleasure, honor, or advantage that comes from his actions.

Therefore the crowning achievement of this work will be to learn the condition of each people: what it was after everything had been subdued and had fallen under Roman authority, down to the disturbance and upheaval that arose again afterward.

Because of the magnitude of the events involved, the unexpected character of what happened, and—most of all—because I was not only an eyewitness to most of it but in some cases a participant and in others the very director, I was led to begin, as it were, a new work.

## Book 3 Chapter 5: The True Theory of Historical Causes

—as if making this the beginning of a fresh narrative to write. The upheaval I have spoken of was that in which the Romans waged war against the Celtiberians and the Vaccaei, and the Carthaginians against Massinissa, king of the Libyans;

while in Asia Attalus and Prusias made war on each other, and Ariarathes, king of the Cappadocians, having been driven from his throne by Orophernes through the agency of King Demetrius, recovered his ancestral kingdom again with the help of Attalus.

Demetrius, son of Seleucus, after holding the throne of Syria for twelve years, was robbed at once of his life and his rule when the other kings combined against him.

The Romans, too, restored to their own country those Greeks who had been accused in connection with the Persian War, having acquitted them of the charge brought against them.

Not long afterward these same Romans laid their hands on the Carthaginians, intending first to compel them to relocate, and then, for reasons to be stated in what follows, to root them out altogether.

Corresponding to these events—as the Macedonians broke away from their friendship with Rome and the Lacedaemonians from their membership in the Achaean confederation—the common calamity of all Greece had at once its beginning and its end.

Such, then, is the design of my work.

But it still has need of Fortune, that the circumstances of my life may run together to bring my purpose to completion.

For I am persuaded that, even if some human fate should befall me, this theme will not be left idle, nor will it lack men worthy of it; rather, because of its excellence, many will pledge themselves to it and be eager to bring it to completion.

Now that I have gone through the most notable events in summary—wishing to give my readers a conception of the whole history both in general and in its parts—it is time, keeping my purpose in mind, to lead the account back to the beginning of my subject.

## Book 3 Chapter 6: Causes of the Second Punic War

Some of those who have written accounts of the deeds of Hannibal, wishing to point out to us the causes from which the aforementioned war between the Romans and the Carthaginians arose, name as the first the siege of Saguntum by the Carthaginians,

and as the second their crossing, in violation of the treaties, of the river called by the local inhabitants the Iber.

For my part, I would say that these were the beginnings of the war, but I would by no means concede that they were its causes. Far from it—

unless one is also going to say that Alexander's crossing into Asia was the cause of the war against the Persians, and that Antiochus' landing at Demetrias was the cause of his war with the Romans: neither of which is either probable or true.

For who could suppose these to be causes, when Alexander earlier, and Philip while still alive, had done and prepared a great deal for the war against the Persians, and likewise the Aetolians had done much before the arrival of Antiochus for the war against the Romans?

But such notions belong to men who have not grasped how a beginning differs from a cause and a pretext, and how far apart they stand—that causes and pretexts come first of all, while the beginning is the last of the things mentioned.

I, however, declare that the beginnings of anything are the first attempts and actions to carry out what has already been decided, while the causes are the things that precede the decisions and judgments. By this I mean the conceptions, the dispositions, and the reasonings about these matters, and the considerations through which we come to decide on something and to set it as our purpose.

What I have said is made clear by what follows.

For what the true causes were, and from what source the war against the Persians sprang, is easy even for an ordinary person to discern.

The first was the return march of the Greeks under Xenophon from the upper satrapies, in the course of which, as they passed through the whole of Asia—hostile though it was—no one dared to stand and face them in the open;

the second was the crossing of Agesilaus, king of the Lacedaemonians, into Asia, in which he found nothing worthy or capable of opposing his designs, and was forced to return midway, without accomplishing his aim, because of the disturbances in Greece.

From these things Philip perceived and reckoned up the cowardice and laziness of the Persians, as well as his own excellence and that of the Macedonians in matters of war; and setting before his eyes, too, the magnitude and splendor of the prizes that would come from the war,

while at the same time securing the acknowledged goodwill of the Greeks, he at once made use of the pretext that he was eager to avenge the lawless treatment the Greeks had suffered from the Persians, conceived an eagerness for the enterprise, resolved to make war, and began preparing everything toward this end.

Accordingly, the first set of circumstances mentioned should be regarded as the causes of the war against the Persians, the second as the pretext, and the beginning as—

## Book 3 Chapter 7: Fabius Pictor on the Cause of the Punic War

—Alexander's crossing into Asia. And so too, in the case of the war between Antiochus and the Romans, it is clear that the cause must be considered the resentment of the Aetolians.

For they, believing that they had been slighted by the Romans in many respects regarding the outcome of the war with Philip—as I said above—not only drew in Antiochus, but undertook to do and suffer anything because of the anger that had arisen in them out of the events of that time.

The pretext should be considered the liberation of the Greeks, which they went about proclaiming from city to city in company with Antiochus, without reason and falsely; and the beginning of the war was the landing of Antiochus at Demetrias.

I have made this rather extended distinction about these matters not for the sake of censuring the historians in question, but for the sake of correcting the understanding of those eager to learn.

For what use is a physician to the sick who does not know the causes of the body's disorders? And what use is a man of affairs who cannot reason out how and why and from what source each of these matters took its origin?

For it is no more likely that the one will ever properly arrange treatments for the body than that the man of affairs will be able to handle any situation that arises as he should, without knowledge of the things just mentioned.

Therefore there is nothing we ought to be more on the watch for and to seek out than the causes of each event that occurs, since the greatest matters often grow out of trifling ones, and it is easiest of all to remedy anything in its first impulses and conceptions.

## Book 3 Chapter 8: The Credibility of Fabius Pictor

Fabius, the Roman historian, claims that the cause of the war with Hannibal was, alongside the wrong done to the Saguntines, the greed and lust for power of Hasdrubal.

For Hasdrubal, he says, had built up great personal authority in the regions of Iberia, and afterward came to Libya with the intention of abolishing the laws and transforming the Carthaginian government into a monarchy.

But the leading men of the state foresaw his design, joined together against him, and resisted him.

Hasdrubal, suspecting their hostility, withdrew from Libya and from then on managed affairs in Iberia entirely according to his own judgment, paying no attention to the Carthaginian senate.

Hannibal, who from boyhood had shared and admired Hasdrubal's outlook, and who then succeeded to the command in Iberia, conducted affairs in the same manner as Hasdrubal.

And so, in this present case too, he launched this war against the Romans on his own authority, contrary to the will of the Carthaginians;

for none of the men of standing in Carthage approved of what Hannibal had done against the city of the Saguntines.

After saying this, Fabius goes on to claim that, following the capture of the aforementioned city, the Romans came forward and demanded that the Carthaginians either hand Hannibal over to them or take on the war.

Now, suppose someone were to ask this historian: what occasion could have suited the Carthaginians better, and what course could have been more just or more advantageous than this one—seeing that, as he himself says, they had been displeased from the very beginning with Hannibal's actions?

What better than to yield then to the Romans' demands, hand over the man responsible for the wrongs, and so reasonably rid themselves, through others' hands, of the common enemy of the state, while securing safety for their territory and warding off the threatened war—all by merely passing a decree? What answer could Fabius give to this? Clearly none.

For so far were the Carthaginians from doing any of the things just described that they fought continuously for seventeen years in accordance with Hannibal's policy, and did not lay down the war until, having exhausted every hope, they came at last to the danger that threatened their homeland and the lives of all in it.

## Book 3 Chapter 9: First Cause of the Second Punic War

For what reason, then, have I brought up Fabius and what he has written?

Not out of any worry that his account is so plausible it might win belief among some readers—for the absurdity of his claims can be seen on its own, even without any explanation from me, by those who come across it—

but rather for the sake of warning those who take up his books, so that they may look not to the author's reputation but to the facts.

For there are some who do not fix their attention on what is said but on the man who says it, and who, bearing in mind that the writer lived through those times and was a member of the Roman senate, at once assume that everything he says is to be trusted.

My own view is that we should not, on the one hand, take the historian's authority lightly, but neither should we treat it as conclusive; rather, readers should base their judgments for the most part on the facts themselves.

But to return to the war between the Romans and the Carthaginians—for it was from this point that we made our digression—we must consider its first cause to have been the resentment of Hamilcar, surnamed Barcas, the natural father of Hannibal.

For that man's spirit had not been broken by the war over Sicily, since he believed that he himself had kept intact the forces at Eryx, where he was stationed, with their fighting spirit undiminished, and that it was only because of the Carthaginians' defeat in the naval battle that he had yielded to circumstances and made the treaty. He therefore held fast to his anger, always watching for a chance to strike.

Now, had the upheaval over the mercenaries not broken out among the Carthaginians, he would at once have sought another pretext and begun preparations for action, so far as it lay in his power.

But he was forestalled by the civil disturbances, and in these...

## Book 3 Chapter 10: Second Cause of the War

...he was occupied, taken up with those events. When the Romans, after the Carthaginians had put down the aforementioned disturbance, declared war on them, the Carthaginians at first were ready to face any extremity, supposing they would prevail on the justice of their cause, as we have explained concerning these matters in the earlier books—

without which it would be impossible to follow properly either what is now being said or what we shall say hereafter.

But since the Romans would not give way, the Carthaginians yielded to the circumstances, and though deeply aggrieved, being unable to do anything, they withdrew from Sardinia and agreed to pay another twelve hundred talents on top of the earlier sum, on condition of not having to take on the war at that time.

This, then, must be reckoned the second—and the most important—cause of the war that later broke out.

For Hamilcar, adding to his own private resentment the anger of his fellow citizens over these matters, as soon as he had subdued the rebellious mercenaries and secured his country's safety, immediately set his energies on affairs in Iberia, eager to use it as a base of preparation for the war against the Romans. This too must be counted a cause—the third—

I mean the prosperous course of Carthaginian affairs in Iberia. For it was by relying on the strength gained there that they confidently entered upon the aforementioned war.

That Hamilcar contributed most to bringing about the second war—even though he died ten years before its outbreak—one could find many proofs; but what follows will be just about...

## Book 3 Chapter 11: Hannibal's Oath

...enough to establish the point. For at the time when Hannibal, after being defeated by the Romans, finally left his homeland and was staying at the court of Antiochus, the Romans, now observing the scheming of the Aetolians, sent envoys to Antiochus, wishing not to be left in the dark about the king's intentions.

These envoys, seeing that Antiochus was inclined toward the Aetolians and eager to make war on the Romans, courted Hannibal, intent on bringing him under suspicion with Antiochus.

And this is just what happened. As time went on, and the king grew ever more suspicious of Hannibal, an occasion arose to bring out into open discussion the estrangement that had been quietly festering between them.

In the course of this, Hannibal offered several explanations, but at last, finding his arguments unavailing, he came out with the following.

He said that at the time when his father was about to set out on his expedition into Iberia with his forces, he himself was nine years old, and that as his father was sacrificing to Zeus he stood beside the altar.

When the omens proved favorable, his father poured the libation to the gods and performed the customary rites, then bade the others present at the sacrifice withdraw a little, and calling Hannibal to him, asked him kindly whether he wished to set out with him on the campaign.

When the boy eagerly assented and even begged, as children will, to be taken along, his father took him by the right hand, led him up to the altar, and bade him lay his hand on the sacrificial victims and swear that he would never be a friend to the Romans.

Knowing this clearly, then, Hannibal urged Antiochus that, so long as he was planning anything hostile against the Romans, he should take heart and trust him, considering that he had in Hannibal a most genuine ally;

but that if ever Antiochus should make a settlement or friendship with them, then he need wait for no slander, but should distrust Hannibal and be on his guard against him—

## Book 3 Chapter 12: Statesmen Must Pay Attention to Motives

—for there was nothing Hannibal would not do against the Romans, if it lay in his power. Antiochus, hearing this and judging that it had been spoken with real feeling and sincerity, set aside all the suspicion he had held before.

As for the hostility of Hamilcar and his whole purpose, this must be regarded as undeniable evidence, as the facts themselves also made plain.

For he prepared such enemies of the Romans in Hasdrubal, his daughter's husband, and in his own natural son Hannibal, that no greater degree of hatred could be left to imagine.

Hasdrubal, dying before his time, did not make his purpose evident to everyone; but circumstances gave Hannibal abundant opportunity to display, all too fully, the hatred of Rome he had inherited from his father.

For this reason, those placed in charge of public affairs ought to attend to nothing more than discerning the true intentions of those who lay aside their enmities or form friendships—whether they are making their agreements as a concession to circumstances or because their spirit has truly been defeated—

so that they may be constantly on guard against the former, regarding them as men lying in wait for an opportunity, while trusting the latter as genuine subjects or friends and giving them, without hesitation, whatever orders the occasion may require.

These, then, are to be considered the causes of the war with Hannibal. As for the beginnings, which I am about to relate...

## Book 3 Chapter 13: Hannibal Succeeds to Command in Spain

...The Carthaginians had taken hard their defeat over Sicily, but their anger was intensified still further, as I said above, by the affair of Sardinia and by the size of the sum of money imposed on them at the end.

For this reason, as soon as they had brought most of Iberia under their control, they were ready to seize on anything that offered a chance against the Romans.

So when the death of Hasdrubal occurred—the man to whom they had entrusted affairs in Iberia after the death of Hamilcar—they at first waited to see which way the armies would lean.

But when news came from the camps that the forces had with one accord chosen Hannibal as their commander, the Carthaginians at once convened the popular assembly and by a unanimous vote ratified the army's choice.

As soon as Hannibal had taken over the command, he set out at once to subdue the tribe of the Olcades; and arriving before Althaea, their most important city, he made camp.

After this, by means of vigorous and terrifying assaults, he quickly took the city. When this happened, the rest of the tribe, struck with terror, surrendered themselves to the Carthaginians.

Having exacted money from the cities and gained control of large funds, he came to New Carthage to winter.

Treating those under him generously—paying part of the wages owed to the men serving with him and promising the rest—he instilled in his forces great goodwill and high hopes...

## Book 3 Chapter 14: Hannibal Attacks the Vaccaei

...for the future. In the following summer he set out again, this time against the Vaccaei. He took Helmantica (Salmantica) by storm at the first assault, but Arbucala—because of the city's size, its large population, and the bravery of its inhabitants—he captured by force only after a laborious siege.

After this, on his march back, he unexpectedly found himself in the gravest danger, when the Carpesii—who are just about the strongest tribe in those regions—rushed upon him.

Their neighbors gathered together with them as well, stirred up chiefly by the exiles of the Olcades, but further inflamed by those who had escaped from Helmantica.

Had the Carthaginians been forced to risk a pitched battle against these foes, they would beyond question have been defeated.

As it was, Hannibal acted with skill and good sense: he wheeled about and withdrew, placing the river called the Tagus as a barrier in front of him, and arranged the engagement around the crossing of the river, using as his allies both the river itself and the elephants he had, about forty in number. As a result, the whole affair turned out, against expectation, just as he had reasoned it would.

For when the barbarians attempted to force a crossing of the river at several points, the greater part of them were destroyed at the places where they emerged, as the elephants moved along the bank and caught those coming out of the water one after another;

and many were killed in the river itself by the cavalry, since the horses were better able to hold their ground against the current, and the horsemen had the advantage of higher ground in fighting against the foot soldiers.

Finally, Hannibal's men crossed over to the other side in their turn, fell upon the barbarians, and routed more than a hundred thousand of them.

After their defeat, no one south of the river Iber any longer dared readily to look him in the face—except the Saguntines.

From this city Hannibal tried his best to keep his distance, wishing to give the Romans no acknowledged pretext for war until he had securely brought everything else under his control, in keeping with the instructions and counsels of his father Hamilcar.

## Book 3 Chapter 15: Hannibal Defies the Romans

The Saguntines kept sending embassies to Rome, partly because they were anxious for themselves and could see what was coming, and partly because they wanted the Romans to be aware of the steady success the Carthaginians were having in their Iberian affairs.

For a long while the Romans paid no attention to them, but at this point they dispatched envoys to investigate the situation.

At about the same time Hannibal, having subdued the peoples he had set out to subdue, came back with his forces to winter at New Carthage, which was in a sense the showpiece and royal seat of the Carthaginians in the regions of Iberia.

There he found the embassy from Rome, granted it an audience, and heard out what it had to say about the present circumstances.

The Romans, for their part, solemnly warned him to keep his hands off the Saguntines—since they were under Roman protection—and not to cross the river Ebro, in keeping with the agreement made in Hasdrubal's time.

But Hannibal—being young, full of warlike energy, successful in his ventures, and long since driven by his hatred of Rome—

answered the envoys by posing as the protector of the Saguntines. He charged the Romans with having, a short time before, when civil strife broke out in the city, taken on the role of arbitrators and unjustly put some of the leading men to death; he said the Carthaginians would not stand by while the Saguntines were treated treacherously like this, for it was Carthage's ancestral policy to overlook no one who was being wronged.

At the same time he sent word to Carthage asking what he should do, on the grounds that the Saguntines, trusting in their alliance with Rome, were committing injustices against some of the peoples subject to Carthage.

In short, he was thoroughly governed by unreason and violent passion. For this reason he did not make use of the genuine grounds for complaint, but took refuge in irrational pretexts—just as men typically do when, because of the impulses already lodged in them, they lose all regard for what is right.

How much better it would have been for him to take the view that the Romans ought to give back Sardinia to the Carthaginians, along with the tribute they had imposed at the same time—both of which the Romans had unjustly seized earlier when they took advantage of Carthage's difficulties—and that, failing that, he would go to war.

Instead, by saying nothing of the real, existing grievance and fabricating a non-existent one concerning the Saguntines, he gave the impression of starting the war not only without reason but, still worse, unjustly.

As for the Roman envoys, since they now clearly understood that war was inevitable, they sailed off to Carthage, intending to lodge the same protest there as well.

They expected, however, that they would be fighting the war not in Italy but in Iberia, and that they would use the city of Saguntum as their base of operations.

## Book 3 Chapter 16: War in Illyria

For this reason, the Senate, in adapting its plans to this situation, decided to secure its interests in Illyria, foreseeing that the war would be a major and protracted one, fought far from home.

For it so happened that at this time Demetrius of Pharos had forgotten the benefits he had previously received from the Romans, and—having come to despise them, first because of the threat surrounding them from the Gauls and then because of the threat from the Carthaginians—

placing all his hopes in the royal house of Macedonia (because he had fought alongside Antigonus and shared in the campaigns against Cleomenes), was plundering and subjugating the cities of Illyria that were subject to Rome, and had sailed beyond Lissus with fifty galleys, in violation of the treaty, and had ravaged many of the Cyclades islands.

In view of this, and seeing that the royal house of Macedonia was flourishing, the Romans were eager to secure their interests east of Italy, convinced that they had ample time to set right the folly of the Illyrians and to censure and punish the ingratitude and recklessness of Demetrius.

But they were mistaken in their calculations, for Hannibal forestalled them by taking the city of Saguntum.

And as a result it came about that the war was fought not in Iberia but right at Rome's doorstep and throughout the whole of Italy.

Nonetheless, with these considerations in mind, the Romans, just before the summer season, sent out Lucius Aemilius with an army to conduct operations in Illyria, in the first year of the one hundred and fortieth Olympiad.

## Book 3 Chapter 17: Hannibal Takes Saguntum

Hannibal, breaking camp with his army, advanced from New Carthage and marched on Saguntum.

This city lies on a spur of the mountain range that runs down toward the sea, where the borders of Iberia and Celtiberia meet, and it stands about seven stades from the sea.

Its inhabitants farm a territory that produces every kind of crop and is superior in quality to any in all of Iberia.

It was here that Hannibal pitched camp and set about the siege in earnest, foreseeing many advantages that would come from taking the city by storm.

In the first place, he reckoned he would deprive the Romans of their hope of basing the war in Iberia. Second, he was convinced that by terrifying everyone he would make the peoples already under his command more obedient, and the still-independent Iberians more cautious.

But the most important thing was that, leaving no enemy behind him, he could safely make his advance forward.

Apart from these considerations, he reckoned that he himself would gain plentiful supplies for his enterprises, that he would inspire eagerness in his forces through the profit each man stood to gain, and that he would win the goodwill of the Carthaginians at home through the spoils that would be sent to them.

With such calculations in mind, he pressed the siege vigorously, at times making himself an example to the troops by personally taking part in the labor of the works, and at other times encouraging the men and recklessly exposing himself to danger.

After enduring every kind of hardship and care, he finally took the city by storm in eight months.

Having become master of a great quantity of money, captives, and goods, he set aside the money for his own undertakings, in keeping with his original plan; the captives he distributed among his fellow soldiers according to each man's merit; and the goods he sent off at once, in their entirety, to the Carthaginians.

In doing this he was not disappointed in his calculations, nor did he fail of his original purpose. Rather, he made his soldiers keener to face danger, kept the Carthaginians ready to do whatever he asked, and afterward, thanks to the supplies he had laid in, accomplished many useful things himself.

## Book 3 Chapter 18: Demetrius Fortifies Dimale

At about this same time, as soon as Demetrius learned of the Romans' intentions, he immediately threw a strong garrison into Dimale, with appropriate supplies, while in the other cities he put to death those who opposed his policies and placed power in the hands of his own friends.

He then picked out six thousand of the most able-bodied men from his subjects and stationed them in Pharos.

When the Roman commander arrived in Illyria with his forces and saw that the enemy were confident in the strength of Dimale and in their preparations—and moreover believed the place to be impregnable—he decided to attack it first, wishing to strike terror into the enemy.

After addressing the various officers in turn and bringing up siege works at several points, he began the siege.

He took the place by storm in seven days, and at once crushed the spirit of all his opponents.

As a result, envoys immediately came from all the cities, surrendering themselves and placing themselves under the protection of Rome.

He accepted each on suitable terms and then sailed to Pharos against Demetrius himself.

Learning that the city was strongly fortified, that a large body of exceptional troops had been gathered into it, and that it was furthermore well stocked with supplies and other equipment, he began to suspect that the siege might prove difficult and drawn out.

So, foreseeing all this, he resorted on the spot to the following sort of stratagem.

Making his approach to the island by night with his whole army, he landed the greater part of his force at some wooded, low-lying places,

while at daybreak he sailed openly with twenty ships toward the harbor nearest the city.

When Demetrius and his men caught sight of the ships and, despising their small number, rushed out of the city to the harbor to prevent the landing.

## Book 3 Chapter 19: Illyrian War Successfully Finished

[The Illyrians rushed out] to prevent the enemy's landing. As soon as battle was joined and the fighting grew fierce, more and more men kept coming out of the city in support, until at last the whole force had poured out into the engagement.

At that point the Romans who had disembarked during the night joined the battle, having made their way through unobserved ground.

Seizing a strong hill between the city and the harbor, they cut off from the city those who had sallied out.

When Demetrius and his men realized what had happened, they gave up trying to stop those landing; rallying themselves and exhorting one another, they set off, having decided to risk a pitched battle against the troops on the hill.

The Romans, seeing the Illyrians' attack coming on vigorously and in good order, charged their companies into them with terrifying force.

At the same time, the men who had made the landing from the ships, seeing what was happening, pressed in from behind; and attacking from every side, they created no small confusion and disorder among the Illyrians.

So, with some of them in difficulty to their front and others to their rear, in the end Demetrius and his men were routed. Some of them fled toward the city, but most scattered across the island by trackless ways.

Demetrius, who kept galleys ready for whatever might happen, lying at anchor at certain deserted spots, made his escape to these. Boarding them when night came on, he sailed away and, against all expectation, made his way to King Philip, with whom he spent the rest of his life—

a man who possessed daring and boldness, but daring of an irrational and utterly undiscerning kind.

And so it came about that his death matched the character of his whole life.

For when, with Philip's approval, he undertook to seize the city of the Messenians, he was killed in a rash and reckless manner at the very moment of the attempt—matters about which I will give the details when I come to that period.

The Roman commander Aemilius, after taking Pharos at once by assault and razing it, gained control of the rest of Illyria, settled everything according to his own plans, and then, as the summer was now drawing to a close, returned to Rome, where he celebrated his entry with a triumph and amid universal acclaim.

For he was thought to have handled the affair not only skillfully but, still more, with manly courage.

## Book 3 Chapter 20: Rome's Reaction to the Fall of Saguntum

As for the Romans, when the news of the capture of Saguntum had already reached them, they certainly did not, by Zeus, then hold a debate about whether to go to war—as some historians claim, even setting down the speeches supposedly delivered on both sides—which is the most absurd thing imaginable.

For how could it be that the Romans, who a year earlier had declared war on the Carthaginians should they so much as set foot on Saguntine territory, would now, after the city itself had been taken by storm, gather to deliberate whether or not to go to war?

And how, and in what manner, can these writers, while introducing the astonishing detail of the gloom of the Senate, at the same time claim that the fathers brought their sons of twelve and older into the Senate House, and that these boys, though present at the deliberations, did not divulge any of the secrets even to their closest relatives?

None of this is either plausible or true in the slightest—unless, by Zeus, on top of everything else, Fortune granted the Romans this gift too: to have good sense the moment they were born.

About such writings as those of Chaereas and Sosilus there is no need to say anything more, for in my view they have the rank and value not of history but of barber-shop and street-corner gossip.

When the disaster that had befallen the Saguntines was reported to them, the Romans at once chose envoys and sent them in haste to Carthage,

presenting them with two options, one of which seemed, if the Carthaginians accepted it, to bring both disgrace and harm, while the other meant the beginning of great troubles and dangers.

For they either ordered that the general Hannibal and the councilors with him be handed over to the Romans, or they declared war.

When the Romans arrived, came before the Senate, and made these demands clear, the Carthaginians found the choice being put to them hard to bear.

Nonetheless, they put forward the ablest man among them and began to plead their case.

## Book 3 Chapter 21: Romans Demand Surrender of Hannibal

[The Carthaginians began] to argue their case. As for the agreement made with Hasdrubal, they passed over it in silence, on the grounds that it had never been made, and that even if it had, it was not binding on them, since it had been concluded without their authorization.

On this point they used a precedent drawn from the Romans themselves: they said that the treaty made by Lutatius during the war over Sicily, after it had already been agreed to by Lutatius, was later annulled by the Roman people on the grounds that it had been made without their authorization.

Throughout their argument they pressed and leaned hard on the final treaty, the one concluded in the war over Sicily.

In it, they said, there was no clause in writing concerning Iberia, but it was expressly laid down that the allies of each party should enjoy security from both.

They pointed out that the Saguntines were not at that time allies of Rome, and to prove this they read out the treaty aloud several times.

The Romans, however, refused outright to engage in legal argument, declaring that while the city of the Saguntines remained intact, the matter admitted of legal pleading and it was possible to settle the disputed points by discussion;

but now that the city had been treacherously destroyed, either the guilty parties had to be surrendered to them—whereby it would be clear to all that the Carthaginians had had no share in the wrongdoing, but that the deed had been done without their authorization—

or, if they were unwilling to do this and admitted that they shared in the wrongdoing, they had to take on the war as well. So the two sides argued in these rather general terms.

But it seems necessary to me not to leave this matter unexamined, so that neither those whose duty and concern it is to know the precise truth of these things may stray from the truth in deliberations of the utmost importance,

nor those who study these matters out of love of learning may go astray, misled along with the errors and partisan ambitions of the historians; but rather that there may be some agreed-upon survey of the rights that existed between the Romans and the Carthaginians toward one another, from the beginning down to our own times.

## Book 3 Chapter 22: Treaties between Rome and Carthage

The first treaty between Rome and Carthage was concluded in the consulship of Lucius Junius Brutus and Marcus Horatius, the first consuls appointed after the abolition of the monarchy—the same men under whom the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol was consecrated.

This was twenty-eight years before Xerxes crossed over into Greece.

I have translated the treaty and set it down below as accurately as I possibly could. For so great is the difference between the Roman language of today and that of antiquity that even the most learned men can scarcely make out certain points, and only after careful study.

The terms of the treaty run roughly as follows: "On these conditions there shall be friendship between the Romans and the allies of the Romans, and the Carthaginians and the allies of the Carthaginians:

The Romans and their allies shall not sail beyond the Fair Promontory, unless they are forced to by storm

or by enemies. If anyone is driven beyond it by necessity, he shall not be permitted to buy or take anything except what is needed for the repair of his ship or for

[7;8]

sacrifices, and within five days he must depart. Those who come to trade shall pay no duty,

except for the fee to the herald or the clerk. Whatever is sold in their presence shall be guaranteed to the seller by the public faith—that is, whatever is sold either in Libya or in Sardinia.

If any Roman comes to the part of Sicily that the Carthaginians govern, he shall have rights equal in all respects

to the Carthaginians. The Carthaginians shall do no wrong to the people of Ardea, Antium, Laurentum, Circeii, or Tarracina, nor to any other of the Latins

who are subject to Rome. From those cities that are not subject to Rome they shall keep their hands; and if they take one,

they shall hand it back to the Romans undamaged. They shall build no fortress in Latium. If they enter the territory in arms, they shall not spend a night within that territory."

## Book 3 Chapter 23: Division of Territory

The Fair Promontory is the headland lying just in front of Carthage itself, toward the north.

The Carthaginians hold that the Romans must under no circumstances sail in warships beyond this point toward the south, because—as it seems to me—they did not want them to become acquainted either with the region around Byzacium or with that around the Lesser Syrtis, the places they call Emporia, on account of the richness of the land.

But if anyone is driven there by force of storm or of enemies and needs some of the necessities for sacrifices or for the repair of his ship, they allow him to take these and nothing beyond them, and those who have put in to anchor must of necessity depart within five days.

To Carthage, and to all the part of Libya lying on the near side of the Fair Promontory, and to Sardinia and to the part of Sicily the Carthaginians govern, the Romans are permitted to sail for trade, and the Carthaginians guarantee by the public faith that justice will be secured.

From the terms of this treaty it is clear that they speak of Sardinia and Libya as their own exclusive possessions; but in the case of Sicily they expressly stipulate the opposite, making the treaty apply only to those parts of Sicily that fall under Carthaginian dominion.

Likewise the Romans make the treaty concerning Latium alone, and make no mention of the rest of Italy, because it did not fall under their authority.

## Book 3 Chapter 24: Treaties Between Rome and Carthage

After these, they made a further treaty, in which the Carthaginians included the Tyrians and the people of Utica as well.

To the Fair Promontory they added Mastia of Tarseium, beyond which they hold that the Romans must neither raid nor found a city.

Its terms run roughly as follows: "On these conditions there shall be friendship between the Romans and the allies of the Romans, and the Carthaginians, the Tyrians, and the people of Utica and their

allies. Beyond the Fair Promontory and Mastia of Tarseium the Romans shall not raid,

nor trade, nor found a city. If the Carthaginians take some city in Latium that is not subject to the Romans, they may keep the goods and the men,

but shall give back the city. If any of the Carthaginians capture men belonging to a people with whom the Romans have a peace in writing, but who are not subject to the Romans, they shall not bring them into the harbors of the Romans; and if such a captive is brought in and a Roman lays a hand on him,

he shall be set free. The Romans shall do likewise.

If a Roman takes water or provisions from any land the Carthaginians govern, he shall not, while supplied with these provisions, do wrong to any

with whom the Carthaginians are at peace and friendship.

A Carthaginian shall do likewise. If one does otherwise, he shall not take redress on his own authority; and if anyone does so,

the wrong shall be treated as a public offense. In Sardinia and Libya no Roman shall trade or found a city—*** unless only to take on provisions

or to refit a ship. If a storm drives him in, he shall depart within five days. In the part of Sicily the Carthaginians govern, and in Carthage, a Roman may do and sell everything that is permitted

to a citizen. A Carthaginian shall do likewise in Rome."

Once again in this treaty they tighten their hold on Libya and Sardinia, claiming them as their own and shutting off every landing-place to the Romans;

but in the case of Sicily they specify the opposite, with respect to the part subject to them.

So too the Romans, in regard to Latium, hold that the Carthaginians must do no wrong to the people of Ardea, Antium, Circeii, or Tarracina. These are the cities that enclose, along the sea, the Latin territory with which the treaty is concerned.

## Book 3 Chapter 25: The Third Treaty

Furthermore, the Romans concluded yet another treaty, the last of them, at the time of Pyrrhus's crossing, before the Carthaginians had embarked on the war over Sicily.

In it they preserve all the other provisions in accordance with the existing agreements, but the following clauses are added to them.

"If they make a written alliance with Pyrrhus, both parties shall make it on such terms that they remain free to come to each other's aid in the territory of whichever is at war.

Whichever of the two has need of the assistance, the Carthaginians shall furnish the ships both for the voyage out and for the return, but each side shall provide the pay for its own men.

The Carthaginians shall also aid the Romans by sea, if there is need; but no one shall compel the crews to disembark against their will."

The oath that had to be sworn was as follows.

In the case of the first treaties, the Carthaginians swore by the gods of their ancestors and the Romans by Jupiter the Stone, in accordance with a certain ancient custom; but in the case of these later treaties, by Mars and Enyalius.

The swearing by Jupiter the Stone went like this: the man taking the oath over the treaty takes a stone in his hand, and once he has sworn in the name of the public faith, he says these words:

"If I keep my oath, may good things be mine; but if I think or do otherwise, then, while all others are kept safe in their own homelands, under their own laws, in possession of their own livelihoods, their sacred things, and their tombs, may I alone be cast out, just as this stone is now."

And having said this, he throws the stone from his hand.

## Book 3 Chapter 26: Treaties Between Rome and Carthage

Since such treaties exist, and are still preserved even now, engraved on bronze tablets beside the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus in the treasury of the aediles,

who would not reasonably feel astonishment at the historian Philinus? Not because he was ignorant of these documents—that is no wonder, since even in our own day the oldest men among both the Romans and the Carthaginians, and those reputed to take the greatest interest in public affairs, were unaware of them—

but rather, how or on what grounds did he have the boldness to write the very opposite of these facts: namely, that there existed treaties between the Romans and the Carthaginians by which the Romans were bound to keep away from the whole of Sicily

and the Carthaginians from the whole of Italy, and that the Romans violated the treaties and their oaths when they made their first crossing into Sicily—when in fact no such written document was ever drawn up or ever existed at all.

For he states this in his second book in explicit terms. I mentioned these matters in the introductory part of my own work, but I deferred treating them in detail until the present occasion, because a good many people too have been led away from the truth in this matter by trusting Philinus's account.

Nevertheless, if anyone finds fault with the Romans on this point regarding the crossing into Sicily—because they took the Mamertines into their friendship at all, and then, when these men asked for it, came to their aid, men who had broken faith not only with the city of the Messenians but also with that of the Rhegians—he might with reason appear to be displeased.

But if anyone supposes that they made the crossing in violation of their oaths and treaties, he is plainly mistaken.

## Book 3 Chapter 27: The Fourth Treaty

When the war over Sicily had been brought to an end, then, they made another treaty, in which the essential points of the written terms were these:

"The Carthaginians shall withdraw from the whole of Sicily and from all the islands

lying between Italy and Sicily. Security shall be guaranteed by each side to the allies of the other.

Neither side shall impose any orders, nor erect any public buildings, nor recruit mercenaries, nor take into alliance the allies of the other, within the other's domains.

The Carthaginians shall pay out two thousand two hundred talents within ten years, and shall hand over a thousand at once.

The Carthaginians shall return all their prisoners to the Romans without ransom."

After this, again,

when the Libyan (Mercenary) war had ended, the Romans, having declared war on the Carthaginians to the point of a formal decree, made additional terms of the following kind:

"The Carthaginians shall withdraw from Sardinia and pay an additional twelve hundred talents," as we stated above.

In addition to the foregoing, the last of the agreements were the ones concluded with Hasdrubal in Iberia, to the effect that "the Carthaginians shall not cross the river Ebro

with arms." These were the legal obligations between the Romans and the Carthaginians from the beginning down to the times of Hannibal.

## Book 3 Chapter 28: No Reasonable Pretext for Rome to Claim Sardinia

Just as, then, we find that the Romans' crossing into Sicily was not made in violation of their oaths, so too, in the case of the second war—the one in connection with which they made the treaty concerning Sardinia—one could find neither a reasonable pretext nor a just cause,

but rather it is generally agreed that the Carthaginians, contrary to all justice, were forced by their circumstances to withdraw from Sardinia and to pay out the sum of money mentioned above.

For the charge brought by the Romans on this matter—that the Carthaginians had wronged Roman seafarers during the Libyan war—had already been settled at the time when, after recovering from the Carthaginians all those who had been carried off, the Romans in return, as a favor, gave back without ransom the prisoners they held.

I have set out these matters in detail in the book before this one.

Since this is how things stand, it remains to examine and determine carefully, concerning the war of Hannibal's time, to which of the two parties the responsibility for it is to be assigned.

## Book 3 Chapter 29: The Roman Case For War

I have now set out what the Carthaginians said on that occasion; next I will give the arguments put forward by the Romans. They did not in fact make use of these at the time, because of their anger at the destruction of Saguntum, but the arguments were voiced often and by many men among them.

First, they said that the agreement made with Hasdrubal could not be set aside, as the Carthaginians presumed to claim it could. For it did not include the clause—

—that the treaty of Lutatius had included, namely that "these terms shall be binding only if the Roman people also approve them." Instead, Hasdrubal made the agreement on his own full authority, and in it stood the provision that "the Carthaginians shall not cross the river Iber for the purpose of war."

Furthermore, in the treaty concerning Sicily there was a written clause—as the Carthaginians themselves admit—that "the allies of each party shall enjoy security from the other." This did not apply only to those who were allies at that time, as the Carthaginians construed it.

For if it had, there would have been an added clause either forbidding each side to take on additional allies beyond those they already had, or excluding from these terms any allies acquired later.

But since neither of these was written in, it was plain that all the allies of both parties—both those existing at the time and those to be taken on afterward—were always to enjoy security from both sides.

And this surely seems entirely reasonable. For they were hardly going to make a treaty that stripped them of their own freedom to take on, as occasions arose, any who might prove to be suitable friends and allies;

nor, having taken such people under their protection, would they then stand by and watch them be wronged by anyone.

Rather, the gist of what both parties had in mind in the treaty was this: with regard to the allies that both sides already had, each would keep its hands off them and in no way receive any of the other's allies into its own alliance;

and with regard to those who would be taken on later, the point was precisely the same—that neither side should recruit mercenaries or levy contributions in the other's provinces and allied territories, and that all should enjoy security from both sides.

## Book 3 Chapter 30: Both Sides Are in the Wrong

Given that this was the situation, it was also a matter of agreement that the Saguntines had placed themselves under Roman protection many years before the time of Hannibal.

The clearest proof of this—and one acknowledged even by the Carthaginians themselves—was that when the Saguntines fell into civil strife among themselves, they entrusted the matter not to the Carthaginians, although these were close at hand and were already active in Iberia, but to the Romans, and through them they set their political affairs right.

Therefore, if one regards the destruction of Saguntum as the cause of the war, it must be granted that the Carthaginians began the war unjustly—both in light of the treaty of Lutatius, under which the allies of each party were to have security from the other, and in light of the treaty of Hasdrubal, under which the Carthaginians were not to cross the river Iber for the purpose of war.

But if one regards the seizure of Sardinia and the money exacted along with it as the cause, then it must be fully admitted that the Carthaginians had good reason for waging the Hannibalian war: for having yielded to circumstance at the time, they took the opportunity, when circumstance favored them, to defend themselves against those who had injured them.

## Book 3 Chapter 31: The Value of Such Discussions

Some who consider such matters without discernment might perhaps say that there is no real need for us to go into such precise detail about arguments of this kind.

For my part, if anyone supposed himself to be self-sufficient in the face of every circumstance, I would say that knowledge of the past, while a fine thing, is perhaps not essential.

But since no human being would dare to make such a claim about either his private or his public affairs—because even if he is prosperous in the present, no one of sound mind can reasonably guarantee anything about the future on the basis of the present—then for these reasons I say that knowledge of the past is not only a fine thing,

but all the more essential.

For how could a man—whether wronged himself or with his country being wronged—find helpers and allies, or, if he were eager to gain something and take the initiative, rouse others to join him in his undertakings?

And how, if he were content with the present state of affairs, could he rightly spur on those who would secure his policy and preserve the established order, if he knew nothing from the record of what each party had done in the past?

For with an eye always to the present, everyone adapts and plays a part, saying and doing such things that each man's true purpose is hard to discern, and in many cases the truth is heavily obscured.

But the deeds of the past, which are tested by the facts themselves, truly reveal the choices and judgments of each person, and show us among whom we have gratitude, kindness, and aid to expect, and among whom the opposite.

From this record one can often find, in many situations, the man who will show pity, the man who will share our indignation, and the man who will see justice done.

These things offer the greatest help, both to communities and to individuals, in human life.

This is why neither those who write history nor those who read it should concern themselves so much with the bare narrative of the events themselves as with what preceded, accompanied, and followed them.

For if you take away from history the why, the how, and the purpose for which something was done, and whether its outcome was reasonable, what is left of it becomes a mere display rather than a lesson;

and though it gives pleasure for the moment, it is of no use whatever for the future.

## Book 3 Chapter 32: This History is Not Difficult to Understand

We must regard those who suppose that my work is hard to acquire and hard to read through, on account of the number and bulk of its books, as simply mistaken.

For how much easier is it both to obtain and to read through forty books—woven together, as it were, on a single thread—and to follow clearly the events in Italy, Sicily, and Libya from the time of Pyrrhus down to the capture of Carthage,

and those in the rest of the world, in unbroken sequence from the flight of Cleomenes the Spartan down to the engagement between the Achaeans and the Romans at the Isthmus—how much easier than to read or to acquire the separate compositions of those who write only of particular events?

For, quite apart from the fact that such works are many times more numerous than my own treatise, it is impossible for their readers to grasp anything from them with any certainty: first, because most of these writers do not give the same account of the same events,

and next, because they leave out the events that run parallel to one another in time—events which, when set side by side and viewed and compared together, lead each to a quite different verdict than they receive under piecemeal treatment—while of the most decisive matters these writers cannot even take hold at all.

For we maintain that by far the most essential parts of history are the consequences that follow upon actions, the circumstances that accompany them, and above all the matter of their causes.

Thus we observe that the war with Antiochus took its origin from the war with Philip, the war with Philip from the war with Hannibal, and the war with Hannibal from the war over Sicily; and that the many and varied developments that lay between these wars all converged upon one and the same end.

Now all of this can be recognized and learned from those who write universal history, but it cannot be learned from those who write of the wars themselves in isolation—the Persian War, say, or the war with Philip—

unless a man, by reading the accounts of the pitched battles that those writers give, imagines that he has thereby gained a clear understanding of the whole conduct and design of the war.

But that is not so at all. Rather, by as much as understanding surpasses mere hearing, by so much, I hold, does my history surpass the compositions that treat only of particular events.

## Book 3 Chapter 33: Hannibal's Preparations

As for the Roman envoys—for it was at this point that we broke off into our digression—when they had heard out the case of the Carthaginians, they said nothing further,

except that the eldest of them, pointing to the fold of his toga before the council, declared that there in it he carried both war and peace for them, and that he would shake out and leave behind whichever of the two they should bid him.

The king of the Carthaginians told him to shake out whichever of the two seemed good to the Romans.

And when the Roman said that he would shake out war, many of those in the council cried out together that they accepted it. On these terms the envoys and the council parted.

Hannibal, meanwhile, while wintering at New Carthage, first dismissed the Iberians to their own cities, wishing them to be ready and eager for what was to come.

Secondly, he gave instructions to his brother Hasdrubal as to how he should manage the government and dominion over Iberia, and conduct the preparations against the Romans, in case he himself should be away somewhere.

Thirdly, he took thought beforehand for the security of affairs in Libya.

Reasoning with great experience and prudence, he transferred soldiers from Libya into Iberia and from Iberia into Libya, binding fast the loyalty of each group toward the other through this arrangement.

Those who crossed over into Libya were the Thersitae, the Mastiani,

and besides these the Oretes (an Iberian people) and the Olcades—altogether from these nations twelve hundred cavalry and thirteen thousand eight hundred and fifty infantry, and in addition eight hundred and seventy Balearic men,

whom they properly call slingers, and from this very function the people themselves and their island take their shared name.

Of the troops just mentioned, he stationed the greater part in Metagonia in Libya, and some in Carthage itself.

From the cities of the people called the Metagonians he sent another four thousand infantry to Carthage, who served at once as hostages and as a body of reinforcement.

In Iberia he left with his brother Hasdrubal fifty quinqueremes, two quadriremes, and five triremes—of these, thirty-two of the quinqueremes were furnished with crews, and all five of the triremes.

There were also cavalry: four hundred and fifty Libyophoenicians and Libyans, three hundred Lergetes, and eighteen hundred Numidians of the Masylii, Masaesylii, Maccoei, and Maurusii who dwell along the Ocean;

and infantry: eleven thousand eight hundred and fifty Libyans, three hundred Ligurians, five hundred Balearic Islanders, and twenty-one elephants.

No one should be surprised at the precision of this enumeration—if I have used a precision concerning Hannibal's operations in Iberia such as a man would scarcely employ even of matters he had managed himself in detail—nor should anyone condemn me beforehand for behaving like those historians who lie plausibly.

For I myself found this record set down on a bronze tablet at Lacinium, drawn up by Hannibal during the period when he was operating in the regions of Italy, and I judged it to be entirely trustworthy, at least for such matters; and therefore I chose to follow this record.

## Book 3 Chapter 34: Hannibal Contacts the Celts

Hannibal, having taken every precaution for the security of affairs both in Libya and in Iberia, now waited expectantly for the messengers being sent to him from the Celts.

For he had clearly informed himself of the excellence of the land at the foot of the Alps and around the river Padus, of the multitude of those who inhabited it, and further of the men's daring in war,

and—most important of all—of the hostility they bore toward the Romans from the earlier war, which I described in the book before this one, so that those who read might be able to follow what is now about to be told.

He therefore clung to this hope and promised everything, sending careful messages to the chieftains of the Celts, both those on the near side of the Alps and those dwelling in the Alps themselves,

since he believed that he could establish the war against the Romans within Italy only if he could pass through the difficult country that lay before him, reach the regions just mentioned, and make use of the Celts as helpers and allies for the undertaking he had in view.

When the messengers arrived and reported the willingness and expectation of the Celts, and declared that the crossing of the Alpine mountains, though very toilsome and difficult, was nevertheless not impossible, he gathered his forces from their winter quarters at the approach of spring.

Since the news from Carthage had also just reached him, his spirit was raised, and, trusting in the goodwill of his fellow citizens, he now openly summoned his forces to the war against the Romans.

He set before them how the Romans had ventured to demand his own surrender and that of all the commanders of the army, and he pointed out the excellence of the country to which they were going, and the goodwill and alliance of the Celts.

When the troops eagerly rallied to his side, he praised them, named a fixed day on which he would make the departure, and for the moment dismissed the assembly.

## Book 3 Chapter 35: Hannibal Crosses the Pyrenees

Having carried out the measures described during the winter quarters, and having provided sufficient security for affairs both in Libya and in Iberia, when the appointed day came he set out, with about ninety thousand infantry and around twelve thousand cavalry.

Crossing the river Iber, he subdued the nation of the Ilurgetes and the Bargusii, and also the Aerenosii and the Andosini, as far as the place called the Pyrenees.

When he had brought all of them under his control and taken some cities by storm—quickly and beyond expectation, but only after many great struggles and heavy loss of men—

he left Hanno as governor over all the country on the near side of the river, and as master over the Bargusii in particular; for he distrusted these people most of all, on account of their goodwill toward the Romans.

He also assigned to Hanno a portion of the forces he had: ten thousand infantry and a thousand cavalry, and he left with him the baggage of those who were setting out with himself.

He sent home an equal number to the troops just mentioned, wishing both to leave these men well disposed toward him and to hold out to the rest a hope of returning home—both to the Iberians serving in his own army and, no less, to those remaining at home—so that all might set out eagerly should there ever be need of reinforcement from them.

Taking up the rest of his army, lightly equipped—fifty thousand infantry and close to nine thousand cavalry—he led them through the mountains called the Pyrenees toward the crossing of the river called the Rhone,

having a force not so much large as serviceable and exceptionally well trained from the continuous fighting in Iberia.

## Book 3 Chapter 36: Geography of Hannibal's March

So that my account may not become entirely obscure through ignorance of the localities involved, I ought to state from where Hannibal set out, through which regions—and how many of them—he passed, and into which parts of Italy he came down.

But I should not merely cite the bare names of the places, rivers, and cities, as some historians do, supposing that this alone is enough in every case to produce knowledge and clarity.

In my view, where places are already familiar, the listing of their names contributes something—and indeed a great deal—toward recollection; but where they are unknown, reciting their names has in the end no more force than meaningless, jangling noises.

For when the mind has nothing to fasten upon and cannot fit what is said onto anything familiar, the narrative becomes ungraspable and dead.

We must therefore point out a method by which it will be possible, in speaking of unknown places, to lead our listeners to some degree toward genuine and recognizable ideas.

The first and most important means of knowledge,

and one moreover that is common to all people, is the division and ordering of the heaven that surrounds us, by which all of us who are worth anything at all recognize east, west, south, and north.

The second is that, by assigning the regions of the earth to each of these distinctions and always carrying in our minds whatever is mentioned onto one of the directions named above, we arrive at familiar and customary notions about things unknown and

## Book 3 Chapter 37: Three Geographic Divisions of the World

unseen. Now that these matters have been laid down concerning the earth as a whole, it follows that we should divide up the inhabited world that is ours by the same principle and bring it to our listeners' attention.

This world is divided into three parts with three names: one part of it they call Asia, the second Libya, and the third Europe.

The boundaries that separate these are the river Don, the Nile, and the strait at the Pillars of Heracles.

Asia, then, happens to lie between the Nile and the Don, and falls within that span of the surrounding heaven that lies between the summer sunrise (northeast) and the south.

Libya lies between the Nile and the Pillars of Heracles, and falls within the southern portion of the heaven and, continuously, within the region of the winter sunset (southwest), as far as the equinoctial setting point, which falls at the Pillars of Heracles.

These two regions, then, viewed in broad terms, occupy the southern zone of our sea, running from east toward west.

Europe lies opposite both of them, facing toward the north, and stretches continuously from east to west.

Its largest and deepest portion lies directly beneath the north, between the river Don and the Narbo—a river not far to the west of Marseilles and of the mouths of the Rhone, through which the aforesaid river empties into the Sardinian Sea.

From the Narbo and the surrounding country the Celts dwell, as far as the mountains called the Pyrenees, which extend without a break from our sea to the outer sea.

The remaining part of Europe, from those mountains onward—the part adjoining the west and the Pillars of Heracles—is enclosed by our sea and the outer sea; and the portion stretching along our sea as far as the Pillars of Heracles is called Iberia,

while the portion lying along the outer sea, which is called the Great Sea, has no common name, because it has only recently been explored. It is inhabited entirely by barbarous and populous peoples, about whom I will give a detailed

## Book 3 Chapter 38: Unknown Areas in the North and the South

account later on. Just as, in the case of Asia and Libya, where they border on each other in the region of Ethiopia, no one down to our own day can say with certainty whether the land toward the south is one continuous mainland or is bounded by the sea,

so too the country that runs up toward the north between the Don and the Narbo remains unknown to us until now—unless, by some later inquiry, we investigate it.

And anyone who says or writes anything about these regions otherwise must be regarded as ignorant and as spinning fables.

Let this much, then, be said by me so that my narrative may not be wholly unintelligible to those unacquainted with the localities, but so that, at least in terms of the broad distinctions, they may grasp something and refer what is said to some point in their minds, judging from the surrounding heaven.

At this time the Carthaginians were masters of all those parts of Libya that face the inner sea, from the Altars of Philaenus, which lie along the Great Syrtis, as far as the Pillars of Heracles.

This stretch of coastline is more than sixteen thousand stades in length.

Having crossed the strait at the Pillars of Heracles, they had likewise gained control of all Iberia as far as the headland on our sea where the Pyrenees range comes to an end—the mountains that form the boundary between the Iberians and the Celts.

This place is about eight thousand stades distant from the mouth at the Pillars of Heracles.

From the crossing of the Rhone, for those marching alongside the river itself toward its sources up to the start of the ascent into the Alps that leads into Italy, it is one thousand four hundred stades.

What remains are the passes over the Alps themselves, about one thousand two hundred stades; once he had crossed these, he would arrive in the plains around the Padus in Italy.

So that the whole distance he had to traverse from New Town was about nine thousand stades.

Of this territory he had by now covered nearly half in terms of sheer distance, but in terms of difficulty the greater part of the march still lay ahead of him.

## Book 3 Chapter 40: Gauls Attack the Military Colonies

Hannibal, then, set about forcing the passes over the Pyrenees, full of fear of the Celts because of the strength of the terrain.

The Romans, meanwhile, at this same period had heard from the envoys they had sent to Carthage both the decisions reached and the words that had been spoken; and when news fell upon them, sooner than they expected, that Hannibal had crossed the river Iber with his army, they resolved to send Publius Cornelius with legions into Iberia, and Tiberius Sempronius into Libya.

While these commanders were busy with the enrolment of their legions and the rest of their preparations, the people pressed on to bring to completion the work of the colonies which they had earlier resolved to send out into Gaul.

They fortified the towns vigorously, and ordered the settlers to be on the spot within thirty days, their number amounting to six thousand for each of the two cities;

one of these they founded on the near side of the river Padus, naming it Placentia, and the other on the far side, calling it Cremona.

These colonies had just been settled when the Gauls called the Boii—who had long been lying in wait, as it were, to throw off their friendship with the Romans, but had until then lacked an opportunity—

now grew excited and, trusting in the presence of the Carthaginians on the strength of the reports passing among them, revolted from the Romans, abandoning the hostages they had given when they came out of the earlier war, which I described in the previous book.

They appealed to the Insubres and, joining forces in keeping with their long-standing anger, overran the land that had been parcelled out by the Romans; pursuing the fleeing colonists, they shut them up in Mutina, a Roman colony, and laid siege to it.

Among those they trapped inside were three distinguished men who had been sent out to divide up the land: one of these was Gaius Lutatius, who had held the consulship, and the other two had held the praetorship (the office of the six axes).

When these men thought it necessary to come to a parley with them, the Boii agreed. But when the men came out, the Boii violated the truce and seized them, hoping by their means to recover their own hostages.

Lucius Manlius, who was a praetor stationed in the area with an army, heard what had happened and hurried to the rescue.

The Boii, learning of his approach, prepared ambushes in some woods, and as soon as he had entered the wooded ground they fell upon him from all sides at once and killed many of the Romans.

The survivors at first rushed to flee, but once they reached open ground they pulled themselves together to such a degree that they managed, with difficulty, to make an orderly withdrawal. The Boii, however, followed close behind and shut these men too up in the village called Tannes.

When news reached Rome that the fourth legion was surrounded by the Boii and being besieged with full force, they hastily dispatched the legions that had been assigned to Publius to the relief of these men, putting a praetor in command of them, and they instructed Publius to gather and enrol other legions from among the allies.

## Book 3 Chapter 41: The Consuls Set Out to Iberia and Libya

Such, then, was the state of Celtic affairs from the beginning down to the arrival of Hannibal, and they had reached the outcome that I have described both earlier and now.

The Roman commanders, having made ready everything for their own undertakings, set sail at the start of the season for the operations before them—Publius to Iberia with sixty ships, and Tiberius Sempronius to Libya with a hundred and sixty quinqueremes.

With these he set out to wage war so formidably and made such preparations at Lilybaeum—mustering everyone from every quarter—as though he meant to lay siege to Carthage itself the moment he landed.

Publius, sailing along the Ligurian coast, came on the fifth day from Pisae to the region around Massalia,

and, anchoring off the first mouth of the Rhone, the one called the Massaliote mouth,

he began disembarking his forces. He had heard that Hannibal was already crossing the Pyrenees, but he was convinced that he was still far off, both because of the difficulty of the terrain and because of the multitude of Celts lying between them.

Hannibal, however, contrary to expectation, partly winning over the Celts with money and partly forcing his way through them, arrived with his forces at the crossing of the Rhone, keeping the Sardinian Sea on his right.

When it was made clear to Publius that the enemy were at hand, he partly disbelieved it because of the speed of their arrival and partly wished to learn the precise truth; so while he himself let his troops recover from the voyage and deliberated with the tribunes about which ground to use and how to engage the enemy,

he sent out three hundred of his most courageous cavalry, attaching to them as both guides and supporting fighters some Celts who happened to be serving as mercenaries with the Massaliotes.

## Book 3 Chapter 42: The Passage of the Rhone

Hannibal, on reaching the region of the river, at once set about making the crossing where the stream runs in a single channel, his camp being about four days' march from the sea.

Making friends in every way with the people living along the river, he bought up from them all their dugout boats and small craft, which were plentiful in number because many of those dwelling beside the Rhone engage in seaborne trade.

He also obtained from them suitable timber for the construction of dugouts, so that within two days a countless number of transport boats was produced, each man eager not to depend on his neighbour but to hold in his own hands his hopes of getting across.

At this time a host of barbarians had gathered on the far bank in order to prevent the Carthaginians from crossing.

Looking at them, Hannibal reasoned from the present circumstances that it was impossible either to force a crossing with so many enemies posted opposite, or to wait where he was, for fear of being attacked by the enemy from every side;

so when the third night came on he sent off a part of his force, providing them with local guides and placing over them all Hanno, the son of Bomilcar the king.

These men marched up against the current along the river for two hundred stades, and when they reached a certain place where the river happened to divide around an island-like piece of ground, they halted there.

From the woodland close at hand, by fastening some pieces of timber together and lashing others, they constructed in a short time many rafts, sufficient for their immediate need; on these they crossed over safely, with no one to stop them.

Seizing a strong position, they stayed there that day to rest from their recent hardships, while at the same time making preparations for the task to come, according to their orders.

Hannibal, for his part, was doing much the same with the forces left behind with him.

The greatest difficulty he faced was getting the elephants across; these numbered thirty-seven.

## Book 3 Chapter 43: Hannibal's Troops Begin to Cross

When the fifth night had come, the detachment that had crossed earlier set out from the far bank at dawn and advanced along the river itself against the barbarians on the opposite side.

Hannibal, meanwhile, with his soldiers ready, was poised to begin the crossing. He had loaded the larger boats with the shield-bearing cavalry and the dugout canoes with the most agile of his infantry.

The larger boats took up position upstream and against the current, while the lighter craft were stationed below them, so that, with the larger boats absorbing most of the force of the stream, the passage across the channel would be safer for the canoes.

They planned to tow the horses swimming behind the sterns of the boats, with one man on each side of the stern steering three or four at once by their lead-ropes, so that a sufficient number of horses could be ferried across together with the very first crossing.

The barbarians, watching the enemy's maneuver, poured out of their stockade in disorder and scattered groups, convinced that they would easily prevent the Carthaginians from landing.

But as soon as Hannibal saw that his own men on the far side were now drawing near—they had signaled their arrival with smoke, as had been arranged—he gave the order for everyone to embark at once, and for those manning the craft to push hard against the current.

This was done quickly, and the men in the boats raced one another with loud shouts, struggling against the force of the river.

The two armies stood drawn up along the banks of the river on either side: Hannibal's own men sharing in the struggle and following its course with shouts, while the barbarians facing them chanted their war cries and dared them to the fight. The whole scene was astonishing and charged with suspense.

At this moment, when the barbarians had abandoned their tents, the Carthaginians from the far side fell upon them suddenly and unexpectedly. Some of them set fire to the encampment, while the greater number rushed against those who were guarding the crossing.

The barbarians, finding the situation utterly unexpected, split: some hurried to the tents to defend them, while others fought back and grappled with the attackers.

Hannibal, with events falling out just as he had planned, immediately formed up the first men as they landed, rallied them with encouragement, and engaged the barbarians.

The Celts, both because of their disorder and because of the unexpected turn of events, were quickly routed and broke into flight.

## Book 3 Chapter 44: Hannibal Addresses His Men

The Carthaginian commander, having now mastered both the crossing and his opponents, at once turned to ferrying over the men still left on the far bank.

When he had brought all his forces across in a short time, he made camp for that night beside the river itself.

The next day, hearing that the Roman fleet had anchored off the mouths of the river, he picked out five hundred Numidian cavalry and sent them off to discover where the enemy were, how many they numbered, and what they were doing.

At the same time he also detailed suitable men to handle the crossing of the elephants.

Then he himself gathered his forces and brought forward the chieftains in Magilus's company—for these had come to him from the plains around the Padus (Po)—and through an interpreter explained to the troops the decisions these men had reached.

Of what was said, the strongest grounds for confidence among the rank and file were, first, the plain fact of the presence of these envoys, who were urging them on and pledging to share in the war against Rome;

second, the trustworthiness of their promise that they would guide the army through such country that, without lacking any of their necessities, they would make their march into Italy both swiftly and safely;

and besides this, the richness and the vast size of the land they were going to reach, and furthermore the eagerness of the men alongside whom they were about to wage their battles against the Roman forces.

So, having spoken to this effect, the Celts withdrew.

After them Hannibal himself came forward and first reminded the troops of their past achievements: in these, he said, though they had undertaken many bold actions and dangers, they had failed in none, so long as they had followed his judgment and counsel.

Next he urged them to be of good courage, seeing that the greatest part of their task had been accomplished, since they had now mastered the crossing of the river and had seen with their own eyes the goodwill and eagerness of their allies.

For this reason he told them not to trouble themselves about the details, as those were his concern, but to obey his commands and so prove themselves brave men, worthy of their past deeds.

When the men responded with approval and showed great drive and enthusiasm, he praised them, prayed to the gods on behalf of them all, and dismissed them, ordering them to look after themselves and to make their preparations with all speed, since the march would begin the next day.

## Book 3 Chapter 45: Skirmish between Reconnoitring Parties

When the assembly had broken up, the Numidians who had been sent ahead to reconnoiter returned, having lost most of their number, while the rest had fled headlong.

For not far from their own camp they had collided with the Roman cavalry that Publius had dispatched on the same errand, and both sides showed such fierce determination in the clash that of the Romans and Celts about a hundred and forty horsemen were killed, and of the Numidians more than two hundred.

After this, the Romans, having pressed close to the Carthaginian stockade in their pursuit and reconnoitered it, turned back and hurried away to report the enemy's presence to their commander; and when they reached their own camp, they delivered their report.

Publius at once loaded his baggage onto the ships, set out with his whole army, and advanced along the river, eager to engage the enemy.

Hannibal, on the day following the assembly, stationed all his cavalry at first light facing toward the sea, holding them in reserve, and moved his infantry force out of the stockade onto the march.

He himself waited behind for the elephants and the men who had been left with them.

## Book 3 Chapter 46: The Crossing of the Elephants

The way the animals were ferried across was roughly this. They built a number of rafts, sturdily put together, and lashed two of these firmly to one another, then anchored both against the ground at the point where the river was to be entered. Together the two had a width of about fifty feet.

To these they joined still others on the outer side and fitted them on, extending the structure of the joined platform out into the channel.

The side facing the current they secured with cables run to the land, fastening them to the trees growing along the bank, so that the whole work would hold together and not be pushed downstream by the river.

When they had made the entire projecting platform about two hundred feet long, they then attached to the outermost rafts two others that had been built especially large. These were bound tightly to the platform's end but connected to the rest only by ties that could be easily cut through. To these last rafts they also fastened several towlines,

with which the boats, towing them, were meant to keep them from being carried off downstream, and instead, holding them by force against the current, were to bring them along and ferry the animals across on them.

After this they heaped a great deal of earth onto all the rafts, piling it on until they had made the surface match, level and the same color as the road that led over dry land to the crossing.

The animals were trained to obey their Indian handlers right up to the water's edge, but would no longer dare in any way to step into the water. So they led them across over the bank of earth, putting two females in front, with the animals following them obediently.

Once the elephants had been brought onto the outermost rafts, the men cut through the ties by which these were attached to the rest, hauled in the towlines with the boats, and quickly pulled both the animals and the rafts beneath them away from the earthen ramp.

When this happened, the beasts, thrown into a panic, at first turned about and rushed in every direction; but, hemmed in on all sides by the current, they grew too frightened to act and were forced to stay where they were.

And it was in just this way, by always attaching a fresh pair of rafts, that most of the animals were carried across.

A few, however, in the middle of the crossing flung themselves into the river out of fear. Of these, all the Indians happened to perish, but the elephants were saved.

For thanks to the strength and length of their trunks, they raised them above the water and breathed through them, while at the same time blowing out everything that got in, and so held out, making most of their way upright along the river bottom.

## Book 3 Chapter 47: Previous Histories of this March either False or Inconsistent

Once the animals had been ferried over, Hannibal took up the elephants and the cavalry and advanced with them as a rearguard along the river, away from the sea and toward the east, making his march as though heading for the interior of Europe.

The Rhone has its sources above the head of the Adriatic Gulf, inclining toward the west, in the parts of the Alps that slope toward the north; it flows toward the winter sunset, and empties into the Sardinian Sea.

It runs for a long way through a valley, to the north of which dwell the Ardyan Celts, while the entire southern side of it is bounded by the foothills of the Alps that incline toward the north. As for the plains around the Po,

about which I have already spoken at some length, the ridges of the aforementioned mountains separate them from the valley of the Rhone; these ridges take their beginning at Massalia and run as far as the head of the whole Adriatic.

It was these that Hannibal then crossed over, passing from the region of the Rhone into Italy.

Some of those who have written about this crossing, in their wish to astonish their readers with marvelous tales about the places mentioned, fall without realizing it into two errors that are more foreign than anything else to all sound history: for they are driven both to tell falsehoods and to write things that contradict themselves.

For on the one hand they bring Hannibal on stage as an inimitable general, both in daring and in foresight, while at the same time they demonstrate to us, by their own account, that he was utterly devoid of judgment;

and on the other hand, being unable to find any resolution or way out of their falsehood, they bring gods and the sons of gods into what is supposed to be factual history.

For having laid it down that the strongholds and ruggedness of the Alpine mountains were such that not only horses and armies, and with them elephants, but not even lightly armed infantry could easily get through; and likewise having sketched the wilderness of those regions as so complete that, unless some god or hero had met Hannibal's men and shown them the roads, they would all have been at a total loss and perished — on both these counts they manifestly fall into the two errors mentioned above.

## Book 3 Chapter 48: Absurd Premises of Other Historians

[They fall into both of these errors.] For, in the first place, what general could be shown to be more devoid of judgment than Hannibal, what commander more inept,

if, leading forces so large and resting his greatest hopes of overall success on them, he knew, as these writers claim, neither the roads nor the country, neither where he was going at all nor against whom,

and, to crown it, not even whether what he was attempting was generally possible?

What men who have utterly failed and are at their wits' end in every way will not bring themselves to do — namely, to plunge into unscouted country with an army — this the writers attribute to Hannibal, who held the greatest hopes for his cause entirely unimpaired.

Likewise their account of the desolation, and further of the ruggedness and difficulty of the terrain, makes their falsehood plain.

For they failed to inquire into the fact that the Celts living along the Rhone, not once nor twice before Hannibal's arrival — and not in the distant past but quite recently — had crossed the Alps with great armies, drawn up against the Romans and fought alongside the Celts inhabiting the plains around the Po, as I have shown in the earlier parts of this work;

and besides this, they did not know that a very large tribe of people happens to live in the Alps themselves. Instead, ignorant of every one of the things I have mentioned, they claim that some hero appeared and pointed out the roads to them.

By this they naturally fall into the same case as the writers of tragedy. For with all of those, the denouements of their dramas require a god and a stage machine, because they take their initial premises to be false and irrational;

and so historians too must suffer and do the same — bringing on heroes and gods to appear — once they have set up beginnings that are implausible and false. For how is it possible to set a reasonable ending upon irrational beginnings?

Hannibal, at any rate, did not act as these men write; on the contrary, he handled his designs in these matters with great practical sense.

For he had clearly investigated both the fertility of the land into which he intended to descend and the hostility of its peoples toward the Romans, and for the difficult ground that lay between he employed local guides and leaders who were going to share in the same hopes as himself.

I speak about these things with confidence because I have inquired into the events from men who were themselves present at the time, and have surveyed the country and used the Alpine route myself, for the sake of knowledge and of seeing it firsthand.

## Book 3 Chapter 49: Scipio Is Too Late To Stop Hannibal

Nevertheless, Publius, the Roman general, arriving at the river crossing three days after the Carthaginians had broken camp, and finding that the enemy had already set out, was as thoroughly astonished as could be,

since he had been convinced that they would never dare to make their march into Italy by this route, because of the numbers and the faithlessness of the barbarians inhabiting those regions.

But seeing that they had in fact dared it, he hurried back to his ships, and on arriving began to embark his forces.

He sent his brother off to handle affairs in Iberia, while he himself turned back and made the voyage to Italy, eager to outpace the enemy by going through Etruria to the crossing of the Alps.

Hannibal, meanwhile, after marching four days in succession from the crossing, came to the place called the Island, a populous and grain-bearing district that takes its name from the very circumstance of its situation.

For where the Rhone runs along one side and the river called the Isère along the other, flowing past on each flank, they bring its shape to a point at the place where they converge with each other.

It is similar in size and shape to what is called the Delta in Egypt, except that in the Delta the sea joins together its one side and the mouths of the rivers, whereas here that side is closed off by mountains hard to approach, hard to enter, and, one might almost say, inaccessible.

On reaching this district, he found in it two brothers quarreling over the kingship and encamped against each other with their armies.

When the elder courted him and called on him to join in helping him secure the throne, he agreed, since the advantage this would bring him for the present was almost obvious.

And so, by joining the attack and helping to drive out the other brother, he gained much assistance from the victor.

For this man not only supplied the army lavishly with grain and the other provisions, but also exchanged all their old and worn-out weapons and renewed the equipment of the whole force at an opportune moment;

and further, by furnishing most of the men with clothing and, on top of that, with footwear, he provided great practical benefit for the crossings of the mountains.

But most important of all, since the Carthaginians were apprehensive about the march through the territory of the Gauls called Allobroges, he brought up the rear with his own force and secured them a safe passage until they drew near to the crossing of the Alps.

## Book 3 Chapter 50: Hannibal Harassed By the Natives

After marching for ten days along the river, covering some eight hundred stades, Hannibal began the climb up toward the Alps, and it was now that he fell into the gravest dangers.

For as long as the army was on level ground, all the local chieftains of the Allobroges held back from attacking, partly out of fear of the cavalry and partly because of the barbarians who were escorting the column. But once the escort had withdrawn to their own country,

and Hannibal's forces began to push forward into the difficult terrain, the Allobrogian chieftains then mustered a sizable body of men and seized in advance the strategic positions through which Hannibal's troops were compelled to make their ascent.

Now if they had kept their plan hidden, they would have utterly destroyed the Carthaginian army. As it was, since they were spotted, they did indeed do serious harm to Hannibal's men, but they suffered no less themselves.

For once the Carthaginian general realized that the natives had occupied the advantageous positions ahead of him, he encamped at the foot of the pass and waited there,

and he sent ahead some of the Gauls who were serving as his guides to scout out the enemy's intentions and their whole plan.

When these men had carried out their orders, the general learned that during the day the enemy kept careful watch and held the positions in force, but at night they withdrew to a nearby town. Adapting himself to this state of affairs, he devised the following scheme.

He took up his army and advanced openly, and on coming near the difficult ground he encamped not far from the enemy. When night fell, he ordered the watch-fires to be lit and left the greater part of his army where it was; then, taking the men best suited for the task and equipping them lightly, he passed through the narrow defiles during the night and occupied the positions the enemy had seized earlier, the barbarians having withdrawn to the town as was their custom.

## Book 3 Chapter 51: The Gauls Attack the Baggage Train

When this had happened and day broke, the barbarians saw what had occurred and at first gave up their attempt.

But afterward, watching the great mass of pack animals and the cavalry struggling to wind their way slowly through the difficult ground, they were drawn by the opportunity to fasten onto the column.

When they did so and the barbarians attacked at many points, the Carthaginians suffered heavy losses—not so much from the enemy as from the terrain itself, and above all among the horses and pack animals.

For the approach was not only narrow and rough but also flanked by sheer drops, so that at every disturbance or panic many of the pack animals were sent tumbling over the cliffs along with their loads.

It was the wounded horses most of all that caused this kind of chaos. Some of them, panicked by their wounds, would collide head-on with the pack animals, while others, charging forward, shoved aside everything in their path on the narrow ground, and so they created enormous confusion.

Watching all this, and reckoning that even those who survived the present danger could not be saved if the baggage train were destroyed, Hannibal took the men who had seized the heights during the night and rushed down to help those who had gone ahead on the march.

In doing so, since Hannibal was charging from higher ground, many of the enemy were killed—but no fewer of his own men perished as well,

for the uproar along the column was now mounting on both sides because of the shouting and the clash described above.

But once he had killed most of the Allobroges and routed the rest, forcing them to flee back to their own land, the remaining mass of pack animals and horses then made its way through the difficult ground, slowly and with great hardship.

Hannibal himself rallied as many men as he could after the engagement and assaulted the town from which the enemy had launched their attack.

Finding it nearly deserted, since everyone had been lured out after plunder, he took control of the town. From this he gained many things useful both for the moment and for the future.

For the immediate present he recovered a quantity of horses and pack animals along with the men captured with them, and for the future he secured a supply of grain and livestock enough for two or three days.

But the most important result was the fear he instilled in the tribes that came next, so that none of those living near the passes would readily dare to attack him.

## Book 3 Chapter 52: Treachery of the Gauls

For the time being, then, he made his camp on the spot, stayed for one day, and then set out again.

Over the following days he led the army on in safety for a while; but by the fourth day he once more found himself in great danger.

For the people living along his route, having conspired together with treacherous intent, came out to meet him carrying branches and garlands—which among nearly all the natives is a token of friendship, just as the herald's staff is among the Greeks.

Wary of trusting such assurances, Hannibal carefully questioned them about their intentions and their whole purpose.

They claimed to be well aware both of the town's capture and of the destruction of those who had tried to do him wrong, and they made clear that this was precisely why they had come: they wished neither to inflict nor to suffer any harm, and they even promised to hand over hostages from among themselves.

For a long time Hannibal remained cautious and distrustful of what they said. But reasoning that if he accepted their offer he might perhaps make these new arrivals more cautious and more peaceable, whereas if he refused he would have them as open enemies, he assented to their proposal and pretended to enter into friendship with them.

When the barbarians had handed over the hostages, supplied cattle in abundance, and altogether put themselves into his hands without reserve, Hannibal's men came to trust them to a certain extent—so much so that they even used them as guides for the difficult ground that lay ahead. But after these guides had led the way for two days,

## Book 3 Chapter 53: The White Rock

While they were crossing, there came a moment when Hannibal's entire force would have been completely destroyed, had he not still been wary to some degree and, foreseeing what lay ahead, kept the baggage train and the cavalry in the vanguard and the heavy infantry in the rear.

Because these troops were posted as a rearguard, the damage that occurred was less severe, for they absorbed the onslaught of the barbarians.

Even so, despite this precaution, a great number of men, pack animals, and horses were destroyed.

For since the high ground was held by the enemy, the barbarians moved along the slopes parallel to the column, rolling rocks down on some and striking others at close range with stones, and so threw them into utter confusion and peril,

Some of them, attacking from the rear and others from the front whenever the opportunity arose, would carry off part of the baggage. His elephants were of the greatest service to him in this:

for at whatever point in the march these animals happened to be, the enemy did not dare to approach that part of the line, terrified by the strange sight of the creatures.

On the ninth day he reached the summit of the pass, encamped there, and remained for two days, wishing both to rest the survivors and to wait for the stragglers to catch up.

## Book 3 Chapter 54: The Descent Into Italy

...and rejoined the camp. By now, since the setting of the Pleiades was drawing near, the snow was already gathering on the peaks; and seeing his men in low spirits, both because of the hardship they had already endured and because of what they still expected to face,

Therefore, by pointing out to them the plains around the Po, and by reminding them in general of the goodwill of the Gauls who inhabited them, and at the same time indicating the location of Rome itself, he succeeded to some extent in restoring the men's confidence.

The next day he broke camp and began the descent. In the course of it he no longer encountered any enemy, except those who did damage by stealth, but because of the terrain and the snow he lost a number of men not much smaller than those he had lost on the ascent.

For since the descent was narrow and steep, and the snow made it impossible for each man to see where he was stepping, anyone who strayed from the path or slipped was carried over the cliffs.

Still, they endured this hardship, being by now accustomed to such troubles.

But when they came to a place that neither the elephants nor the pack animals could get past because of its narrowness—where the cliff face had fallen away for nearly a stade and a half, having been broken before but having now collapsed even more freshly—there once again the men fell into despair and confusion.

At first the Carthaginian general attempted to go around the difficult ground; but when fresh snow fell and made this route also impossible to travel,

## Book 3 Chapter 55: Snow

he abandoned the attempt. For what was happening was peculiar and out of the ordinary. The new snow of that year had just fallen on top of the old snow, which had remained from the previous winter; the fresh snow was easy to cut through, both because it was recent and soft and because it had not yet gained any depth.

But once they had trodden through this and stepped onto the snow underneath, which had hardened, they no longer cut through it; instead they slid on the surface, both feet skidding at once, just as happens on level ground to people walking through mud.

And what followed from this was still more difficult.

For the men, being unable to break through the lower snow, whenever they fell and tried to brace themselves on their knees or hands in order to get up, only slid all the more, with every support giving way at once, since the ground sloped steeply downward.

The pack animals, on the other hand, when they fell, did break through the lower snow as they struggled to rise; but once they had broken through, they remained stuck there with their loads, as if frozen in place, both because of the weight and because of the firmness of the old snow underneath.

So, giving up this hope, he encamped on the ridge, clearing away the snow on it, and then set the men to work and, with great toil, built up a road along the cliff face.

For the pack animals and the horses he made a passage wide enough in a single day. He therefore led these across at once and, encamping at the spots that were already below the snow line, sent them off to pasture;

while he brought up the Numidians in relays to work on the road, and only after three days of painful labor did he get the elephants across. These were in a wretched state from hunger,

for the highest parts of the Alps and the areas reaching up toward the passes are entirely without trees and completely bare, because the snow lies there continuously in both summer and winter, whereas the parts about halfway down the slopes, on both sides, are wooded, bear trees, and are altogether habitable.

## Book 3 Chapter 56: Hannibal Reaches the Plains

Hannibal then gathered his whole force together and continued the descent, and on the third day after passing the cliffs just described he reached the level country.

He had lost many of his soldiers to the enemy and at the river crossings during the march as a whole, and many more to the cliffs and difficult terrain in the Alps—not only men, but still greater numbers of horses and pack animals.

In the end, having made the entire march from New Carthage in five months, and the crossing of the Alps in fifteen days, he boldly descended into the plains around the Po and the territory of the Insubres,

with the surviving portion of his army: twelve thousand Libyan infantry, about eight thousand Iberian infantry, and cavalry numbering altogether not more than six thousand, as he himself sets out on the inscribed column concerning his numbers at Lacinium.

At the same time, as I stated above, Publius, having left his forces to his brother Gnaeus and urged him to hold fast to affairs in Iberia and to wage war vigorously against Hasdrubal, sailed back himself with a few men to Pisae.

He then marched through Etruria, took over from the praetors the legions that were stationed there and campaigning against the Boii, and arrived at the plains around the Po; pitching his camp, he kept watch on the enemy, eager to engage them in battle.

## Book 3 Chapter 57: The Limits of History

Now that we have brought both the narrative and the commanders of both sides, along with the war itself, into Italy, I wish, before the battles begin, to discuss briefly a few matters that bear on my work.

For some readers will perhaps ask why, having devoted so much discussion to the regions of Libya and Iberia, I have said nothing further about the strait at the Pillars of Heracles, or about the outer sea and its distinctive features,

nor indeed about the British Isles and the production of tin there, nor about the silver and gold mines of Iberia itself—subjects on which the historians, disputing with one another, expend a great deal of discussion.

Now, I passed these over not because I think this branch of inquiry foreign to history, but, in the first place, because I did not wish to keep breaking up the narrative at every turn, nor to lead those eager to listen away from the practical subject before us;

and, in the second place, because I judged it best not to treat these matters in a scattered way or as a mere by-the-way digression, but to set aside a place and an occasion of their own for this subject and, as far as I am able, to give a truthful account of them there.

For this reason no one should be surprised if, in what follows as well, when I come to regions of this kind, I pass over this subject for the reasons already stated.

But if there are those who absolutely insist on hearing about such matters region by region and part by part, they are perhaps unaware that they are doing much the same as greedy dinner guests.

For those guests, by sampling everything set before them, neither truly enjoy any of the dishes at the moment nor draw from them any wholesome digestion and nourishment for the future, but quite the opposite;

and those who do the same with their reading likewise fail to attain, as they should, either genuine pleasure in the present or benefit for the future.

## Book 3 Chapter 58: Necessity of Refuting Past Errors

That this department, if any in history, also requires discussion and a more accurate correction is plain from many considerations, but above all from the following.

For since nearly all writers—or at any rate the great majority—have attempted to set out the peculiar characteristics and positions of the regions at the far edges of the world we inhabit, yet in many points most of them have gone badly wrong,

it is by no means proper to pass this over; rather, something must be said to them, not as a by-the-way or scattered remark, but with careful attention—and it must be said not by way of censure or rebuke,

but rather with praise and with a correction of their ignorance, recognizing that they too, had they had access to our own times, would have brought many of their statements to correction and revision.

For in earlier times one could find very few Greeks who undertook to investigate the matters of these remote regions, because the attempt was impossible.

For the dangers by sea were then many and hard to reckon, and those by land many times greater still.

But even if someone did reach the ends of the inhabited world, whether by necessity or by choice, even so he did not accomplish his purpose.

For it was difficult to become an eyewitness of more than a few of these places, since some had grown wholly barbarous and others were uninhabited; and it was harder still to learn and understand by conversation anything about what one had seen, because of the strangeness of the language.

And even if someone did gain such understanding, it was harder still than any of these things for one who had seen the places to handle the matter with restraint—to scorn marvel-mongering and sensationalism for his own glory, to value the truth above all, and to report to us nothing of what lies outside it.

## Book 3 Chapter 59: Recent Advances in Geography

Since, then, a true account of the matters just mentioned was, in former times at least, not merely difficult but very nearly impossible, it is not right to censure the historians if they omitted something or made mistakes;

rather, it is just to praise and admire them for whatever knowledge they did gain and for the way they advanced our experience of these matters in such an age.

But in our own day, the regions of Asia have become accessible through the empire of Alexander, and the remaining regions through the supremacy of the Romans, so that nearly all of them can now be reached by sea and by land;

and since men of action, released from their ambitions in military and political affairs, have through these means gained many great opportunities for investigation and for the pursuit of knowledge about the matters just mentioned,

it would only be fitting that we should have both better and truer knowledge concerning what was formerly unknown. This is what I myself will also try to do,

taking a fitting place in my work for this subject, and I will wish to inform those eager to learn more fully about the matters just mentioned;

for it was largely for this purpose that I undertook the dangers [and the hardships] that befell me in my wanderings through Libya and Iberia, and also Gaul and the sea that lies beyond these countries and borders upon them,

in order that, by correcting the ignorance of those who came before, I might make these parts of the inhabited world too familiar to the Greeks.

But now, returning from this digression to the narrative, I will try to set out the pitched battles that took place in Italy between the Romans and the Carthaginians.

## Book 3 Chapter 60: Hannibal Attacks the Taurini

I have already given an account of the size of the army with which Hannibal invaded Italy.

After completing the invasion, he encamped at the very base of the Alps and at first set about restoring his forces.

For his entire army had not only been terribly worn down by the climbs and descents, and still more by the rough ground along the passes, but it was also in a wretched condition from the scarcity of provisions and from the lack of any care for the men's bodies.

Many had given themselves up entirely, broken by the want and by the unrelenting hardships. It had been impossible to carry an ample supply of food for so many tens of thousands across such terrain, and even what they did bring along was for the most part lost when the pack animals perished.

Thus, when he set out from the crossing of the Rhône he had some thirty-eight thousand infantry and more than eight thousand cavalry, and he lost nearly half of his force in the passes, as I stated above.

Even those who survived had become almost like wild beasts, in both their appearance and their general condition, because of the relentless hardships I have described.

Hannibal therefore took great care over their treatment and worked to restore both the spirits and the bodies of his men, and likewise of his horses.

After this, once his army had recovered its strength, he found that the Taurini, who happen to live near the foot of the mountains, were at odds with the Insubres and distrustful of the Carthaginians.

At first he invited them into friendship and alliance; but when they would not listen, he laid siege to their strongest city and stormed it within three days.

By slaughtering those who had resisted him, he instilled such fear in the neighbouring barbarian tribes that they all came over to him at once, surrendering themselves into his trust.

As for the rest of the Celts inhabiting the plains, they were eager to make common cause with the Carthaginians, in keeping with their original intention.

But since the Roman legions had by now advanced past most of them and cut them off, they kept quiet; and some were even compelled to serve alongside the Romans.

Seeing this, Hannibal decided not to delay but to push forward and to do something that would give heart to those who wished to share in the same hopes as himself.

## Book 3 Chapter 61: Approach of Scipio

While he was set on this purpose, Hannibal heard that Publius had already crossed the Padus with his forces and was close at hand. At first he refused to believe the reports,

reflecting that only a few days earlier he had left Publius behind near the crossing of the Rhône, and reckoning that the voyage from Massalia to Tyrrhenia was long and difficult to make.

Besides this, he had learned that the route from the Tyrrhenian Sea through Italy up to the Alps was a long one and hard for an army to traverse.

But as more and more men reported it, each with greater certainty, he was astonished and amazed at the consul's whole plan and the way he had carried it out.

Publius felt much the same. At first he had not even expected that Hannibal would attempt the march through the Alps with an army of foreign peoples; and even if he did dare it, he supposed the man would clearly be destroyed. So, while he was occupied with such thoughts,

when he learned that Hannibal had come through safely and was already besieging some towns in Italy, he was amazed at the man's audacity and reckless daring.

The people at Rome experienced just the same feelings about the news that reached them.

For the last report about the Carthaginians—that they had taken Saguntum—had only just ceased to be the talk of the city, and they had only just adopted measures in response to it: they had sent off one of the consuls to Libya to besiege Carthage itself, and the other to Iberia to wage war against Hannibal there. Then word arrived that Hannibal was present in Italy with his army and was already besieging some of its towns.

Since what was happening seemed to them incredible, they were thrown into immediate confusion and sent dispatches to Tiberius at Lilybaeum, informing him of the enemy's arrival and judging that he must abandon his current operations and come in all haste to aid their own affairs.

Tiberius at once assembled the crews of the fleet and sent them off, with orders to make the voyage home; and through the military tribunes he put the land forces under oath, fixing a day by which all of them were to be present at Ariminum for the night.

This is a city lying on the Adriatic, at the southern edge of the plains around the Padus.

With this upheaval breaking out on every side, and with events befalling everyone contrary to expectation, there was in every quarter no small anxiety about what was to come.

## Book 3 Chapter 62: Gallic Prisoners

At this time, as Hannibal and Publius were now drawing near to one another, each set about exhorting his own troops, presenting whatever suited the present circumstances.

Hannibal undertook to deliver his encouragement in the following way.

He gathered the men together and brought forward some young captives whom he had taken while they were harassing his march in the difficult country around the Alps.

He had treated these men harshly, preparing them for what he now intended: they wore heavy chains, were wasted by hunger, and their bodies were ruined by the lashes they had received.

Seating these prisoners in the middle, he set out before them suits of Gallic armour, of the kind in which their kings are accustomed to deck themselves when they are about to fight in single combat; and in addition he placed horses there and brought in costly cloaks.

Then he asked the young men which of them were willing to fight one another in single combat, on the terms that the victor would take the prizes set before them, while the loser would be released from his present miseries by death.

When they all cried out together that they wished to fight, he ordered them to draw lots, and commanded the two on whom the lot fell to arm themselves and fight each other.

The moment the young men heard this, they lifted up their hands and prayed to the gods, each eager that he himself might be one of those chosen by lot.

And when the result of the lottery was announced, those who had been picked were overjoyed, while the rest felt the opposite.

When the combat took place, the captives who were left behind counted the dead man no less fortunate than the victor, since the one had been released from many great miseries, while they themselves were still left to endure them.

The same view was held by most of the Carthaginians as well: for as they watched, by way of comparison, the wretchedness of those who were being led off still alive, they pitied these men, while the one who had died they accounted

altogether fortunate. Having by these means worked the desired disposition into the spirits of his troops, Hannibal

then came forward himself and declared that this was why he had brought in the captives: so that, seeing vividly in the misfortunes of others what was at stake, his men might make better plans regarding their own situation.

For Fortune, he said, had confined them to a struggle and a moment very like that of the captives, and had set before them prizes very like those just offered.

They must either conquer, or die, or fall alive into the hands of their enemies. The prize for victory was not horses and cloaks, but to become the most fortunate of all men, having mastered the prosperity of Rome.

If they should fall while fighting—battling to their last breath for the noblest of hopes—they would exchange this life in the press of combat without ever tasting any misery;

but for those who were beaten, and who out of a craving to stay alive chose to flee, or by some other means clung to life, every misery and every misfortune would be their portion.

For not one of them, he said, could be so unreasoning or so dull that, remembering the length of the road he had travelled from his homeland, remembering the multitude of enemies that lay between, and knowing the size of the rivers he had crossed, he could ever hope to reach his own country by running away.

Therefore he thought that, with such a hope cut off entirely, they ought to take the very same view of their own situation that they had just now taken of the misfortunes of others.

For just as, in the case of those captives, everyone counted both the victor and the dead man fortunate while pitying the survivors, so, he thought, they ought to judge their own case, and all of them go into the contest meaning above all to win, but, if that should not be possible, to die.

The hope of living on in defeat he urged them not to entertain in their minds in any way whatever.

For if they adopted this reasoning and this resolve, then both victory and survival would clearly follow for them.

For all those who, whether by deliberate choice or by necessity, have adopted such a resolve have never been cheated of mastery over their opponents.

And when it also happens that the enemy hold the opposite hope—as is now the case with the Romans, so that for most of them safety plainly lies in flight, with their homeland near at hand—then it is clear that the daring of men who have abandoned all hope would prove irresistible.

Since the mass of the men accepted both the example and the words, and took on the drive and resolve that the speaker had striven to produce, Hannibal then praised them and dismissed them; and on the next day he ordered them to break camp at first light.

## Book 3 Chapter 64: Scipio Crosses the Ticinus

Around the same days Publius, having now crossed the river Padus and having decided to advance still farther by crossing the Ticinus, ordered the men skilled in such work to build a bridge over the latter river, while he gathered the rest of his forces together and addressed them.

Most of what he said concerned the prestige of their homeland and the achievements of their ancestors, but the part bearing on the present moment ran as follows.

He told them that, even though they had as yet gained no actual experience of the enemy, the mere fact that they knew they were about to face Carthaginians in battle should make their hope of victory beyond all dispute.

And in general he said it would be an outrageous and absurd thing if the Carthaginians dared to look the Romans in the eye—men who had often been beaten by them, who had paid them heavy tribute over and over, and who for so many years now had been all but their slaves.

"And when, quite apart from all this, we have already had some experience of these very men now before us—namely that they do not dare to face us head-on—what conclusion should those who reason correctly draw about what is to come?

What is more, their cavalry, when it clashed with our horsemen at the river Rhone, came off badly: they lost many of their own men and fled in disgrace all the way back to their own camp.

And their general together with his whole army, the moment they learned that our soldiers were near, made a retreat that was little better than a flight, and out of fear, against their own intentions, resorted to the march over the Alps.

Hannibal is here now, he said, having destroyed the greater part of his army, while what remains is feeble and unfit for service because of the hardships they have suffered; likewise he has lost the greater part of his horses and rendered the rest useless owing to the length and difficulty of the journey.

By all this he tried to show that they had only to make their appearance before the enemy.

Above all he urged them to take heart by looking to his own presence among them: for he would never have left his fleet and his campaign in Iberia, the task he had been sent to carry out, and come here in such haste, had he not seen very clearly, on rational grounds, that this action was both necessary for his country and certain to bring victory.

Since all the men, both from their trust in the speaker and from the truth of what he said, were eager for the battle, he welcomed their enthusiasm and dismissed them, urging them in addition to be ready for whatever order should be passed along.

## Book 3 Chapter 65: Cavalry Engagement on the Ticinus

On the very next day both sides advanced along the river on the side toward the Alps, the Romans keeping the stream on their left and the Carthaginians on their right.

On the second day, learning through their foraging parties that they were close to one another, they encamped where they were and stayed there for the time being.

On the following day both sides took all their cavalry—and Publius also the javelin-throwers from among his infantry—and advanced across the plain, eager to scout out each other's forces.

As soon as they drew near and saw the dust rising up, they at once began to form up for battle.

Publius, placing his javelin-throwers and the Gallic cavalry that accompanied them in front, and stationing the rest in a line abreast, advanced at a walking pace.

Hannibal, drawing up his bridled cavalry and all the steadier part of his horse facing the front, went to meet the enemy, while he held the Numidian cavalry ready on each wing for an encircling movement.

Since both the commanders and the horsemen on each side were keen for the contest, the first clash turned out to be such that the javelin-throwers did not even manage to let fly their first missile, but reeled back and fled at once through the gaps to the rear of their own squadrons, panicked by the charge and terrified of being trampled by the cavalry bearing down on them.

Those who met one another face to face kept the contest evenly balanced for a long time;

for it was at once a cavalry battle and an infantry battle, because of the number of men who dismounted in the very midst of the fighting.

But when the Numidians had encircled them and fallen on them from behind, the foot javelin-throwers, who at first had escaped the cavalry clash, were now trampled down by the mass and the onslaught of the Numidians;

while those who from the start had been fighting the Carthaginians face to face—having lost many of their own men, but having destroyed still more of the Carthaginians—now, attacked from the rear by the Numidians, were put to flight: most of them scattered, but some rallied around their commander.

## Book 3 Chapter 66: Scipio Crosses the Po

Publius then broke camp and advanced across the plains toward the bridge over the Padus, in haste to get his legions across first.

For seeing that the ground was level, that the enemy was superior in cavalry, and that he himself was weighed down by his wound, he judged it necessary to bring his forces back to safety.

Hannibal for a while supposed that they would risk a battle with their infantry too; but when he saw that they had moved out of their camp, he followed as far as the first river and the bridge over it.

Finding that most of the planks had been torn up, but that the men guarding the bridge were still left behind near the river, he took these prisoner—about six hundred of them in number.

Then, hearing that the rest had already gotten a long way ahead, he turned about again in the opposite direction and made his march along the river, hurrying to reach a point on the Padus where it could easily be bridged.

On the second day he halted and bridged the crossing with river boats, and assigned to Hasdrubal the task of bringing the main body across, while he himself crossed at once and dealt with the envoys who had arrived from the neighboring districts.

For as soon as he had gained the advantage, all the neighboring Celts hastened, in keeping with their original intention, to become his friends, to supply him, and to join the Carthaginians on campaign.

After receiving those who had come in a friendly manner and bringing his forces over from the far side, he advanced along the river, taking the opposite direction to his earlier march: for he now made his way downstream, eager to come to grips with the enemy.

Publius, having crossed the Padus and encamped near the city of Placentia, which was a Roman colony, attended at once to himself and the other wounded, and at the same time, considering that he had brought his forces into a safe position, kept quiet.

Hannibal, arriving on the second day after his crossing near the enemy, on the third day drew up his army in full view of his opponents.

But when no one came out against him, he pitched camp, leaving an interval of about fifty stades between the two armies.

## Book 3 Chapter 67: The Gauls Join Hannibal

The Celts who were serving alongside the Romans, seeing that the Carthaginians' prospects were the more promising of the two, made an agreement among themselves and watched for the right moment to strike, each remaining meanwhile in his own tent.

After the men in the camp had eaten their supper and gone to sleep, the Celts let most of the night pass, and then, around the time of the morning watch, they armed themselves and fell upon the Romans encamped nearest to them.

They killed many of them and wounded a good number more; and in the end, after cutting off the heads of the dead, they withdrew to the Carthaginians, numbering about two thousand infantry and a little under two hundred cavalry.

Hannibal welcomed their arrival warmly. He at once encouraged them, promised each of them suitable rewards, and sent them off to their own cities to report to their fellow citizens what they had done and to urge them to join his alliance.

For he knew that, once they learned of the act of treachery committed against the Romans by their own countrymen, they would all be compelled to throw in their lot with him.

At the same time the Boii also arrived and handed over to him the three men whom the Romans had sent out to distribute the land—men whom they had seized at the very start of the war by breaking the truce, as I have already described above.

Hannibal welcomed their goodwill and gave pledges of friendship and alliance to those who had come; but he returned the men to them, instructing them to keep them safe, so that through them they might recover their own hostages, in keeping with their original purpose.

Publius, indignant at the treachery that had occurred, reasoned that since the Celts had long been hostile toward the Romans, the defection of these men would cause all the surrounding Gauls to go over to the Carthaginians; and so he decided that he must guard against what was coming.

For this reason, when night fell, he broke camp toward dawn and set out toward the river Trebia and the hills adjoining it, trusting in the strength of the terrain

and in the support of the allies who lived nearby. When Hannibal learned that they had broken camp, he immediately sent out the Numidian cavalry, and shortly afterward the rest, while he himself followed close behind with his main army.

The Numidians, finding the camp deserted, set it on fire.

This proved a great benefit to the Romans; for if the Numidians had instead pressed on at their heels and overtaken the baggage train, many of the Romans would have been cut down by the cavalry on the open plains.

As it was, however, most of them managed to cross the river Trebia first; of those left behind in the rearguard, some were killed and others taken alive by the Carthaginians.

Publius, then, having crossed the river just mentioned, made camp on the first line of hills,

and after surrounding his position with a ditch and a palisade, he waited for Tiberius and the forces with him, while attending carefully to his own recovery, eager to be able to take part in the coming engagement.

Hannibal pitched his camp about forty stades away from the enemy.

The mass of the Celts who inhabited the plains, having committed themselves to the Carthaginians' hopes, supplied the army abundantly with provisions and were ready to share in every undertaking and danger alongside Hannibal.

The people in Rome, when news of the cavalry engagement reached them, were taken aback that the outcome had run contrary to their expectations; yet they were not at a loss for excuses to convince themselves that what had happened was not a defeat.

Some blamed the rashness of the general, others the deliberate treachery of the Celts, which they inferred from the latter's recent defection.

In general, since their infantry legions remained intact, they reckoned their hopes for the overall outcome were likewise intact.

And so, when Tiberius and the legions with him arrived and were marching through Rome, the people imagined that the mere sight of them would decide the battle.

When the soldiers had gathered at Ariminum in accordance with their oath, the general took them up and led them forward, hurrying to join Publius's forces.

Having effected the junction and pitched camp beside them with his own troops, he let his men recover their strength—for they had marched on foot continuously for forty days from Lilybaeum to Ariminum—while he made every preparation as if for battle,

and he himself conferred carefully with Publius, inquiring into what had already taken place and deliberating together about the present situation.

## Book 3 Chapter 69: A Skirmish Near the Trebia

## Book 3 Chapter 75: Better Success in Spain

Tiberius, well aware of what had happened but wishing as far as possible to hide the disaster from the people in Rome, sent messengers to report that a battle had been fought but that the storm had robbed them of the victory.

For the moment the Romans believed the reports that reached them; but before long they learned that the Carthaginians were holding their own camp and that all the Celts had gone over to friendship with them,

while their own men had abandoned their camp, had withdrawn from the field, had all gathered together into the cities, and were being supplied with necessities by sea up the river Padus. They then understood all too clearly what had really happened in the battle.

## Book 3 Chapter 76: Gnaeus Scipio in Spain

## Book 3 Chapter 77: Hannibal's Treatment of Roman Prisoners

Such was the state of affairs in Iberia. As the spring season set in, Gaius Flaminius took up his forces and advanced through Etruria, pitching camp before the city of the Arretines,

## Book 3 Chapter 78: Hannibal Marches Southward

During his time in winter quarters he also employed a certain stratagem that was thoroughly Phoenician in character.

Because he was anxious about the unreliability of the Celts and about possible plots against his life—the alliance between himself and them being so recently formed—he had a set of false hairpieces made, each one suited to the appearance natural to a different age of life.

These he wore constantly, changing them from one to another, and in the same way he kept changing his clothing as well, always to match whichever wig he was wearing.

By this means he was hard to recognize, not only for those who caught sight of him suddenly, but even for those who were on familiar terms with him.

He observed, however, that the Celts were resentful at the war dragging on in their own territory, and that they were eager and on edge to push into enemy land—ostensibly out of anger toward the Romans, but mostly for the sake of plunder. He therefore decided to break camp as quickly as possible and to satisfy the army's impulses.

And so, as soon as the season began to change, he made inquiries of those reputed to have the best knowledge of the country. He found that the other routes for invading enemy territory were long and obvious to the enemy, whereas the one leading through the marshes into Etruria, though difficult, was short and would catch Flaminius and his men completely off guard.

Since he was always by temperament drawn to this kind of approach, he resolved to make his march by this route.

## Book 3 Chapter 80: Flaminius in Hannibal's Way

Having crossed the marshy terrain in this unexpected fashion, and finding Flaminius in Etruria encamped before the city of Arretium, Hannibal for the moment pitched camp there beside the marshes,

He learned that the country in front of him was rich in plunder, and that Flaminius was a consummate rabble-rouser and demagogue but had no natural aptitude for the genuine conduct of military affairs, and on top of this was overconfident in his own resources.

He therefore calculated that if he marched past their camp and descended into the territory beyond, Flaminius—partly anxious about the jeers of the crowd, unable to bear watching the land being ravaged, and partly stung by personal feeling—would readily come along, following him into any region, eager to win the success on his own and not to wait for the arrival of his colleague, who held equal authority.

From all this he reckoned that Flaminius would hand him many openings for an attack.

## Book 3 Chapter 81: A General Must Know his Enemy's Character

He reasoned all this out with intelligence and practical sense. For it would not be wrong to say that anyone who supposes there is any more decisive element of generalship than knowing the purpose and character of the opposing commander is ignorant and deluded.

Just as in contests between individuals or between ranks the man who means to win must observe carefully how he can reach his goal and what part of his opponent appears exposed or insufficiently armored,

so too those in command of whole armies must look not for where some part of the body is unguarded, but for where some vulnerable point in the mind of the enemy commander shows itself.

For many men, through laziness and sheer idleness, utterly throw away not only the affairs of the state but their own private lives as well;

many, through their craving for wine, cannot even fall asleep without a stupefying bout of drunkenness;

and some, through their lust for sexual pleasure and their obsession with it, have not only brought ruin on cities and lives but have even forfeited their own existence in disgrace.

And indeed, cowardice and slackness bring reproach on the individuals who have them privately, but when they reside in a supreme commander they become a public matter and the greatest of calamities.

For such a commander not only renders his subordinates ineffective, but often brings the gravest dangers upon those who have placed their trust in him.

Recklessness, too, and audacity and irrational temper, and likewise vainglory and conceit, are easy prey for the enemy but most perilous for one's friends. For a man of that sort is an easy mark for every plot, ambush, and deception.

So if a man can discern the failings of those around him, and approach his adversaries precisely at the point and by the means through which the enemy's leader is most vulnerable, he would very quickly master the whole campaign. For just as, if you remove the helmsman from a ship,

the entire vessel with all its crew falls into the hands of the enemy, in the same way, if a man outmaneuvers the commander of an army through his designs and calculations, he often gains possession of the opposing force, men and all.

It was precisely by foreseeing and reasoning out these things about the enemy commander that Hannibal, on this occasion, did not fail in his design.

## Book 3 Chapter 85: Roman Reaction

So that affair was brought to its conclusion. Hannibal, once the men who had surrendered under terms had been brought back to him, along with the rest of the prisoners as well, gathered them all together — more than fifteen thousand of them.

He then let his own forces recover their strength, and he buried the most distinguished of the dead from his own army, about thirty in number; for in all about fifteen hundred had fallen, most of whom were Celts.

Having done this, he began to deliberate with his brother and his friends about where and how he ought to press his advance, since he was now confident about the whole campaign.

When news of the disaster that had occurred reached Rome, the leaders of the state were unable to disguise or play down what had happened, because of the sheer scale of the calamity, and they were forced to tell the people what had taken place, calling the citizens together into an assembly.

And so, the moment the magistrate announced to the crowds from the Rostra, "We have lost a great battle," such a panic broke out that, to those who had been present on both occasions, the event then seemed far greater than at the actual moment of the battle itself.

And this was natural enough. For having had no experience, for many years, either of the word or of the fact of an acknowledged defeat, they bore the reversal with no moderation and no composure.

The Senate, however, did not do so: it held to the reasoning that the situation demanded, and deliberated about the future — how, and what, each group ought to do.

## Book 3 Chapter 86: A Second Disaster in Etruria

At the time of the battle, Gnaeus Servilius, the consul stationed forward in the region of Ariminum —

having heard that Hannibal had invaded Etruria and was encamped opposite Flaminius, intended to join all his own legions to him. But, unable to do so because of the weight of his army, he hurriedly sent Gaius Centenius ahead with four thousand cavalry, wishing them to arrive in advance of his own coming, should circumstances require it.

Hannibal, when the enemy's relief force was reported to him after the battle, sent out Maharbal with the spearmen and a portion of the cavalry.

These troops fell upon Gaius's men and, in the very first engagement, destroyed nearly half of them, and after driving the rest together onto a certain hill, on the following day took them all prisoner.

In Rome, with the report of the battle now three days old, and the suffering throughout the city at that very moment, so to speak, at its most inflamed, when this further reversal also came on top of it, not only the populace but the Senate itself was thrown into dismay.

For this reason they set aside the annual conduct of public business and the election of magistrates, and undertook to deliberate more seriously about the present situation, judging that affairs and the surrounding circumstances called for a commander with absolute authority.

Hannibal, now thoroughly confident about the whole campaign, decided against approaching Rome for the present, and instead advanced through the countryside, plundering it without resistance, making his march toward the Adriatic.

Passing through the country called Umbria and that of Picenum, he reached the Adriatic region on the tenth day,

having taken so much plunder that the army could neither drive nor carry off the spoils, and having killed a great many people along the way;

for, just as in the storming of cities, on this occasion too an order had been given to kill any grown men who fell into their hands. This he did because of the

innate hatred toward the Romans that was rooted in him. During this period, encamping beside the Adriatic in a country surpassingly rich in every kind of produce, he took great pains over the recovery and care of his men, and no less of his horses.

For since their winter quarters in the region of Gaul had been spent in the open, and what with the cold and the lack of oil for grooming, and on top of that the subsequent march and hardship through the marshes, nearly all the horses, and likewise the men, had developed what is called scurvy and a wretched condition of that sort.

So, having gained control of a prosperous country, he put the horses back into good condition, and restored the bodies and the spirits of his soldiers. He also re-equipped the Libyans in the Roman fashion with select weapons, since he had become master of so much captured gear.

At this time he also sent men by sea to report to Carthage about what had happened; for this was the first time he had reached the sea since he made his invasion into Italy.

The Romans appointed as dictator Quintus Fabius, a man outstanding in judgment and of fine natural endowment. Indeed, even down to our own day the members of this household are called the Maximi — that is, "the Greatest" — on account of that man's successes and achievements.

The dictator differs from the consuls in this respect: each of the consuls is attended by twelve axes,

## Book 3 Chapter 88: Hannibal Marches Through Iapygia

Hannibal, shifting his camp from place to place over short distances, lingered in the region along the Adriatic. Because he had a great supply of old wine, he washed down his horses with it and so cured them of their poor condition and their mange.

In much the same way he restored his men, healing the wounded and bringing the rest into good physical shape and a keen readiness for the tasks ahead.

Then, after passing through and laying waste the territories of Praetutia and Hadria, and further those of the Marrucini and the Frentani, he set out and made his march into Iapygia.

This region is divided under three names—its peoples being called Daunii, Peucetii, and Messapii—and the first district he invaded was Daunia.

Beginning there from Luceria, which is a Roman colony, he plundered the countryside.

After this he encamped near the place called Vibo, overran the territory of Arpi, and ravaged all of Daunia without opposition.

At this same time Fabius, after his appointment, sacrificed to the gods and then set out together with his master of the horse and the four legions that had been enrolled for the occasion.

Joining the forces that had come to his aid from Ariminum near Narnia, he relieved Gnaeus, the existing commander, of the land command and sent him off under escort to Rome, with instructions that, should the Carthaginians make any movement by sea, he should always come to the rescue as circumstances required.

Fabius himself, with his master of the horse, took over the forces and encamped facing the Carthaginians near the place called Aecae, keeping about fifty stades from the enemy.

## Book 3 Chapter 89: Fabius Defers Battle

Hannibal, realizing that Fabius had arrived and wishing to strike fear into his opponents by an immediate assault, led out his army, drew close to the Roman entrenchment, and formed up for battle. But after waiting for some time, with no one coming out against him, he again withdrew to his own camp.

For Fabius had resolved neither to expose himself to risk nor to stake everything on a battle, but to make the safety of those under his command his first and foremost aim; and he held firmly to this decision.

At the outset he was despised for it and gave occasion for talk that he had turned coward and was terrified of the danger. But in time he compelled everyone to admit and agree that no one could possibly have handled the circumstances then prevailing with greater sense or prudence.

And events soon bore witness in favor of his reasoning—and reasonably so.

For it happened that the enemy's forces had been continuously trained in warfare from their earliest years; that they had as their leader a man brought up among them and schooled from boyhood in operations in the field;

that they had won many battles in Iberia, and had twice in succession beaten the Romans and their allies; and, most important of all, that having given up everything else, they had one single hope of survival—in victory.

With the Roman army the very opposite of all this was the case.

For this reason Fabius was unable to commit to a decisive engagement, since his inferiority was plain to see. Instead, falling back in his calculations upon his own advantages, he kept to these and conducted the war by their means.

And the Romans' advantages were inexhaustible resources

and an abundance of manpower. And so, in the period that followed, he kept maneuvering alongside the enemy, and by his experience he would seize beforehand all the favorable positions.

He did this with a double purpose: at once to keep whittling down the enemy from their fixed numbers, and at the same time gradually to build up and restore the spirits of his own troops, which had been beaten down in the larger contests, through these successes in detail.

But to come down to a decisive trial of strength by mutual agreement he was in no way willing.

None of this, however, pleased Marcus, his master of the horse. Currying favor with the crowd, he disparaged Fabius to everyone, alleging that he was conducting affairs in an ignoble and sluggish way, while he himself was eager to take the risk and fight it out.

The Carthaginians, after laying waste the districts mentioned, crossed the Apennines and descended into the land of Samnium—a prosperous country untouched by war for many years—and found themselves amid such abundance of provisions that they could not exhaust the plunder by use or by destruction. They also overran the territory of Beneventum,

which is a Roman colony, and took the city of Venusia, which was unwalled and full of property of every kind in great quantity.

The Romans kept following continually behind, staying one or two days' march away, yet they were unable to come near or grapple with the enemy.

Therefore Hannibal, seeing that Fabius was plainly avoiding battle but was not on the whole giving up the open country, made a bold move into the plains around Capua, and within them to the place called Falernum,

convinced that one of two things would result: either he would force the enemy to fight, or he would make it clear to all that he was master of the field and that the Romans were yielding the open country to him.

Should that happen, he hoped the cities, struck with dismay, would be moved to revolt from Rome.

For up to that time, though the Romans had now been defeated in two battles, not a single city in Italy had gone over to the Carthaginians; they all kept their loyalty, even though some were suffering badly.

From this one may gauge the awe and esteem the Roman state inspired among its allies.

## Book 3 Chapter 91: Fertility and Beauty of the Plains Near Capua

And in fact Hannibal had good reason for arriving at these calculations.

For the plains around Capua are the most renowned in all Italy, both for their richness and their beauty and because they lie right on the sea and have at their disposal those trading ports into which sailors bound for Italy run from nearly every part of the inhabited world.

They also contain within them the most famous and most beautiful cities of Italy.

Their coastline is occupied by the people of Sinuessa, of Cumae, and of Puteoli, and besides these the Neapolitans, and lastly the nation of the Nucerians.

Of the interior, the parts toward the north are inhabited by the people of Cales and of Teanum, and those toward the east and south by the Daunii*

and the Nolans. In the very center of the plains lies Capua, the city that was once the most blessed of all.

The story told even by the mythographers about these plains is most plausible: these too are called the Phlegraean plains, just like others among the famous plains. And it is most likely that the gods quarreled over these in particular because of their beauty and their fertility.

Along with the advantages already mentioned, the plains are thought to be strong and altogether difficult to enter; for they are enclosed on one side by the sea and for the greater part by great mountains, continuous on every side, through which there are only three passes from the interior, narrow and hard to traverse:

one from Samnium, a second from Latium, and the last from the region around the Hirpini.

For this reason the Carthaginians, by encamping in these plains as in a theater, were bound to strike everyone with astonishment at the unexpected sight, to expose their enemies as cowards shrinking from battle, and to show themselves beyond dispute the masters of the open country.

## Book 3 Chapter 92: Hannibal Selects Winter Quarters

Reasoning along these lines, then, Hannibal marched out of Samnite territory through the pass by the hill called Eribianus and encamped beside the river Vulturnus, which divides the plains just described into roughly two halves.

He kept his camp on the side facing Rome, and with his foraging parties he ranged over the whole plain and plundered it without hindrance.

Fabius was thoroughly alarmed at the enemy's daring stroke, but for that very reason he held all the more firmly to the course he had decided upon.

His colleague Marcus, however, along with all the military tribunes and company commanders in the army, believed they had caught the enemy in an excellent position, and thought they ought to press on, close with them on the plain, and not stand by while the most celebrated part of the country was being ravaged.

Fabius pressed forward only as far as reaching the area, going along with those who were eager and ready to take risks,

but once he had drawn near to Falernum, he showed himself along the lower slopes and kept pace with the enemy, so as not to seem to his own allies to be surrendering the open country.

He would not, however, bring his army down into the plain, taking care to avoid an all-out engagement, both for the reasons already mentioned and because the enemy was plainly far superior in cavalry.

As for Hannibal, when he had tested the enemy and ravaged the entire plain, gathering an immense quantity of plunder,

## Book 3 Chapter 95: Hasdrubal Equips a Fleet

Such, then, was the situation in Italy.

From this time on, the Carthaginians went about their foraging more warily and cautiously, while the Romans, on the contrary, did so more boldly and rashly.

## Book 3 Chapter 103: Minucius Made Co-Dictator

The people in Rome, when news of what had happened reached them magnified beyond the truth, were overjoyed: first, because out of their previous despair about the whole situation some sort of change for the better seemed to be dawning for them,

and second, because they could now believe that the inaction and dismay of the armies up to this point had come about not through any cowardice in the forces, but through the caution of their commander.

And so everyone blamed and found fault with Fabius for handling his opportunities without daring, while they exalted Marcus so highly for what had occurred that something now happened which had never happened before:

they made him too a commander with absolute authority, convinced that he would soon bring the campaign to an end. Thus there came to be two dictators for the same operations—something that had never before occurred among the Romans.

When Marcus was informed of the people's goodwill and of the office granted him by the popular assembly, he was doubly spurred on to take risks and act boldly against the enemy.

Fabius too returned to the army, in no way altered by what had happened, but holding even more firmly to his original judgment.

Seeing that Marcus was puffed up, contending against him at every turn, and on the whole strongly bent on risking a battle, he offered him the following choice: either to command by turns, or to divide the forces and let each use his own legions according to his own preference.

Marcus accepted the division all too gladly, and so they split the troops and encamped apart from one another, about twelve stades apart.

## Book 3 Chapter 104: Minucius In Difficulties

Hannibal, partly from what he heard from the prisoners taken and partly from what he observed of what was being done, came to understand the rivalry between the two commanders, as well as the impetuosity and ambition of Marcus.

For this reason, judging that what was happening among his opponents told not against him but in his favor, he turned his attention to Marcus, eager to strip away his boldness and to forestall his rash impulse.

There was a certain rise of ground between his own camp and that of Marcus, capable of harming either side, and he set out to seize it. Knowing clearly, from the success that had gone before, that Marcus would come to oppose this move at once, hand to hand, he devised the following scheme.

Since the ground around the hill was bare of trees but full of many folds and hollows of every kind, he sent out during the night, into the most suitable hiding places, parties of two and three hundred at a time — five hundred horsemen in all, and light-armed and other foot soldiers numbering in all about five thousand.

And so that they should not be spotted early by the men going out to forage, at the first light of dawn he seized the hill with his light troops.

When Marcus saw what was happening, he thought it a piece of luck and immediately sent out his light-armed troops, ordering them to fight and contend for the position; after them he sent the cavalry.

Next, behind these, he himself led up the heavy infantry in a continuous line, just as before, handling every part of the operation

## Book 3 Chapter 105: Cautious Fabius Saves the Day

in much the same way. Just as day was breaking, and everyone's thoughts and eyes were wholly absorbed by the men fighting on the hill, the planting of the ambush went unsuspected.

As Hannibal kept sending up fresh reinforcements to his men on the hill, and followed close on their heels himself with his cavalry and his main force, it soon came about that the cavalry of the two sides also clashed.

When this happened, and the Roman light troops were being hard pressed by the mass of the cavalry, they fell back upon the heavy infantry and threw it into confusion;

and at the same moment the signal was given to the men in the ambush, who appeared on every side and charged in. So now not only the light troops but the whole army faced grave danger for the Romans.

At this critical moment Fabius, seeing what was happening and gravely afraid that they would suffer a complete disaster, led out his forces and hurried to the aid of the men in danger.

When he quickly drew near, the Romans took heart again; and although they had by now lost all order, they gathered once more around their standards and withdrew, taking refuge under the protection of his army — having lost many of their light troops and still more of the men from the legions, including their best soldiers.

Hannibal's men, struck by the unbroken strength and good order of the relieving army, broke off the pursuit and the fighting.

To those who had been in the thick of the danger it was plain that everything had been all but lost through the rashness of Marcus, and had been saved — both now and before — through the caution of Fabius.

And to those at Rome it now became openly clear what a difference there is between a soldier's recklessness and vainglory on the one hand, and a general's foresight and steady, sensible calculation on the other.

In any case, the Romans, taught by events, threw up a single palisade and all encamped together in one place, and from then on gave their attention to Fabius and to the orders he issued.

The Carthaginians, for their part, dug a trench across the ground between the hill and their own camp, and threw a palisade around the crest of the captured hill and set a guard upon it; and from then on they made their preparations for wintering in safety.

## Book 3 Chapter 106: New Consuls Elected

As the season for elections drew near, the Romans chose as their commanders Lucius Aemilius and Gaius Terentius. Once these had been appointed, the dictators laid down their office.

The consuls of the previous year, Gnaeus Servilius and Marcus Regulus — who had been appointed in succession after the death of Flaminius — were then commissioned as proconsuls by Aemilius and his colleagues; and, taking over command in the field, they managed the affairs of the army according to their own judgment.

Aemilius and his colleagues, after consulting with the Senate, at once enrolled the number of soldiers still lacking for the whole undertaking and dispatched them.

They instructed Gnaeus and his men by no means to risk a general engagement of any kind, but to carry out partial skirmishes as vigorously and as continuously as possible, in order to train the young men and prepare them, full of confidence, for the decisive battles —

since they reckoned that their earlier reverses had come about, in no small part, from their having relied on troops that were newly levied and wholly untrained.

They themselves sent Lucius Postumius, a praetor with six lictors, to Gaul with an army, wishing to create a diversion against the Celts who were serving with Hannibal.

They also took thought for bringing back the fleet wintering at Lilybaeum, and sent across to the commanders in Iberia everything that was urgently needed for the campaign.

So these men busied themselves carefully with these and the rest of their preparations.

Gnaeus and his officers, having received their instructions from the consuls, handled every detail according to their judgment.

For this reason I will refrain from writing more about them. Nothing decisive or worth recording was done at all, because of these instructions and because of the circumstances of the season;

there were only more frequent skirmishes and partial engagements, in which the Roman commanders won a good name — for they were thought to handle each matter both bravely and sensibly.

## Book 3 Chapter 107: Hannibal Occupies Cannae

So through the winter and the spring season the two armies remained encamped facing each other. But when the time was already at hand for drawing supplies from the year's crops, Hannibal moved his army out of the camp near Geronium.

Judging it to his advantage to force the enemy by every possible means to give battle, he seized the citadel of the town called Cannae.

For it was here that the Romans had been gathering their grain and other supplies from the region around Canusium, and from here these were continually brought up to the camp as need required.

The town itself had in fact already been razed earlier; but now that the stores and the citadel had been captured, no small disturbance arose among the Roman forces —

for they were in difficulty not only on account of the supplies, because the aforesaid place had been taken, but also because of its commanding position over the surrounding country.

So they kept sending to Rome and asking what they should do, since, if they drew near the enemy, they would not be able to avoid battle, with the country being laid waste and all their allies hanging in suspense.

The Senate resolved that they should fight and engage the enemy. They told Gnaeus and his men to hold back still, while they themselves sent out the consuls.

It happened that everyone looked to Aemilius and rested most of their hopes on him, both because of the nobility of his life in general and because, a short time before, he was thought to have conducted the war against the Illyrians both bravely and with advantage to the state.

They resolved to risk the venture with eight legions, something that had never before happened among the Romans, each legion containing about five thousand men apart from the allies.

For the Romans, as we have said somewhere before, always levy four legions. Each legion takes about four thousand foot and two hundred horse.

But whenever some more pressing need arises, they make the foot in each legion about five thousand and the horse three hundred.

Of the allies they make the number of foot equal to that of the Roman legions, but the horse, as a rule, three times as many.

Half of these allies, together with two legions, they assign to each of the consuls and send them out on campaign.

Most of their battles they decide with a single consul and two legions and the aforesaid number of allies, and only rarely do they employ them all at one moment and for a single venture.

On this occasion, however, they were so dismayed and fearful of what was to come that they chose to risk the venture not with four but with eight Roman legions together.

## Book 3 Chapter 108: Aemilius Paulus Harangues the Army

For this reason they summoned Aemilius and his colleague, set vividly before their eyes the magnitude of what would follow from the battle in either direction, and sent them off with instructions to bring the whole matter to a decision at the right moment, with courage and in a manner worthy of their homeland.

When the two men reached the army and assembled the troops, they made the Senate's decision clear to the rank and file and urged upon them what the present circumstances demanded, Lucius delivering his words out of genuine personal feeling.

Most of what he said was directed toward this single aim: the recent disasters that had befallen them. For it was on this account, more than anything, that the men had become demoralized and stood in need of encouragement.

He therefore tried to establish that, as for the defeats in the earlier battles, one could find not just one or two but several causes that had brought about such an outcome,

but that under present conditions no excuse remained—if only they proved themselves men—for failing to defeat the enemy.

For at that earlier time, he said, the two commanders had never fought side by side with the legions, nor had they made use of well-trained forces; instead the troops had been newly levied and had never set eyes on any danger at all.

And, most important of all, the men had been so ignorant of the enemy's situation that they had drawn up for battle and descended into all-out combat practically without ever having laid eyes on their opponents.

"For the men who were beaten at the Trebia River had arrived from Sicily only the day before, and at first light on the very next day they were drawn up for battle.

And as for those who fought in Etruria, not only had they not seen the enemy beforehand, but they could not even make them out during the battle itself, because of the misfortune that occurred with the weather.

Now, at any rate, everything is the opposite of what has just been described."

## Book 3 Chapter 109: Paulus's Speech Continued

"For in the first place, both of us consuls are here, not only to share the dangers with you ourselves, but we have also persuaded last year's commanders to remain on hand and take part in these same struggles.

You yourselves, moreover, have not only seen the enemy's weaponry, their formations, and their numbers, but you have now spent a second year fighting against them almost daily.

Since, then, every detail is the opposite of what obtained in the previous battles, it is reasonable to expect that the outcome of the present contest will likewise be the opposite.

For it would be strange—indeed, one might say, impossible—that men who in piecemeal skirmishes, engaging on equal terms, have for the most part come out on top should, when drawn up all together and outnumbering the enemy by more than two to one, be defeated.

Therefore, men, since everything has been prepared for you to win, the situation needs only one thing: your will and your eagerness—and on that point I do not think it fitting to exhort you further.

For those who serve someone else for pay, or who as allies are about to risk their lives on behalf of others—men for whom the moment of battle itself is most fearful, while the consequences make little difference to them—for such men a manner of exhortation is necessary.

But for men like you now, whose danger is not for the sake of others but for yourselves, your homeland, your wives, and your children, and for whom what follows afterward makes a difference many times greater than the dangers of the moment—for such men only a reminder is needed, not exhortation.

For who would not wish, above all, to win as he fights, and, if that should prove impossible, to die fighting first rather than live to look upon the outrage and ruin of those I have named?

Therefore, men, apart from anything I say, take it upon yourselves to set before your eyes the difference between losing and winning and all that goes with each, and so present yourselves for the battle as though it is not merely the legions that your country now risks, but its very existence.

For if the present situation should somehow be decided otherwise, she has nothing left to add to her current resources that would let her prevail over the enemy.

She has staked all her eagerness and strength upon you, and she holds all her hopes of survival in your hands. Do not now disappoint her of them,

but render to your homeland the thanks that are its due, and make it clear to all mankind that the earlier defeats came about not because Romans are lesser men than Carthaginians, but because of the inexperience of those who fought at the time and because of the circumstances arising from the conditions then."

Having delivered these and similar words of encouragement, Lucius then dismissed the troops.

## Book 3 Chapter 110: Skirmishes Before Cannae

The next day they broke camp and led the army to where they heard the enemy was encamped. Arriving on the second day, they pitched camp about fifty stades from the enemy.

Now Lucius, observing that the surrounding terrain was flat and bare, said they ought not to engage where the enemy was superior in cavalry, but should instead draw the enemy on and advance into ground of a kind where the battle would be decided more by the infantry forces.

But since Gaius, out of inexperience, held the opposite view, there was disagreement and friction between the commanders—which is the most dangerous thing of all.

When command fell to Gaius for the following day—since the consuls take over authority on alternate days, according to custom—he broke camp and advanced, wanting to draw near the enemy, despite Lucius's many protests and attempts to stop him.

Hannibal took up his light-armed troops and his cavalry, went out to meet them, and, falling upon them unexpectedly while they were still on the march, came to grips with them and threw them into great confusion.

The Romans withstood the first onslaught by putting forward some of their heavy-armed troops; then, sending in their javelin-men and cavalry, they gained the upper hand all through the engagement, because the Carthaginians had no significant reserves on hand, whereas the Romans had some maniples mixed in with their light troops to share the fighting.

For the moment, then, with night coming on, the two sides separated, the attack having turned out for the Carthaginians otherwise than they had hoped.

But on the next day Lucius, judging it best neither to fight nor any longer able to lead the army away safely, encamped with two-thirds of it beside the river called the Aufidus—the only river that cuts right through the Apennines—

(this being a continuous mountain range that separates all the watercourses of Italy, sending some to the Tyrrhenian Sea and others to the Adriatic; the Aufidus runs through it, having its sources in the regions of Italy facing the Tyrrhenian side and discharging into the Adriatic)—

while with the remaining third he set up a fortified camp across the river, eastward from the crossing, about ten stades from his own camp and a little more from the enemy's,

intending that these men should both cover the foraging parties from the camp on the far side and harass those of the Carthaginians.

## Book 3 Chapter 111: Hannibal Encourages his Troops

Hannibal, at this same time, seeing that circumstances were calling for a battle and an engagement with the enemy, but wary that his men might be demoralized by their recent setback, decided that the moment called for some encouragement and assembled the troops.

When they had gathered, he ordered them all to look around at the surrounding terrain and asked them what greater thing they could have prayed to the gods for, under the present circumstances, had they been given the choice, than to settle the whole issue in ground like this while holding so great a superiority in cavalry over the enemy.

When they all signaled their agreement at what he said, because its truth was so plain, he went on: "For this, then, first give thanks to the gods; for it is they who, in helping to secure our victory, have led the enemy into ground like this.

And second, give thanks to me, because I have forced the enemy to fight—for they can no longer escape it—and to fight openly on ground favorable to us.

But to exhort you now at any length to be confident and eager for the danger does not seem to me at all appropriate.

When you had no experience of fighting the Romans, that was necessary, and I did then address many arguments to you, with examples;

but now that you have, by common agreement, beaten the Romans three times in a row in battles of such magnitude, what argument could instill stronger confidence in you than the deeds themselves?

So then, through the earlier battles you have gained control of the countryside and its riches, in keeping with my promises, since I have proved truthful in everything I told you. But the present contest is for the cities and the riches within them.

If you prevail in it, you will at once be masters of all Italy; freed from your present hardships and gaining control of the whole prosperity of Rome, you will become, through this battle, the leaders and lords of everything.

Therefore there is no longer any need for words, but for deeds; for, if the gods are willing, I am convinced that I shall make good my promises to you any moment now."

Having said this and things much like it, while the men eagerly signaled their approval, he praised them, welcomed their enthusiasm, and dismissed the assembly. He at once made camp, setting his palisade along the same side of the river as the larger camp of the enemy.

## Book 3 Chapter 112: The Armies Face To Face

On the following day he ordered everyone to busy themselves with their equipment and with getting into condition. The day after that he drew up his troops along the river and made it clear that he was eager to fight the enemy.

Lucius Aemilius, however, was displeased with the ground, and since he could see that the Carthaginians would soon be forced to move camp in order to procure supplies, he kept quiet, securing his encampments with reserve guards.

Hannibal waited a considerable time, and when no one came out against him, he withdrew the rest of his army back into the entrenched camp, but loosed his Numidians against the men who were fetching water from the smaller camp.

When the Numidians charged right up to the rampart itself and prevented the watering, Gaius was provoked still further by this; the troops too were straining for battle and bore the postponements with impatience.

For waiting is the heaviest of all burdens to any man: but once a thing has been decided, then whatever one must suffer, even out of all the things reckoned terrible, must be endured.

When word reached Rome that the two armies were encamped facing each other and that clashes between the advance forces were taking place day after day, the city was on edge and full of fear,

for the mass of the people dreaded what was coming, because they had so often already been beaten before, and in their minds they foresaw and reckoned in advance what would happen if they were utterly defeated.

At that time all the oracles preserved among them were on everyone's lips; every temple and every house was full of portents and omens, so that vows and sacrifices and supplications and prayers to the gods filled the city.

For in times of crisis the Romans are formidable at propitiating both gods and men, and they consider nothing of the sort unseemly or beneath them in such circumstances.

## Book 3 Chapter 113: The Order of Battle

When Gaius took over the command on the next day in succession, just as the sun was beginning to rise, he set the army in motion from both camps at once.

Those from the larger camp he led across the river and at once drew up in line; joining to them those from the other camp, he arranged the whole force on the same straight line, making them all face south.

The Roman cavalry he stationed on the right wing, right beside the river; the infantry he extended continuously next to them along the same line, setting the maniples closer together than usual and making the depth of each company many times greater than its frontage.

The cavalry of the allies he posted on the left wing; and in front of the whole force he placed the light-armed troops, at a distance.

Together with the allies there were about eighty thousand infantry and a little more than six thousand cavalry.

At the same time Hannibal sent his Balearic slingers and javelin-men across the river and posted them in front of his force; the rest he led out of the camp and, bringing them over the stream at two places, drew them up against the enemy.

Right at the river, on his left, he placed the Iberian and Celtic cavalry facing the Roman cavalry; next to them, half of the Libyan infantry armed in the heavy fashion; next to these, the Iberians and Celts; beside them he placed the remaining portion of the Libyans; and on the right wing he posted the Numidian cavalry.

Once he had extended everything along a single line, he then took the central units of the Iberians and Celts and pushed them forward, arranging the other units next to them in proportionate gradation, so that the line formed a crescent-shaped bulge and grew thin in its own formation,

his intention being that the Libyans should hold the position of a reserve in the battle, while the Iberians and Celts bore the brunt of the first fighting.

## Book 3 Chapter 114: Arms of the Libyans, Iberians, and Celts

The Libyans' equipment was Roman, for Hannibal had armed them all with a selection from the spoils taken in the earlier battle.

As for the Iberians and Celts, their shields were much alike, but their swords were of opposite design.

For the Iberian sword could thrust just as effectively as it could strike downward, whereas the Gallic blade had only one use, the downward cut, and that one only with room to swing.

Since their companies were drawn up alternately—the Celts naked, the Iberians arrayed in their national fashion in short linen tunics bordered with purple—the sight that resulted was at once strange and terrifying.

The whole strength of the Carthaginian cavalry came to about ten thousand, and that of the infantry to not much more than forty thousand, including the Celts. Aemilius held the Roman right,

Gaius the left, and the center was held by Marcus and Gnaeus, who had been the commanders of the previous year.

On the Carthaginian side, Hasdrubal held the left and Hanno the right, while at the center was Hannibal himself, with his brother Mago beside him.

Since the Roman line faced south, as I said above, and the Carthaginian line faced north, the rising sun did no harm to either of them.

## Book 3 Chapter 115: The Battle of Cannae

When the first clash had come about between the forward troops, at the outset the fighting among the light-armed themselves was evenly matched.

But as soon as the Iberian and Celtic cavalry on the left closed with the Romans, these men made the battle a real and barbaric one:

for the combat did not follow the rules, with wheeling and counter-maneuver, but once they had collided they fought grappling man against man, dismounting from their horses.

When the Carthaginian horsemen had won the upper hand and killed most of their opponents in the struggle—for the Romans all fought with passion and courage—and were driving the rest along the river, slaughtering them and laying hands on them without mercy, then the infantry lines took over from the light-armed and engaged one another.

For a short time the ranks of the Iberians and Celts held and fought the Romans gallantly; but after that, crushed by the weight, they gave way and fell back, breaking up the crescent.

The Roman maniples, following them eagerly, easily cut through the enemy's line, since the Celts had been drawn up thinly while the Romans had massed together from the wings toward the center and the point of danger.

For the wings and the center did not come into action at the same time; rather the center engaged first, because the Celts, having been arranged in a crescent formation, projected far ahead of the wings, since the convex of the crescent faced the enemy.

But as the Romans pursued these troops and crowded in toward the center and the yielding part of the enemy, they pressed forward so far that on either side, along their exposed flanks, the Libyans in their heavy armor came up against them:

those on the right wing wheeled to the left and, charging from the spear side, took position alongside the enemy's flank,

while those on the left made their turn to the right and charged in from the shield side—the situation itself showing them what had to be done.

The result, just as Hannibal had foreseen, was that the Romans were cut off in the middle by the Libyans, caught there by their headlong pursuit of the Celts.

These men, then, no longer fighting in phalanx formation but turning individually and by maniples against those who were attacking them on the flanks...

## Book 3 Chapter 116: The Slaughter at Cannae

The Romans now had to carry on the fight over the bodies of the fallen. Lucius Aemilius, though he had been stationed on the right wing from the start and had shared in part of the cavalry struggle, was nevertheless still alive at this point.

Wishing to act in keeping with the words he had spoken in his address to the troops, and seeing that the crux of the whole decision of the battle lay in the infantry legions,

he rode along to the center of the entire battle line. There he both engaged personally and laid hands on the enemy, and at the same time encouraged and urged on his own soldiers.

Hannibal did the same on his side, for he too had taken up his position from the beginning at this part of his forces.

The Numidians on the right wing, charging the enemy cavalry posted on the Roman left, neither inflicted nor suffered much harm because of the peculiar manner of their fighting; but they did render the enemy ineffective by keeping them occupied and charging in upon them from every side.

But when Hasdrubal's men, having killed all but a very few of the cavalry by the river, came across from the left to support the Numidians, the Roman allied cavalry, foreseeing their attack, gave way and withdrew.

At this moment Hasdrubal appears to have done a practical and shrewd thing. Seeing that the Numidians were many in number and most effective and most fearsome against troops once they had been forced to give ground, he handed the fugitives over to the Numidians and led his own men toward the infantry battle, eager to bring help to the Libyans.

Falling upon the Roman legions from the rear and launching his squadrons against them in relays at many points at once, he both heartened the Libyans and humbled and dismayed the spirits of the Romans.

It was at this moment that Lucius Aemilius too, after suffering violent blows in the thick of hand-to-hand fighting, ended his life — a man who, if any ever did, rendered his country every duty throughout the rest of his life and at its final hour.

The Romans, as long as they could fight by wheeling about on the surface of their ranks to face those who had encircled them, held out;

but as those on the outside were continually being killed, and they were gradually pressed into a smaller and smaller compass, in the end they all fell there on the spot — among them Marcus Atilius and Gnaeus Servilius, the consuls of the previous year, men who had proved themselves brave and worthy of Rome in the moment of danger.

During this slaughter and struggle the Numidians, pursuing the fleeing cavalry, killed most of them and threw the rest down from their horses.

A few escaped to Venusia, among them Gaius Terentius, the Roman commander, a man who made his own soul a disgrace and his magistracy a loss to his country.

## Book 3 Chapter 117: Superiority in Cavalry Wins Battles

So the battle of Cannae between the Romans and the Carthaginians was fought out in this way — a battle in which the bravest of men were found among both the victors and the vanquished.

This became clear from the facts themselves. Of the six thousand cavalry, seventy escaped to Venusia with Gaius, and about three hundred of the allied horse made their way safely, scattered, into the towns;

while of the infantry, about ten thousand who were not part of the main battle were taken prisoner fighting, and from the battle itself only about three thousand perhaps escaped to the neighboring towns.

All the rest, numbering about seventy thousand, died nobly — the throng of cavalry having, both on this occasion and before it, furnished the Carthaginians their greatest asset for winning.

And it became clear to later generations that for the occasions of war it is better to have half the number of infantry but be superior in cavalry than to risk a decisive battle with all forces evenly matched against the enemy.

On Hannibal's side, about four thousand Celts fell, about fifteen hundred Iberians and Libyans, and about two hundred cavalry.

The Romans who were taken alive had been outside the main battle, and for the following reason.

Lucius had left ten thousand infantry at his own camp, so that if Hannibal, neglecting the protection of his palisade, should draw up his whole army for battle, these men might fall upon it during the fighting and gain control of the enemy's baggage;

or, if Hannibal should foresee what might happen and leave a sufficient guard, the contest over the main issue would be against fewer of the enemy.

They were taken captive in roughly the following way. When Hannibal had left an adequate guard at his palisade, the Romans, as soon as the battle began, laid siege to it according to their orders, assaulting those left behind in the Carthaginian camp. At first the defenders held out;

but when they were already under pressure, and Hannibal had by then decided the battle at every point, he came to their relief, routed the Romans, penned them up in their own camp, killed two thousand of them, and took all the rest prisoner.

In the same way the Numidians stormed those who had fled together to the strongholds in the countryside and brought them back — about two thousand of the cavalry who had been put to flight.

## Book 3 Chapter 118: The Consequences of the Battle of Cannae

The battle having been decided in the way described, the whole situation took a turn that followed from what both sides had expected.

For the Carthaginians, through this action, were at once masters of almost all the rest of the coast.

The Tarentines, indeed, immediately put themselves into their hands; the Argyrippans and some of the Capuans summoned Hannibal; and all the rest were now already looking to the Carthaginians.

These had high hopes of becoming masters of Rome itself at the first onset.

The Romans, for their part, at once gave up the rule of Italy on account of the defeat, and were in great fear and danger both for themselves and for the soil of their fatherland, expecting that at any moment Hannibal himself would arrive.

And indeed, as though Fortune were measuring out yet more and joining in the contest against them on top of what had already happened, it came about that a few days later, while fear gripped the city, the general who had been sent to Gaul fell unexpectedly into an ambush and was utterly destroyed, together with his army, by the Celts.

Nevertheless the Senate left undone none of the things in its power, but exhorted the people, secured the affairs of the city, and deliberated about the present crisis like men. This became evident from what happened afterward;

for although the Romans were on that occasion admittedly beaten and had yielded their claim to excellence in arms,

by the distinctive character of their constitution and by deliberating well they not only recovered the rule of Italy, afterward defeating the Carthaginians, but within a few years became masters of the whole inhabited world.

Accordingly, we shall close this book at these events — those of Spain and of Italy that the one-hundred-and-fortieth Olympiad embraced, having now set them forth.

When, in recounting the Greek affairs that took place during the same Olympiad, we come up to these same times, then at last we propose to compose, on its own, an account of the Roman constitution itself,

believing that an exposition of it is not only suited to the framework of our history, but also contributes greatly, for men who love learning and are concerned with practical affairs, toward the reform and construction of constitutions.
