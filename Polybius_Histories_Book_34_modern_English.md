# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXXIV

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 34 Chapter 1

Those historians who, in the course of a general history, set out separately the geography of the continents did as Ephorus and Polybius did.

Polybius says that, on Greek matters, Eudoxus gave a good account, but Ephorus the best account of city foundations, kinships, migrations, and founders.

“But we,” he says, “will describe the world as it now is, dealing with the position of places and their distances from one another.”

For that is the subject most properly belonging to geography.

And yet, Polybius, by introducing sweeping judgments about distances not only in lands outside Greece but also within Greece itself, you make yourself answerable in some cases to Posidonius, in others to Artemidorus, and in others to many more besides.

Polybius the historian also composed a book entitled *On the Habitation under the Equator*. This region lies in the middle of the torrid zone.

He says that these places are inhabited and that their climate is more temperate than that of the regions lying at the edges of the torrid zone. Some of his evidence comes from reports by men who had observed those inhabited regions and confirmed what was seen there; other arguments he draws by reasoning from the natural motion of the sun.

For the sun lingers a long time around the tropic circles, both as it approaches them and as it moves away, so that to perception it remains near the tropics for almost forty days.

For this reason, since it lingers over the regions lying beneath the tropics, those regions must be scorched and become uninhabitable because of the intensity of the heat.

But from the equatorial circle its departure is rapid. It is therefore reasonable that the regions lying under the equator should have a milder climate, since the sun does not linger at the point overhead but quickly moves away from it.

For all who live between the tropic circles stand in the same relation to the sun’s passage overhead; but it remains longer over those who live near the tropics.

For that reason, the regions beneath the equator, though they lie in the middle of the torrid zone, are in fact more temperate than the settlements near the outer edges of that zone, which lie under the tropic circles. Polybius makes six zones in all,

two beneath the arctic circles, two between those and the tropics, and two between those and the equator.

But Polybius is not right in this, when he marks out zones by the arctic circles, making two under them and two between them and the tropics.

If, as Eratosthenes says, the region beneath the equator is temperate—as Polybius agrees, adding also that it is the highest region, and therefore receives abundant rainfall because the northern clouds driven by the etesian winds strike the heights there—then it would be much better to make this a narrow third temperate zone than to introduce zones beneath the tropics.

Posidonius objects to Polybius on the ground that he says the equatorial region is the highest.

And after him there was great disagreement about the number of the zones. Some made them six, as Polybius and Posidonius did, by dividing the torrid zone into two.

## Book 34 Chapter 2

To build an empty tale of marvels on no basis of truth at all is not Homer’s way.

For, as one would expect, falsehood is more persuasive when it includes some elements of truth.

That is what Polybius also says when he undertakes to explain the wanderings of Odysseus.

And Polybius is right in the way he interprets those wanderings.

He says that Aeolus, because he indicated the routes for sailing out through the regions of the strait—where the currents run in both directions and departure is difficult because of the tides—came to be called the keeper of the winds and was thought of as a king,

just as Danaus, who pointed out the reservoirs in Argos, and Atreus, who recognized the sun’s course as opposite to that of the heavens, came to be represented as seers and priest-kings.

So too the priests of the Egyptians, the Chaldaeans, and the Magi, because they surpassed other men in wisdom, received authority and honor among earlier generations.

In the same way each of the gods is honored as the discoverer of some useful thing.

Starting from this principle, Polybius does not allow Aeolus to be treated as a merely mythical figure, nor Odysseus’ entire wandering as pure fiction. Rather, he says that a small amount of myth has been added, just as it has been added to the story of the Trojan War,

but that the main setting of the whole story is Sicily, both in the poet and in the other writers who describe the local features of Italy and Sicily.

Nor does he approve Eratosthenes’ remark that one will discover where Odysseus wandered when one finds the cobbler who stitched up the bag of the winds.

He also says that Homer’s words fit what happens at Scyllaeum and the hunting of the great fish there, where Scylla herself is described as

raging around the rock,

fishing for dolphins and sea-dogs and whatever larger creature she may catch.

For tuna, swimming in schools along the coast of Italy, whenever they are driven off course and prevented from reaching Sicily, fall in with larger animals such as dolphins, sharks, and other sea-creatures,

and from feeding on these the hunters called galeotes grow fat—fish which, he says, are also called swordfish or sea-dogs.

The same thing happens during the flooding of the Nile and of other waters, just as it does in a fire when woodland is burning: the animals gather together in flight from fire or flood and become prey for the stronger creatures.

## Book 34 Chapter 3

After saying this, he describes the hunting of the galeotes, which takes place around Scyllaeum.

A single lookout is stationed over many small two-oared boats lying in wait, with two men in each boat.

One man rows, while the other stands on the prow holding a spear whenever the lookout signals the appearance of a swordfish, for the creature travels with a third of its body above the water.

When the boat comes alongside, the spearman strikes at close range and then pulls the shaft back out of the body, leaving only the harpoon-head in the fish.

For the point is barbed and is deliberately fitted loosely to the shaft, and a long line is attached to it. They let this line run with the wounded fish until it is exhausted from its struggling and attempts to escape.

Then they drag it ashore or lift it into the boat, if its body is not too large.

And even if the shaft falls into the sea, it is not lost. It is made from oak and fir together, so that when the oak end sinks by its weight, the rest remains afloat and easy to recover.

Sometimes it even happens that the rower is wounded straight through the little boat because of the great size of the fish’s sword and the boar-like violence of the creature in the hunt.

From such facts, he says, one may infer that Homer placed Odysseus’ wandering around Sicily, because he connected Scylla with this kind of fishing, which is especially characteristic of Scyllaeum,

and because what is said about Charybdis resembles the actual behavior of the strait.

But the line “for three times she spews it up” instead of “twice” is either a copyist’s error or a geographical mistake.

And Meninx too agrees with what is said about the Lotus-Eaters.

## Book 34 Chapter 4

If some point does not agree with reality, one should explain it by changes over time, by ignorance, or by poetic license, which is composed of history, vivid presentation, and myth.

The aim of history is truth, as when in the Catalogue of Ships the poet mentions the features proper to each place, calling one city rocky, another farthest out, another rich in doves, and another beside the sea.

The aim of vivid presentation is immediacy, as when he introduces men fighting; the aim of myth is pleasure and wonder.

But to invent everything is neither convincing nor Homeric. For everyone regards his poetry as a work of thought, not as Eratosthenes says when he tells us not to judge poems by their meaning or to look for history in them.

It is more plausible to take the line, “From there for nine days I was carried by destructive winds,” to refer to a short distance—for destructive winds do not keep a straight course—than to send Odysseus out into the outer ocean as though steady fair winds were blowing all the while.

And if, he says, we reckon the distance from Malea to the Pillars as 22,500 stades, and suppose that this was covered at an even rate in nine days, the voyage would come to 2,500 stades each day.

Who, then, has ever recorded that someone sailed from Lycia or Rhodes to Alexandria—a distance of 4,000 stades—and arrived on the second day?

And to those who ask how Odysseus could have come to Sicily three times and yet never once sailed through the strait, he replies that later sailors also all avoided that passage. Such is his argument.

## Book 34 Chapter 5

III. Polybius argues against earlier geographical writers

When Polybius describes Europe, he says that he will pass over the ancient writers and instead examine those who criticize them: Dicaearchus, Eratosthenes, the most recent author to write on geography, and Pytheas,

by whom, he says, many have been misled, since he claimed to have gone over the whole of Britain on foot and gave the island a perimeter of more than forty thousand stades,

and also told stories about Thule and those regions where there was no longer land by itself, nor sea, nor air, but some compound made from all of them, like a sea-lung,

in which, he says, earth and sea and everything together are suspended, and that this serves as a sort of bond holding the whole together, being neither passable on foot nor navigable by ship.

He says that he saw for himself the thing that resembled a sea-lung, but that he reports the rest from hearsay. Such, then, are the tales of Pytheas,

including his further claim that after returning from there he traveled all along the ocean-facing coast of Europe from Gades to the Tanais.

Polybius says that even this is incredible: how could a private man, and a poor man besides, have crossed such vast distances by sea and by land?

And yet Eratosthenes, though he raised the question whether these stories should be believed, nevertheless accepted what Pytheas said about Britain and the regions around Gades and Iberia.

He says it would be far better to believe the Messenian than this man. Euhemerus, after all, says only that he sailed to one country, Panchaea; but Pytheas claims that he personally inspected the whole northern coast of Europe as far as the ends of the world, something one would not believe even if Hermes himself said it.

Yet Eratosthenes calls Euhemerus a Bergaean, and still believes Pytheas, although Dicaearchus did not believe him either.

As for the phrase, "although Dicaearchus did not believe him," that is ridiculous, as though Dicaearchus ought to be used as the standard, though Polybius himself brings forward so many refutations against him.

The ignorance of Eratosthenes about the western and northern parts of Europe has already been mentioned.

But Eratosthenes and Dicaearchus may be excused, since they had not seen those regions. But who could excuse Polybius and Posidonius?

And yet Polybius is precisely the man who calls such pronouncements mere popular guesswork, the sort people make about distances in these regions and in many others, but not even in...

## Book 34 Chapter 6

With arguments of this kind he refutes those earlier writers while claiming precision. For example, Dicaearchus said that from the Peloponnese to the Pillars there were ten thousand stades, and that the distance from the Peloponnese to the head of the Adriatic was greater than this; and since he gave the distance from the Peloponnese to the strait as three thousand, the remainder from the strait to the Pillars came to seven thousand,

Polybius says that he will let the three thousand stand, whether it is correctly reckoned or not, but that the seven thousand works in neither way, neither for someone measuring along the coast nor for someone measuring straight through the open sea.

For the coastline, he says, most closely resembles an obtuse angle whose two ends rest on the strait and on the Pillars,

with Narbo as its apex, so that a triangle is formed, having as its base the straight course through the open sea, and as its sides the two lines that form the angle just mentioned.

One of these, from the strait to Narbo, is ten thousand two hundred stades and more; the other, the remaining side, is a little less than eight thousand.

And indeed, it is generally agreed that the greatest distance from Europe to Libya across the Tyrrhenian Sea is no more than three thousand stades; across the Sardinian Sea one may make a corresponding estimate.

But let that too, he says, be taken as three thousand, and in addition let two thousand stades be assumed for the depth of the gulf near Narbo, as though it were a perpendicular from the apex to the base of the obtuse-angled triangle.

It is therefore clear, he says, from schoolboy geometry that the entire coastal route from the strait to the Pillars exceeds the straight course across the open sea by roughly five hundred stades.

And when the three thousand stades from the Peloponnese to the strait are added, the total number of stades, even by the straight route itself, will be more than double what Dicaearchus said.

And, he says, the distance to the recess at the head of the Adriatic will have to be set still greater than this, according to Dicaearchus' own account.

But, dear Polybius, one might say, just as experience makes the refutation of that falsehood plain enough from the very things you yourself have said,

namely, seven hundred stades from the Peloponnese to Leucas, then the same distance from there to Corcyra, and again the same distance from there to the Ceraunian Mountains, and then to the right from the Ceraunians to Iapydia along the Illyrian coast, six thousand one hundred and fifty stades,

so too these two claims are both false: Dicaearchus' statement that the distance from the strait to the Pillars is seven thousand stades, and the result that you think you have proved.

For most authorities agree in saying that the distance across the open sea is twelve thousand stades.

How could one not reasonably think that Antiphanes of Berge had been surpassed in extravagance and sheer madness, and that he had left no excess of folly at all for later generations?

## Book 34 Chapter 7

Next he corrects Eratosthenes, speaking rightly in some matters, but in others worse than Eratosthenes himself.

For when Eratosthenes said that the distance from Ithaca to Corcyra was three hundred stades, Polybius says that it is more than nine hundred; and when Eratosthenes gave nine hundred stades from Epidamnus to Thessalonice, Polybius says it is more than two thousand. In this, he is right.

But when Eratosthenes says there are seven thousand stades from Massalia to the Pillars and six thousand from the Pyrenees, Polybius himself says, worse than Eratosthenes, that from Massalia there are more than nine thousand and from the Pyrenees a little less than eight thousand; for Eratosthenes is closer to the truth.

For the authorities now agree that, if one cuts short the irregular windings of the roads, the whole length of Iberia from the Pyrenees to its western side is no more than six thousand stades.

Yet Polybius places the length of the Tagus River itself at eight thousand stades from its source to its mouth, surely not including all its bends—for that is not the geographer's method—but speaking in a straight line; and yet the sources of the Tagus are more than a thousand stades away from the Pyrenees.

Again, he is right in declaring that Eratosthenes is ignorant of Iberian geography and that in some places he makes contradictory statements about it,

since, after saying that its outer parts as far as Gades are inhabited by Gauls—if indeed they occupy the western regions of Europe as far as Gades—he then forgets this and, in his survey of Iberia, nowhere mentions the Gauls at all.

But when he lays down that the length of Europe is less than that of Libya and Asia together, he is not making the comparison correctly.

For he says that the mouth at the Pillars lies at due west, while the Tanais flows from the point of summer sunrise.

So Europe is made shorter than the combined length by the interval between the summer sunrise and the equinoctial sunrise; for Asia gets that much start, toward due east, within the northern semicircle.

Europe, however, projects in several headlands,

and on this point Polybius has spoken better than Eratosthenes, though still not adequately.

For Eratosthenes said there were three peninsulas: one reaching to the Pillars, on which Iberia lies; one reaching to the strait, on which Italy lies; and a third at Malea, on which lie all the peoples between the Adriatic and the Euxine and the Tanais.

Polybius likewise sets out the first two, but makes a third at Malea and Sunium, on which lie all Greece, Illyria, and part of Thrace,

a fourth at the Thracian Chersonese, where the narrows at Sestus and Abydus are—this peninsula is occupied by Thracians—and a fifth at the Cimmerian Bosporus and the mouth of Lake Maeotis.

## Book 34 Chapter 8

IV. On Lusitania

Polybius of Megalopolis, in the thirty-fourth book of his Histories, while discussing the land of Lusitania in Iberia, says that acorn-bearing trees are rooted deep in the sea there, and that tunny grow fat by feeding on their fruit.

For that reason, one would not be wrong to call the tunny sea-pigs.

Polybius also says that these acorns drift as far as Latium, unless perhaps Sardinia and the neighboring island produce them as well.

When describing the prosperity of Lusitania—this is a region of Iberia, which the Romans now call Spain—Polybius of Megalopolis says in the thirty-fourth book of his Histories that, because of the mildness of the climate there, both animals and human beings are highly fertile, and the produce of the land never fails.

For roses, white violets, asparagus, and similar plants are absent there for no more than three months,

while the seafood there is greatly superior to what is found in our sea in abundance, quality, and beauty.

There a Sicilian medimnus of barley costs one drachma, and a medimnus of wheat nine Alexandrian obols.

A metretes of wine costs a drachma, and an average kid or a hare costs an obol. Lambs are priced at three or four obols. A fat pig weighing one hundred minas costs five drachmas, and a sheep two. A talent of figs costs three obols,

a calf five drachmas, and a yoke-ox ten.

The meat of wild animals was considered hardly worth setting a price on at all; instead, people exchanged it freely as a favor and a courtesy.

## Book 34 Chapter 9

V. Spain

The inhabitants are called the Turdetani and the Turduli; some think these are the same people, others that they are different.

Polybius is among those who say that the Turduli live to the north of the Turdetani as their neighbors.

The fertility of the land also gave the Turdetani a gentle and civilized character, and likewise the Celtic peoples because of their proximity—or, as Polybius says, because of their kinship.

Dicaearchus, Eratosthenes, Polybius, and most Greek writers place the Pillars at the strait.

Polybius says that there is a spring in the Heracleum at Gades, with a short flight of steps leading down to drinkable water, and that it behaves opposite to the sea tides: at high tide it runs low, and at ebb tide it fills.

His explanation is that the breath rising from the depths to the earth’s surface, when the ground is covered by the sea during the incoming tide, is blocked from its natural outlets; turned back inward, it chokes the channels of the spring and causes a lack of water.

But when the ground is laid bare again, it finds an open passage and frees the veins of the spring, so that the water gushes up readily.

Polybius, mentioning the silver mines around New Carthage, says that they are extremely large, about twenty stades from the city, and enclosed within a circuit of four hundred stades.

There forty thousand workers live, and at that time they brought in for the Roman people twenty-five thousand drachmas each day.

As for the rest of the working process, I pass it over—it would take too long—but he says that the silver-bearing ore is crushed and washed through sieves in water; then the sediment is crushed again, and after being filtered again and the water poured off, it is crushed once more.

When the fifth sediment has been smelted, and the lead has been run off, the pure silver is extracted.

Polybius also says that the Ana and the Baetis both flow from Celtiberia, about nine hundred stades apart from one another.

Polybius, in going through the peoples and territories of the Vaccaei and the Celtiberians, includes among their other cities Segesama and Intercatia.

In its design and splendor it resembles the palace of a certain Iberian king mentioned by Polybius.

He says that this king rivaled the luxury of the Phaeacians, except that the mixing bowls standing in the middle of the house, though made of silver and gold, were full of barley wine.

## Book 34 Chapter 10

VI. Gaul

Polybius, in the thirty-fourth book of his Histories, says that from the Pyrenees to the river Narbo there is a plain, through which flow the rivers Illeberis and Rhoscynus, passing by cities of the same names inhabited by Celts.

In this plain are found what are called underground fish.

The plain has a light soil and produces a great quantity of agrostis grass. Beneath this, because the ground is sandy to a depth of two or three cubits, the water that spreads out from the rivers filters below.

With this water the fish also make their way under the ground during the overflow in search of food—for they are fond of the root of the agrostis—and so they have made the whole plain full of underground fish, which people dig up and catch.

As for the mouths of the Rhone, Polybius criticizes Timaeus, saying that the river does not have five mouths but two.

The Liger empties between the Pictones and the Namnitae. Formerly there was an emporium on this river called Corbilo, of which Polybius speaks when mentioning the fictions invented by Pytheas.

He says that none of the people from Massalia who met Scipio could tell him anything worth remembering when he asked about Britain, nor could any from Narbo or Corbilo, though these were the finest cities in that region; yet Pytheas had the boldness to tell so many lies.

Polybius also says that in these regions there is bred an unusual animal, shaped like a deer except for its neck and coat, and in those respects resembling a wild boar.

Under its chin it has a tuft about a span long, hairy at the tip and as thick as a colt’s tail.

Polybius further says that in his own time, near Aquileia among the Norican Taurisci, a gold mine was discovered so rich by nature that, after scraping away only two feet of topsoil, one immediately found gold-bearing ore.

The vein was no more than fifteen feet deep.

Some of the gold was already pure, in pieces the size of a bean or lupin, and when smelted lost only one eighth; the rest required more working in the furnace, but was still highly profitable.

When Italians joined the barbarians in working it, within two months the price of gold throughout Italy immediately fell by a third.

When the Taurisci realized this, they drove out their partners and kept the monopoly themselves.

The same writer, speaking of the size and height of the Alps, compares them with the greatest mountains in Greece—Taygetus, Lycaeus, Parnassus, Olympus, Pelion, and Ossa—and in Thrace with Haemus, Rhodope, and Dunax.

He says that each of these can more or less be climbed by active men in a single day, and likewise gone around in a single day.

But no one could climb the Alps even in five days; and their length along the plains is two thousand two hundred stades.

He names only four passes: one through Liguria, nearest the Tyrrhenian Sea; next the one through the Taurini, which Hannibal used; then that through the Salassi; and fourth that through the Raeti, all of them precipitous.

He says that there are several lakes in the mountains, but three are large. The first, Benacus, is five hundred stades long and one hundred and thirty wide, and from it flows the river Mincius.

The next, Larius, is four hundred stades long and narrower than the first, and it sends out the river Addua.

The third, Verbanus, is nearly three hundred stades long and thirty wide, and it sends out the great river Ticinus. All these rivers flow into the Padus.

## Book 34 Chapter 11

VII. Italy

Polybius says that at Capua a remarkable wine is produced, called Anadendrites, with which no other can be compared.

He says that the coast from Iapygia to the strait is three thousand stades by land, washed by the Sicilian Sea, but by sea voyage it is less than five hundred.

They say that the greatest length of the Etrurian coast, from Luna to Ostia, is about two thousand five hundred stades, while its width inland toward the mountains is less than half that. From Luna to Pisa is more than four hundred stades; from there to Volaterrae two hundred and eighty; then from there to Populonium two hundred and seventy; and from Populonium to Cosa nearly eight hundred, though some say six hundred. Polybius is not correct in giving the total as one thousand three hundred and thirty.

Aethaleia, an island of the Tyrrhenians.

Polybius in the thirty-fourth book says that Aethaleia is called Lemnos.

The gulf between the two promontories, Misenum and Athenaeum, is called the Crater.

Above these shores lies all Campania, the most fertile plain of all.

Antiochus says that this land was inhabited by the Opici, who were also called Ausones.

Polybius, however, indicates that he regards these as two peoples; for he says that both the Opici and the Ausones inhabit this land around the Crater.

Polybius says that the distances from Iapygia have been measured in miles,

and that there are five hundred and sixty-two miles to the city of Sila, and from there to Aquileia one hundred and seventy-eight.

Then comes Lacinium,

a sanctuary of Hera, once wealthy and full of many offerings.

The lengths are not stated clearly; but for the most part Polybius gives the distance from the strait to Lacinium as one thousand three hundred stades,

and from there across to the Iapygian promontory as seven hundred.

Polybius says that of the three craters one has partly collapsed, while the other two remain intact.

The largest has a circular rim with a circumference of five stades, but it gradually narrows to a diameter of fifty feet.

From there the depth to the sea is one stade, so that in calm weather the sea can be seen. If this is trustworthy, then perhaps the stories told about Empedocles should not be dismissed either.

He says that when a south wind is about to blow, a misty haze spreads all around the little island, so that even Sicily cannot be seen from it at a distance; but when a north wind is coming,

bright flames leap up from the crater mentioned above to a great height, and louder rumblings are sent out; the west wind produces something between the two.

The other craters are of similar shape, but weaker in the force of their eruptions.

From the differences in the rumblings, and from the point where the blasts, flames, and smoke begin, it is possible to foretell which wind will blow again on the third day.

He says that when ships were weather-bound in the Lipari Islands, some men there would predict what wind was coming and were not proved wrong.

So it appears that what seems most mythical in the poet was not spoken in vain, but that he expressed the truth in a riddle when he called Aeolus the keeper of the winds.

## Book 34 Chapter 12

VIII. Thrace, Macedonia, Greece

He also says other things that are not trustworthy: that the seas are connected by channels because Chian and Thasian pottery is found in the Naro; that both seas can be seen from a certain mountain; that among the Liburnian islands is Titheis, with a circuit of five hundred stades; and that the Ister pours one of its mouths into the Adriatic.

Some statements of Eratosthenes too are misunderstandings shaped by popular talk, as Polybius says when discussing him and other historians.

From Apollonia to Macedonia the Via Egnatia runs eastward, measured out by the mile and marked with milestones as far as Cypsela and the river Hebrus; the total is five hundred and thirty-five miles.

If one reckons, as most people do, eight stades to the mile, the total comes to four thousand two hundred and eighty stades.

But according to Polybius, by adding to the eight-stade mile two plethra, which is one third of a stade, one must add another one hundred and seventy-eight stades, that is, one third of the number of miles.

It turns out that those who set out from Apollonia and those who set out from Epidamnus come together onto the same road after an equal distance.

The whole road is called the Egnatian, but the first stretch is called the Candavian road, from Mount Candavia in Illyria, and it runs through the city of Lychnidus and through Pylon, the place on the route that marks the boundary between Illyria and Macedonia.

From there it goes past Barnous, through Heracleia and the regions of the Lyncestae and Eordi, to Edessa and Pella, and on to Thessalonica.

Polybius says this section is two hundred and sixty-seven miles.

Since the distance from Perinthus to Byzantium is six hundred and thirty stades, and from the Hebrus and Cypsela to Byzantium as far as the Cyanean Rocks is three thousand one hundred, as Artemidorus says, the total length from the Ionian Gulf at Apollonia to Byzantium is seven thousand three hundred and twenty stades; Polybius adds another one hundred and eighty,

counting the extra third of a stade on top of the eight stades in each mile.

The circumference of the Peloponnese, if one does not follow every inlet, is four thousand stades, according to Polybius.

When Polybius said that the distance from Malea northward to the Ister was about ten thousand stades, Artemidorus corrects this, and not unreasonably. According to him, the distance from the Ister to Malea is six thousand five hundred. The reason is that Polybius did not measure the direct route, but some incidental one that one of the generals had taken.

## Book 34 Chapter 13

IX. Asia

As for the regions lying directly beyond these as far as India, Artemidorus gives the same account as Eratosthenes. Polybius too says that, on matters concerning those lands, Eratosthenes is the authority one should trust most.

## Book 34 Chapter 14

X. Alexandria, City of Egypt

Polybius, who had himself been in the city, says that he was disgusted by its condition at that time.

He says that three groups lived in the city: first, the Egyptian and native population, sharp-witted and fit for civic life;

a second, the mercenary class, a heavy, numerous, and unruly body. For from ancient custom they maintained foreign men under arms, men trained to rule rather than obey because of the weakness of the kings.

A third group was that of the Alexandrians themselves, not clearly suited for civic life either, for the same reasons, yet still better than the others.

For even if they were of mixed descent, they had nevertheless originally been Greeks and still remembered the common customs of the Greek people. But when even this element had been wiped out,

chiefly under Euergetes Physcon, in whose reign Polybius came to Alexandria—

for Physcon, whenever he was caught up in civil strife, repeatedly let the soldiers loose upon the crowds and destroyed them—

with things in the city being in such a state, he says, the poet's words were literally true:

“To go to Egypt is a long and painful road.”
