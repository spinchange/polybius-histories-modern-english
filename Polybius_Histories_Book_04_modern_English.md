# Polybius — The Histories, Book IV

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 4 Chapter 1: Review of Achaean History

In the book before this one I set out the causes of the second war that broke out between the Romans and the Carthaginians, and I gave an account of Hannibal's invasion of Italy.

In addition to this, I described the battles the two sides fought against one another, down to the engagement that took place by the river Aufidus and the city of Cannae.

Now I shall go through the affairs of Greece that were carried out during the same period as the events already related, beginning from the 140th Olympiad.

But first I shall briefly remind those who take up this work of the preliminary account I gave in the second book concerning Greek affairs, and especially concerning the Achaean nation—since this political body too achieved a remarkable growth, both in the times before our own and in our own day.

I began with Tisamenus, one of the sons of Orestes, and stated that from his time the Achaeans were ruled by kings in unbroken succession down to Ogygus; that afterwards they adopted, by an excellent choice, a democratic constitution; and that at first they were broken apart, city by city and village by village, by the kings from Macedonia.

Following this, I set out to tell how they began once more to unite in common purpose, and when and which cities were the first to band together.

Next in order I explained by what method and with what policy they went about winning over the cities and undertook to bring all the Peloponnesians under the same name and constitution.

After giving a general account of the plan just described, I then touched continuously on the particular actions, until I came to the downfall of Cleomenes, king of the Lacedaemonians.

Having summed up the events of this preliminary survey down to the deaths of Antigonus, Seleucus, and Ptolemy—since all of these passed away at about the same time—I announced, as what remained, that I would make the beginning of my own work the events that follow upon those already described.

## Book 4 Chapter 2: Why Begin With This Period of History

I judged this to be the best starting point, first because it is here that the history of Aratus ends; and by joining my narrative to his I have chosen to give the account of Greek affairs that follows directly upon it.

Second, because the periods that follow, and those that fall within my own history, run together in such a way that some belong to our own time and some to the time of our fathers; from which it comes about that I was personally present at some of the events, while others I have heard from those who saw them.

To reach further back in time, and to write down a report of a report, did not seem to me a safe basis either for forming judgments or for making assertions.

But above all I began from these times because it was then that fortune, so to speak, made everything in the inhabited world new.

For Philip, the natural son of Demetrius, while still a boy, had just taken over the rule of the Macedonians;

Achaeus, who held power over the region on this side of the Taurus, possessed not merely the outward standing of a king but real power as well;

and Antiochus, surnamed the Great, a little earlier, on the death of his brother Seleucus, had succeeded while still quite young to the kingdom of Syria.

At the same time as these, Ariarathes took over the rule of Cappadocia, and Ptolemy Philopator had in the same period become master of affairs in Egypt.

Not long afterward Lycurgus was established as king of the Lacedaemonians, and the Carthaginians had recently chosen Hannibal as their general for the operations already mentioned.

With such a renewal taking place in all the dynasties, there was bound to be the beginning of new affairs; for this is the natural course of things and tends to happen so—and that is just what came about at this time.

For the Romans and the Carthaginians entered upon the war already described; Antiochus and Ptolemy, at the same time as these, the war over Coele-Syria; and the Achaeans and Philip the war against the Aetolians and the Lacedaemonians. The causes of this last came about as follows.

## Book 4 Chapter 3: Dorimachus In the Peloponnese

The Aetolians had long chafed at peace and at having to meet expenses from their own resources, since they were accustomed to live off their neighbors and needed lavish means to support their ingrained arrogance. Enslaved to it, they always live a grasping, beast-like life, regarding nothing as belonging to others and everything as theirs to plunder.

Nevertheless, in the time before this, so long as Antigonus was alive, fear of the Macedonians kept them quiet.

But when he departed this life, leaving behind the boy Philip, they grew contemptuous and began looking for openings and pretexts for meddling in the Peloponnese—drawn by their old habit of plundering that country, and at the same time reckoning that they were a match for the Achaeans themselves in war.

While they were set on this purpose, chance gave them a little assistance, and they got the following openings for their design.

Dorimachus of Trichonium was the son of that Nicostratus who broke the truce at the festival of the Pamboeotia. Being young and full of Aetolian impetuosity and greed, he was sent out by public authority to the city of the Phigaleans,

which lies in the Peloponnese, on the borders of the Messenians, and which at that time happened to be in political union with the Aetolians—

ostensibly to guard the territory and city of the Phigaleans, but in fact holding the post of a spy upon affairs in the Peloponnese.

When a band of pirates gathered and came to him at Phigalea, he had no way of providing them with plunder by lawful means, since the general peace among the Greeks that had been concluded under Antigonus was still in force.

At last, at a loss what to do, he allowed the pirates to carry off the livestock of the Messenians, although they were friends and allies.

At first they did their wrong to the flocks on the outlying lands, but afterward, as their recklessness grew, they set about breaking into the farmhouses as well, appearing without warning by night.

The Messenians were indignant at this and sent embassies to Dorimachus; but at first he turned a deaf ear, wishing partly to benefit the men under his command and partly to profit himself by taking a share of the plunder.

When the embassies kept coming in greater numbers because of the continual wrongdoing, he said that he would come in person to Messene to argue the case against those who were bringing charges against the Aetolians.

But when he arrived, and the injured parties approached him, he ridiculed some with mockery, browbeat others, and frightened still others with abuse.

## Book 4 Chapter 4: Dorimachus Leaves Messene

While he was still staying in Messene, the pirates approached the city by night, set scaling-ladders against the walls, and broke into the farmstead called Chiron's villa; those who resisted they cut down, and the rest of the household slaves they bound and led away, together with the cattle.

The Messenian ephors, who had long been grieved both at what was happening and at Dorimachus's presence among them, and who now felt themselves insulted on top of it, summoned him before the assembled magistrates.

On this occasion Sciron, who was at that time an ephor of the Messenians and was also highly esteemed by his fellow citizens for the rest of his conduct, advised that they should not let Dorimachus leave the city unless he restored to the Messenians everything that had been lost and handed over the wrongdoers to stand trial for those who had been killed.

When everyone signified that Sciron was speaking justly, Dorimachus flew into a rage and declared that they were complete fools if they imagined they were now insulting Dorimachus and not the common body of the Aetolians; in general he regarded what was happening as outrageous, and said that they would incur a collective reckoning, and would suffer it deservedly.

Now there was at that time in Messene a depraved fellow named Babyrtas, a man debased in every possible way, upon whom, if one put Dorimachus's sun-hat and cloak, it was impossible to tell them apart,

so closely did he resemble the said Dorimachus in his voice and in the other features of his body—and this did not escape Dorimachus's notice.

So while he was speaking to the Messenians in this threatening and exceedingly arrogant manner, Sciron, in a burst of anger, said: "Do you suppose we care a whit for you or your bluster, Babyrtas?" When this was said,

Dorimachus for the moment yielded to the circumstances and agreed to make redress to the Messenians for all the wrongs that had been done;

but on returning to Aetolia he took what had been said so bitterly and so hard that, having no other reasonable pretext, for this reason alone he kindled the war against the Messenians.

## Book 4 Chapter 5: The Revenge of Dorimachus

At this time the general of the Aetolians was Ariston. Because of certain physical infirmities he was unfit for active military service, and since he was moreover a kinsman of Dorimachus and Scopas, he had in effect handed over the whole of his command to Dorimachus.

Now Dorimachus did not dare to urge the Aetolians publicly to take up the war against the Messenians, because he had no pretext worth mentioning. Everyone knew that his impulse arose from his own lawless conduct and from the insult he had suffered.

So he gave up that idea and instead set about privately persuading Scopas to join him in his design against the Messenians. He pointed out that there was no danger from Macedonia, on account of the youth of its ruler — for Philip was then no more than seventeen years old —

and he set before him the hostility of the Lacedaemonians toward the Messenians, and reminded him of the goodwill and alliance of the Eleans toward themselves. From all this he argued that their invasion of Messenia would be perfectly safe.

But the heart of his Aetolian appeal was that he held before their eyes the profits to be had from the Messenian territory, which was unguarded and had remained the only part of the Peloponnese untouched throughout the Cleomenic war.

On top of all this he urged the popularity they would win among the mass of the Aetolians as a result.

As for the Achaeans, he argued that if they tried to block the passage through their land, the Aetolians could not be blamed for defending themselves; and if the Achaeans kept quiet, they would be no hindrance to the enterprise.

Against the Messenians, he said, they would not be short of a pretext, since the Messenians had long been guilty of wrongdoing by promising the Achaeans and Macedonians that they would join their alliance.

By saying this, and other things to the same effect, he stirred up such eagerness in Scopas and his friends that, without waiting for a general assembly of the Aetolians, without consulting the Apocleti, and without taking any of the proper constitutional steps —

acting entirely on their own impulses and decisions — they launched a war simultaneously against the Messenians, Epirotes, Achaeans, Acarnanians, and Macedonians.

## Book 4 Chapter 6: Acts of Hostility Against Macedonia, Epirus, and Acarnania

At sea they at once sent out privateers, who fell in with a royal Macedonian vessel near Cythera, brought it crew and all to Aetolia, and sold off the ship-owners, the sailors, and the marines, along with the ship itself. They also ravaged the coast of Epirus,

using Cephallenian ships to carry out the outrage; and they set about seizing Thyrium in Acarnania as well.

At the same time they secretly sent some men through the Peloponnese, who occupied the stronghold called Clarium in the heart of the territory of Megalopolis. Using it as a market for their plunder, they made it their base for their raids.

However, Timoxenus, the general of the Achaeans, together with Taurion — who had been left by Antigonus in charge of the king's affairs in the Peloponnese — stormed the place completely within a few days.

For King Antigonus held Corinth by the consent of the Achaeans on account of the circumstances of the Cleomenic war; and having taken Orchomenus by force, he had not restored it to the Achaeans but had appropriated and kept it,

wishing, as it seems to me, not only to control the entrance into the Peloponnese but also to keep watch over its interior by means of his garrison and military stores in Orchomenus.

Dorimachus and Scopas watched for the moment when little time remained of Timoxenus's term of office, and when Aratus, though appointed by the Achaeans as general for the coming year, was not yet due to take up the command.

Then, gathering the Aetolians in full force at Rhium, preparing transport vessels and making the Cephallenian ships ready, they ferried their men across into the Peloponnese and advanced on Messenia.

Marching through the territory of the Patraeans, Pharaeans, and Tritaeans, they pretended that they wished to do no wrong to the Achaeans;

but since their forces could not keep their hands off the country, in their lack of restraint where booty was concerned, they passed through doing harm and damage until they reached Phigalea.

From there they made a sudden and reckless dash, and burst into the territory of the Messenians, taking no account whatever either of the friendship and alliance that had long existed between them and the Messenians, or of the principles of justice recognized among all mankind.

Setting all this below their own greed, they plundered without fear, while the Messenians did not dare to come out against them at all.

## Book 4 Chapter 7: Strength and Weakness of Aratus

The Achaeans, since their regular assembly fell due by law at this time, gathered at Aegium. When they had come together in the assembly,

the Patraeans and Pharaeans gave an account of the wrongs done to their territory during the Aetolians' passage through it, and the Messenians, present as envoys, begged them to help, since they were being wronged and treated in violation of the treaty.

After hearing what was said, the Achaeans shared the anger of the Patraeans and Pharaeans and sympathized with the misfortunes of the Messenians.

But they thought it especially outrageous that, without anyone having granted the Aetolians a passage, and without their ever having so much as asked permission, the Aetolians had dared to set foot with an army on Achaean soil in breach of the treaty.

Provoked by all this, they voted to assist the Messenians, and that the general should call the Achaeans together under arms; and whatever the assembled levy decided when it met should be binding.

Now Timoxenus, who was still general at the time — his term being all but over — and who moreover had no confidence in the Achaeans, since at present they had grown slack in their training under arms, kept putting off the expedition and the gathering of the levy altogether.

For after the downfall of Cleomenes, king of the Spartans, the Peloponnesians, worn out by the earlier wars and trusting in the present state of peace, had all neglected their preparations for war.

Aratus, however, indignant and provoked at the audacity of the Aetolians, dealt with the situation more hotly, the more so because of the long-standing hostility he already felt toward them from earlier times.

He was therefore eager to call the Achaeans together under arms and keen to engage the Aetolians.

In the end, five days before his proper term began, he took over the public seal from Timoxenus and wrote to the cities, summoning all those of military age, under arms, to Megalopolis.

About this man it seems to me fitting to say a few words first, on account of the peculiar nature of his character.

## Book 4 Chapter 8: Character of Aratus

In all other respects Aratus was a man perfectly suited to practical statesmanship.

He was capable of speaking, of devising, and of keeping a decision to himself; he was second to none in bearing political quarrels with composure, in binding men to him as friends, and in winning over allies;

and he was supremely skilled in mounting actions, deceptions, and plots against his enemies, and in carrying them through to the end by his own endurance and daring.

Clear proofs of such qualities are many, but the most conspicuous to those who have studied the details are the captures of Sicyon and Mantinea, the expulsion of the Aetolians from the city of the Pelleneans, and, greatest of all, the affair of the Acrocorinthus.

Yet this same man, whenever he wished to contest the open field, was sluggish in his plans, timid in his undertakings, and unable to face danger in plain sight.

And so he filled the Peloponnese with trophies erected against him, and in this respect he was always, in one way or another, easy prey for his enemies.

So true is it that human natures contain much that is varied, not only in their bodies but still more in their minds; so that the same man is not only gifted for some kinds of activity and incapable of others, but often, even in closely similar matters, the very same man proves both highly intelligent and exceedingly slow, and likewise both extremely bold and extremely cowardly.

This is no paradox; it is a familiar and well-known thing to those willing to take note of it.

Some men, for instance, are bold in hunting when it comes to closing with wild beasts, yet those same men are faint-hearted in the face of weapons and enemies; and in warfare itself some are ready and effective in fighting man to man and on their own account, but useless when acting in common and in equal battle order with the line.

The Thessalian cavalry, at any rate, are irresistible by squadron and in phalanx formation, but apart from a set battle line, when it comes to risking themselves man by man as time and place demand, they are awkward and slow; the Aetolians are the opposite of this.

The Cretans, both by land and by sea, are irresistible at ambushes, raids, thefts from the enemy, night attacks, and every kind of operation involving guile and piecemeal action, but for a frontal charge in line, agreed upon and face to face, they are faint-hearted and crooked in spirit; the Achaeans and Macedonians are the opposite of this.

Let this be said by me so that my readers may not distrust what I say, if at some point I make contradictory statements about the same men in regard to closely similar pursuits.

## Book 4 Chapter 9: Dorimachus Consents To Depart

When the men of military age had assembled under arms at Megalopolis in accordance with the decree of the Achaeans —

for it was from this point that I digressed — and when the Messenians once again came before the assembly and begged them not to look on while they were so flagrantly being wronged in violation of the treaty, declaring also their wish to join the common alliance and their eagerness to be enrolled together with the rest,

the leaders of the Achaeans rejected the request for alliance, saying that it was not possible to admit any new member apart from Philip and the other allies.

For the alliance that had been concluded through Antigonus during the period of the Cleomenic war was still binding by oath on them all — on the Achaeans, the Epirotes, the Phocians, the Macedonians, the Boeotians, the Acarnanians, and the Thessalians.

They said, however, that they would march out and come to the Messenians' aid, provided that the envoys present handed over their own sons as hostages to the city of the Lacedaemonians, as a guarantee that they would not make a settlement with the Aetolians without the consent of the Achaeans.

The Lacedaemonians too were encamped on the borders of Megalopolitan territory, having marched out in accordance with the alliance, though they held the position of reserves and onlookers rather than active allies.

Aratus, having settled matters with the Messenians in this way, sent word to the Aetolians, making the decisions known and ordering them to withdraw from Messenian territory and not to set foot in Achaea; otherwise, he warned, he would treat any who entered it as enemies.

When Scopas and Dorimachus heard what was said and learned that the Achaeans had mustered, they judged that it was to their advantage for the moment to comply with the orders.

So they immediately dispatched letter-carriers both to Cyllene and to Ariston, the general of the Aetolians, requesting that the transport vessels be sent to them with all speed from Elis to the island called Pheias.

Two days later they themselves struck camp, laden with booty, and advanced as if toward Elis. For the Aetolians always cultivated the friendship of the Eleans, so that through them they might gain a foothold for their raids and piratical expeditions out of the Peloponnese.

## Book 4 Chapter 10: Aratus Dismisses the Achaean Troops

Aratus waited two days and, naively believing that they would make their return by the route they had indicated, dismissed all the rest of the Achaeans and the Lacedaemonians to their homes,

but keeping three thousand foot and three hundred horse together with the soldiers under Taurion, he advanced along the road to Patrae, intending to march parallel to the Aetolians.

When Dorimachus and his men learned that Aratus was keeping pace with them and remaining under arms — partly fearing that they might be attacked while they were preoccupied with embarking on the ships, and partly being eager to provoke the war —

they sent their booty off to the boats, assigning enough capable men suited to its transport, with instructions to those sent out that the goods should be brought to Rhium, where they intended to make their embarkation.

They themselves at first lay in wait, attending to the dispatch of the booty, and after that changed direction and advanced as if toward Olympia.

But hearing that Taurion with the aforementioned force was in the neighborhood of Cleitor, and reckoning that even so they would not be able to make the crossing from Rhium without danger and a clash,

they decided it was to the advantage of their cause to engage with Aratus's men as quickly as possible, while they were still few in number and unsuspecting of what was coming,

supposing that, if they routed them, they could plunder the country in advance and so make the crossing from Rhium secure, during the time the Achaean levy was hesitating and deliberating about mustering again,

while if Aratus's men were alarmed and avoided battle, unwilling to engage, they could effect their withdrawal without danger whenever it seemed advantageous to them.

So, reasoning along these lines, they advanced and pitched camp near

## Book 4 Chapter 11: Bad Strategy of Aratus

Methydrium in Megalopolitan territory. The Achaean commanders, perceiving the presence of the Aetolians, handled the situation so badly that they left no room for greater folly.

For having turned back from Cleitor, they encamped near Caphyae.

When the Aetolians made their march from Methydrium past the city of Orchomenus, the Achaean leaders led their troops out into the plain of Caphyae and drew them up there, using as a protective barrier the river that flows through it.

But the Aetolians — both because of the difficult ground in between (for in front of the river there were also several ditches hard to cross) and because of the apparent readiness of the Achaeans for battle — lost their nerve for attacking the enemy according to their original intention,

and instead made their march in good order toward the passes leading up to Olygyrtus, content if no one would attack them and force them to risk an engagement.

But Aratus and his officers — when the Aetolian vanguard was already beginning its ascent toward the passes, while the cavalry were bringing up the rear across the plain and approaching the place called Propus at the foot of the hills — sent out the cavalry and the light-armed troops, putting Epistratus the Acarnanian in command, with orders to fasten upon the enemy's rearguard and harass them.

And yet, if an engagement had to be risked at all, they ought not to have come to grips with the rear, when the enemy had already crossed the level ground, but with the vanguard, the moment it had pushed forward into the plain.

For in that way the whole battle would have taken place on flat, level ground, where the Aetolians happened to be at their most ineffective, owing to their equipment and their whole order of arms, while the Achaeans were at their most effective and powerful, for the opposite reasons just stated.

As it was, abandoning the ground and circumstances suited to themselves, they descended into conditions that favored the enemy. The outcome, accordingly,

matched their plans. For as the light-armed troops fastened upon them, the Aetolian cavalry kept their formation and withdrew toward the lower slopes, eager to join up with their own infantry.

But Aratus and his men, neither seeing clearly what was happening nor reckoning properly what would result from it, the moment they saw the cavalry retiring, supposed that they were in flight,

and so sent out the heavy-armed troops from the wings, ordering them to go to the aid of their own light-armed men and link up with them, while they themselves wheeled the army into column and led it forward at a run and in haste.

The Aetolian cavalry, meanwhile, having crossed the plain, as soon as they joined their infantry drew back and halted under cover of the lower slopes,

while they massed their infantry on the flanks and called them up, the men readily running back at the shouting and constantly reinforcing them as they arrived from the line of march.

And when they judged that they were now a match for the enemy in numbers, they closed up and charged the Achaean cavalry and light troops fighting at the front. Being more numerous, and making their assault from higher ground, they fought for a long time, and finally routed those drawn up against them.

As these men gave way and fled, the heavy-armed troops coming to their aid, arriving in disorder along the line of march and scattered — some at a loss over what was happening, others colliding head-on with the fleeing men during the withdrawal — were forced to turn back and do the same.

The result was that those actually defeated in the engagement numbered no more than five hundred, while those in flight numbered more than two thousand.

And since the affair itself taught the Aetolians what they had to do, they followed close behind, employing their shouting in an overbearing and relentless way.

As the Achaeans made their withdrawal toward their heavy infantry, on the assumption that these were standing fast in safety in their original formation, the flight at first was orderly and free from danger.

But when they saw that these troops too had abandoned the safety of their position and were strung out and broken up on the march, some of them at once dispersed and made their retreat in disorder to the neighboring towns,

while others, colliding head-on with the phalangites advancing toward them, had no need of the enemy at all, but threw themselves into a panic and forced one another into headlong flight. They directed their flight, during the withdrawal,

as I said before, toward the towns; for Orchomenus and Caphyae, being close by, were the salvation of many. Had this not been so, they would all have been in danger of being destroyed in an unexpected disaster.

So this, then, was the engagement that took place near Caphyae —

## Book 4 Chapter 13: Aratus Denounced For His Failure

...in this way it turned out. When the Megalopolitans learned that the Aetolians were encamped near Methydrium, they came at the call of the trumpet, with their whole population, to give aid on the day immediately following the battle.

And the very men alongside whom, while still alive, they had hoped to face the danger against the enemy—these they were now forced to bury, men who had been killed by their foes.

So, having dug a trench in the plain of Caphyae and gathered up the dead, they performed the funeral rites for those unfortunate men with every mark of honor.

The Aetolians, meanwhile, having unexpectedly won their advantage by means of their cavalry and light-armed troops alone, from then on marched in complete safety straight through the middle of the Peloponnese.

During this time they made an attempt on the city of the Pellenians, overran the territory of Sicyon, and finally made their withdrawal by way of the Isthmus.

These, then, were the cause and the pretext from which the Social War arose; its formal beginning came from the decree that was afterward passed by all the allies.

## Book 4 Chapter 14: Aratus is Denounced and Defends Himself

-lip the king presiding. A few days later the general body of the Achaeans, gathered for their regular assembly, were bitterly hostile—both collectively and individually—toward Aratus, on the grounds that he was, by common agreement, the man responsible for the disaster just described.

And so, when his political opponents brought charges against him and presented vivid demonstrations of his guilt, the multitude grew still more angry and incensed.

For it seemed that his first plain error was this: that, before his term of office had even legally begun, he had seized upon a time that belonged to another in order to take up the very sort of enterprises in which he knew he had often failed.

His second error, and greater than this, was that he had disbanded the Achaeans while the Aetolians were still in the heart of the Peloponnese—and this especially when he had known in advance that Scopas and Dorimachus were eager to upset the established order and stir up war.

His third error was to engage the enemy as he did, with so few men and under no pressing necessity, when he could have withdrawn safely to the neighboring cities, mustered the Achaeans, and only then given battle to the enemy, if he judged that course to be wholly advantageous.

His last and greatest error was that, having decided to fight, he conducted matters so recklessly and so heedlessly that he abandoned the plains and the usefulness of his heavy infantry, and made the contest against the Aetolians on the hillsides with his light-armed troops alone—the very ground than which nothing could have been more useful or more suited to the Aetolians.

Nevertheless, as soon as Aratus came forward and reminded them of his earlier political career and of what he had accomplished before, and offered his defense on the charges—arguing that he was not responsible for what had happened, and asking their pardon if he had indeed overlooked something during the engagement, and urging that they should consider the whole matter not with bitterness,

but with humanity—the multitude so quickly and so magnanimously changed their minds that they became thoroughly displeased with those of his political opponents who had joined in attacking him, and resolved all subsequent measures in accordance with Aratus' judgment. These events, then, fell in the earlier Olympiad,

while what follows fell in the one hundred and fortieth.

## Book 4 Chapter 15: Lukewarmness of the Allies

The Achaeans' resolutions were these: to send envoys to the Epirotes, the Boeotians, the Phocians, the Acarnanians,

and to Philip, and to make clear in what manner the Aetolians, in violation of the treaties, had already twice invaded Achaia under arms; to call upon them for aid in accordance with the agreements; and also to receive the Messenians into the alliance;

and that the general of the Achaeans should pick out five thousand foot and five hundred horse from among the Achaeans and go to the support of the Messenians, should the Aetolians set foot in their territory;

and to arrange with both the Lacedaemonians and the Messenians how many horse and foot were to be furnished by each for the common needs. When these measures had been resolved upon,

the Achaeans, bearing what had happened nobly, abandoned neither the Messenians nor their own purpose; and those appointed as envoys to the allies carried out their missions,

while the general picked out the men from Achaia in accordance with the decree, and arranged with the Lacedaemonians and the Messenians that each should furnish two thousand five hundred foot and two hundred and fifty horse,

so that the whole combined force for the needs that would arise should be ten thousand foot and a thousand horse.

The Aetolians, meanwhile,

when their regular assembly had come around, met and resolved to keep peace with the Lacedaemonians, the Messenians, and all the rest—acting in bad faith and wishing to ruin and injure the allies of the Achaeans;

but with the Achaeans themselves they voted to keep peace on condition that they withdraw from the alliance with the Messenians, and otherwise to make war—a thing utterly absurd.

For being themselves allies of both the Achaeans and the Messenians, if these two should maintain friendship and alliance with each other, they declared war on the Achaeans; whereas if the Achaeans chose enmity toward the Messenians, they would grant them peace separately—

so that their injustice could not even be brought within the bounds of reason, because of the perversity of their very schemes.

## Book 4 Chapter 16: The Aetolians and Illyrians Invade Achaia

The Epirotes and King Philip, on hearing the envoys, admitted the Messenians into the alliance;

and as for what the Aetolians had done, though they were indignant for the moment, they were not greatly astonished, since the Aetolians had done nothing extraordinary but only something in keeping with their usual ways.

For this very reason they were not angered for long, but voted to keep peace with them—so true is it that continual wrongdoing meets with more forgiveness than rare and unexpected wickedness.

The Aetolians, at any rate, behaving in this manner—constantly plundering Greece and waging undeclared wars upon many peoples—no longer even thought it worth their while to answer those who accused them, but actually mocked anyone who summoned them to arbitration over what had happened, or, by Zeus, over what they intended to do.

And the Lacedaemonians, though they had recently been set free through Antigonus and the generous zeal of the Achaeans, and were bound to do nothing hostile toward the Macedonians and Philip, sent secret messages to the Aetolians and concluded a covert treaty of friendship and alliance with them.

When the Achaean young men had already been picked out and assigned to the support of the Lacedaemonians and Messenians, Scerdilaidas, together with Demetrius of Pharos, sailed out from Illyria with ninety galleys beyond Lissus, in violation of the treaty with the Romans.

These men first put in at Pylos and, after making assaults upon it, were beaten off;

afterward Demetrius, taking fifty of the galleys, set out for the islands, and sailing around them exacted money from some of the Cyclades and plundered others;

while Scerdilaidas, making his voyage homeward, put in at Naupactus with forty galleys, persuaded by Amynas, king of the Athamanians, who happened to be his brother-in-law.

Having made an agreement with the Aetolians, through Agelaus, concerning the division of the spoils, he promised to join the Aetolians in invading Achaia together with them.

Having concluded this agreement with Scerdilaidas, Agelaus, Dorimachus, and Scopas—since the city of the Cynaethians was being intrigued into their hands—mustered the Aetolians with their whole force and invaded...

## Book 4 Chapter 17: Factions At Cynaetha

...Achaea, together with the Illyrians. Meanwhile Ariston, the general of the Aetolians, pretended to take no notice of any of these events and stayed quietly at home, claiming that he was not making war on the Achaeans but keeping the peace—a foolish and childish way to behave.

For it is plain that a man of this sort is bound to look silly and absurd whenever he imagines that mere words can hide what the facts make obvious.

Dorimachus and his men, however, marched through Achaean territory and suddenly appeared before Cynaetha.

Now the Cynaethans, who are Arcadians, had for many years been gripped by relentless and violent civil strife. The rival parties had repeatedly inflicted on one another massacres and banishments, and on top of these the plundering of property

and redistributions of land. In the end the faction that favored the Achaean cause prevailed and took control of the city, holding the walls with a garrison and a commandant brought in from Achaea.

Such was the situation when, a little before the Aetolians arrived, the exiles sent appeals to those in the city, begging to be reconciled with them and allowed to return to their homeland.

The party holding the city was persuaded and sent an embassy to the Achaean League, wishing to carry out the reconciliation with the League's approval.

The Achaeans readily gave their consent, convinced that both sides would be well disposed toward them—since the party in control of the city had placed all its hopes in the Achaeans, while those about to return would owe their safe restoration to Achaean agreement.

So the Cynaethans dismissed the garrison and the commandant from the city, made peace, and brought back the exiles, who numbered close to three hundred, after exacting from them the pledges reckoned the most binding among mankind.

But once they were home, the returned exiles did not even wait for some cause or pretext to arise that might give the appearance of a fresh quarrel breaking out; on the contrary, the moment they were back they immediately began plotting against their country and against the very men who had saved them.

Indeed, it seems to me that at the very moment when, over the sacrificial victims, they were exchanging oaths and pledges with one another, they were already turning over in their minds this crime against the gods and against those who had trusted them.

For as soon as they were admitted to citizenship, they at once began drawing in the Aetolians and intriguing with them to seize the city, eager to bring utter ruin upon those who had saved them and upon the city that had nurtured them.

## Book 4 Chapter 18: Treason of the Cynaethans

...to destroy it root and branch. The deed they carried out with the following piece of daring, and in the following way.

Some of the returned exiles had been appointed polemarchs. It is the duty of this office to close the gates, to keep custody of the keys during the time they remain shut, and to spend the day on station at the gatehouses.

The Aetolians, then, fully equipped and with their ladders ready, watched for their opportunity;

and the polemarchs from among the exiles, after butchering their fellow officers at the gatehouse, threw open the gate.

When this happened, some of the Aetolians rushed in through the gate, while others set up their ladders, forced their way over them, and took possession of the walls. The whole population of the city,

panic-stricken at what was happening, were helpless and at a loss how to meet the situation. They could not concentrate on resisting those pouring in through the gate because of the men attacking the walls, nor could they defend the walls because of the men forcing their way through the gate.

The Aetolians, having for these reasons quickly gained control of the city, did amid all their unjust acts one wholly just thing: the very men who had let them in and betrayed the city to them they slaughtered first of all, and plundered their property.

They treated all the others of that party in the same way; and finally, quartering themselves in the houses, they ransacked them for valuables and tortured many of the Cynaethans whom they suspected of having money, plate, or anything else of unusual worth hidden away.

Having ruined the Cynaethans in this fashion, they broke camp and moved off, leaving a garrison to guard the walls, and advanced in the direction of Lusi.

On reaching the temple of Artemis, which lies between Cleitor and Cynaetha and is regarded as inviolable by the Greeks, they threatened to carry off the goddess's herds and the other property around the temple.

But the people of Lusi prudently handed over some of the goddess's sacred objects and so dissuaded the Aetolians from their impiety, sparing themselves any irreparable harm.

The Aetolians accepted the gift, immediately moved on, and pitched their camp before the city of the Cleitorians.

## Book 4 Chapter 19: More Aetolian Outrages

About this same time Aratus, the general of the Achaeans, sent off to Philip an appeal for help, began mustering his picked troops, and sent for the contingents that Sparta and Messene were bound to supply under the terms of their agreements.

The Aetolians at first tried to persuade the Cleitorians to abandon the Achaeans and choose alliance with them instead.

But when the Cleitorians flatly refused to entertain the proposal, the Aetolians launched assaults, set their ladders against the walls, and tried to take the city by storm.

When the defenders inside resisted bravely and boldly, the Aetolians gave way to circumstances, broke camp, and marched off once more toward Cynaetha, driving away with them, even so, the goddess's herds.

At first they offered Cynaetha to the Eleans; but when the Eleans refused to take it, they resolved to hold the city themselves and put Euripidas in command of it.

Afterward, however, taking fright at reports of the relief force coming from Macedonia, they set fire to the city and withdrew, advancing once more toward Rhium, where they had decided to make their crossing.

Now Taurion, learning of the Aetolian invasion and of what had been done at Cynaetha, and observing that Demetrius of Pharos had sailed in from the islands to Cenchreae, urged him to come to the aid of the Achaeans and, after hauling his galleys across the Isthmus, to fall upon the Aetolians as they made their crossing.

Demetrius, who had made his return from the islands profitable but inglorious—since the Rhodians had put to sea against him—gladly agreed to Taurion's request, once Taurion had undertaken to cover the cost of getting the galleys across.

So Demetrius hauled his ships over the Isthmus but arrived two days too late for the Aetolian crossing; after raiding some places along the Aetolian coast in advance, he sailed back to Corinth.

As for the Lacedaemonians, they shirked sending the relief force as arranged, dispatching only a very small body of cavalry and infantry, merely to keep up appearances.

Aratus, though he had his Achaean troops, dealt with the situation more as a politician than as a general.

For up to this point he kept quiet, holding back and mindful of his earlier disaster, until Scopas and Dorimachus had accomplished everything they intended and returned home—even though they made their march through country easy to attack, full of narrow defiles, where a single trumpeter would have sufficed to bring them down.

As for the Cynaethans, although they had fallen into great misfortunes and great calamities at the hands of the Aetolians, they were nevertheless thought to have suffered, of all people, the most deservedly.

## Book 4 Chapter 20: Music in Arcadia

Since the Arcadian nation as a whole enjoys a certain reputation for virtue among all the Greeks—not only for the hospitality and kindness of their character and way of life, but above all for their reverence toward the gods—

it is worth pausing briefly to consider the savagery of the Cynaethans, and to ask how it was that, while admittedly Arcadians, they so far surpassed the rest of the Greeks at that time in cruelty and lawlessness.

In my view it was because they were the first and only Arcadians to abandon those practices which their ancestors had wisely devised and thoughtfully adapted, in keeping with nature, to all the inhabitants of Arcadia. I mean music—

genuine music, that is—which it is beneficial for all people to practice, but for Arcadians is positively essential. For we must not suppose, as Ephorus does—

who, in the preface to his whole work, tossed off a remark utterly unworthy of him—that music was introduced among mankind to deceive and bewitch them.

Nor should we imagine that the ancient Cretans and Spartans had no good reason for substituting the pipe and rhythmic movement for the trumpet in war,

or that the early Arcadians acted without purpose in giving music so prominent a place in their whole constitution that they made it a compulsory part of upbringing not only for children but for young men up to the age of thirty, though in all other respects they led the most austere of lives.

For these facts are familiar to everyone: that among the Arcadians, almost alone, the children are first trained from infancy to sing, in accordance with custom, the hymns and paeans by which each community celebrates its local heroes and gods in the traditional way;

and that afterward they learn the compositions of Philoxenus and Timotheus, and dance with great enthusiasm each year to the Dionysian pipers in the theaters—the boys in the boys' contests, the young men in what are called the men's contests.

Likewise throughout their whole lives, at their gatherings, they provide their entertainment not so much through imported performers as by their own efforts, each in turn being called on to sing.

And while they consider it no disgrace to admit ignorance of any other accomplishment, singing is something they can neither disclaim—since all are compelled to learn it—nor, having learned it, openly refuse to practice, because that too is reckoned shameful among them.

Moreover, practicing marching-songs to the pipe and in formation, and laboring at dances, they display these every year in the theaters to their fellow citizens, with shared attention and at public expense.

## Book 4 Chapter 21: Why the Ancient Arcadians Turned to Music

It seems to me that the men of old introduced these practices not for the sake of luxury or abundance, but because they observed, on the one hand, that everyone worked the land with their own hands and that their way of life was, in a word, toilsome and harsh; and on the other hand, they saw the severity of character that goes with such a life because of the coldness and gloom that prevail throughout most of the region—conditions to which all of us human beings are naturally bound to conform.

For it is by this cause, and no other, that, in our distinctions by nation and by broad geographical separation, we differ so widely from one another in character, in physical form, and in coloring, and likewise in most of our customary pursuits.

Wishing, then, to soften and temper the willfulness and harshness of their nature, they introduced all the things mentioned above, and in addition established the custom of common assemblies and frequent sacrifices shared alike by men and women, and also choral dances of girls together with boys.

In short, they devised every means, eager to tame and gentle the unyielding quality of the soul through the cultivation of these habits.

The people of Cynaetha neglected these things entirely—and that although they needed such help more than anyone, since they hold by far the harshest air and territory in all Arcadia—and instead gave themselves over to their mutual feuds and rivalries.

In the end they grew so savage that no Greek city has ever been the scene of greater or more continual acts of impiety.

Here is a sign both of the Cynaethans' misfortune in this regard and of the displeasure of the other Arcadians toward such conduct:

at the time when the Cynaethans, after committing their great massacre, sent an embassy to the Spartans, every other Arcadian city they entered on the road immediately ordered them out by herald;

and the Mantineans, after the envoys had departed, even performed a purification, carrying sacrificial victims around the city and the whole of their territory.

Let this much, then, be said by me so that the common character of the Arcadians may not be slandered on account of a single city, and likewise so that no one among the inhabitants of Arcadia may suppose that the cultivation of music is carried so far among them merely for the sake of indulgence and so be tempted to neglect this branch of education;

and also for the sake of the Cynaethans themselves, that, if ever god grants them better fortune, they may humanize themselves by turning to education, and above all to music; for only in this way could they ever be rid of the savagery that then prevailed among them.

Now that we have set out what came to mind concerning the Cynaethans, let us return once more to our digression.

## Book 4 Chapter 22: Philip V. In the Peloponnese

So the Aetolians, having done such things throughout the Peloponnese, returned home safely.

Philip, coming with an army to the aid of the Achaeans, arrived at Corinth. Finding that he had come too late, he sent dispatch-bearers to all the allies, urging each of them to send delegates with all speed to Corinth to deliberate about the common interest.

He himself broke camp and advanced toward Tegea, having learned that the Spartans had fallen into bloodshed and turmoil against one another.

For the Spartans, being accustomed to being ruled by a king and to obeying their leaders in all things, had recently been set free through Antigonus; and now, with no king among them, they were in a state of faction with one another, since they all assumed they had an equal share in the government.

At first, two of the ephors kept their views concealed, while the other three made common cause with the Aetolians, convinced that because of his youth Philip would not yet be able to deal effectively with affairs in the Peloponnese.

But when, contrary to their expectation, the Aetolians made a swift withdrawal from the Peloponnese, and Philip arrived from Macedonia even more swiftly,

the three distrusted one of the other two, Adeimantus, because he was privy to all their schemes but did not very much approve of what was being done; and they were anxious that, once the king drew near, he would reveal everything that was going on to Philip.

For this reason, after conferring with some of the young men, they issued a proclamation that all those of military age should go in arms to the sanctuary of Athena of the Brazen House, on the pretext that the Macedonians were advancing on the city.

When, owing to the suddenness of it, a crowd had quickly gathered, Adeimantus, displeased at what was happening, came forward and tried to exhort and instruct them, saying:

"These proclamations and these musters under arms ought to have been ordered earlier, at the time when we were hearing that the Aetolians, who were our enemies, were drawing near the borders of our land—not now, when we learn that the Macedonians, our benefactors and saviors, are approaching with their king."

But while he was still pressing this argument, the young men who had been suborned fell upon him and stabbed him to death, and with him Sthenelaus, Alcamenes, Thyestes, Bionidas, and a good many other citizens.

But Polyphontas and certain men with him, prudently foreseeing what was to come,

## Book 4 Chapter 23: Differences of Opinion Among the Lacedaemonians

withdrew to join Philip. Having done this, the ephors now in control of affairs at once sent men to denounce to Philip those who had been killed and to urge him to hold back his approach until the city's affairs, after the disturbance that had occurred, had returned to their settled state—and meanwhile to be assured that it was their fixed intention to maintain toward the Macedonians everything that was just and friendly.

These men, meeting the king when he was already near Mount Parthenium, addressed him in accordance with their instructions.

When he had heard them out, he urged the envoys to make their return home with all speed and to inform the ephors that he would continue his march without a break and make his camp at Tegea, and that he thought they ought, as quickly as possible, to send him men of sufficient standing to confer with him about the present situation.

When those who had met him had carried out his command, the leaders of the Spartans, after hearing the king's message,

sent ten men to Philip. These men went to Tegea and entered the king's council; with Omias at their head, they accused the party of Adeimantus of being responsible for the disturbance,

and promised that they themselves would do everything for Philip that pertained to the alliance, and would show themselves second to none in goodwill toward him among those reckoned to be his true friends.

So the Spartans, having spoken these things and others like them, withdrew, and the members of the council differed with one another in their opinions.

Some, knowing the malice of those in Sparta and convinced both that the party of Adeimantus had perished because of their loyalty to the Macedonians and that the Spartans had set their minds on making common cause with the Aetolians, advised Philip to make an example of the Spartans, treating them in the same way that Alexander had treated the Thebans immediately after taking power.

But others, the older men, declared that such anger was too severe for what had happened, and that the right course was to censure those responsible, depose them, and put the government and the magistracies into the hands of his own

## Book 4 Chapter 24: The King Decides Not to Punish Sparta

friends. The king then decided the whole matter—if one ought to speak of these views as the king's: for it is not likely that a boy of seventeen could reach clear judgments about matters of such magnitude.

It is, however, proper for us who write history to attribute the opinions that decided the deliberations to those in supreme command; yet the readers themselves should bear in mind that such proposals and decisions probably came from his associates, and especially from those closest to him.

Of these, one would most fairly assign to Aratus the opinion then expressed by the king.

For Philip said that, as for the wrongs the allies committed privately against one another, it fell to him to correct and mark these only by word of mouth and by letter;

but that matters affecting the alliance as a whole were the only ones that called for joint attention and correction by all.

Since the Spartans had committed no manifest offense against the common alliance, and were promising to do everything that was just toward us, it was not right to deliberate any irrevocable measure concerning them;

for it would be absurd that his father, having conquered them when they were enemies, had done nothing harsh, while he himself, for so small a cause, should decide on some irreparable action against them.

This opinion having been ratified—that the incident should be overlooked—the king at once sent Petraeus, one of his own friends, together with the party of Omias, to urge the people to hold fast to their goodwill toward himself and the Macedonians, and at the same time to give and receive the oaths concerning the alliance.

He himself broke camp with his army and advanced once more toward Corinth, having set before the allies, in his decision regarding the Spartans, a fine example of his own policy.

## Book 4 Chapter 25: War Declared With the Aetolians

When he reached Corinth, he found that the representatives from the allied cities had already arrived. He held a council with them and deliberated about what should be done and how they ought to deal with the Aetolians.

The Boeotians charged that the Aetolians had plundered the temple of Athena Itonia while a state of peace prevailed; the Phocians, that they had marched against Ambrysus and Daulium and tried to seize those cities;

the Epirotes, that they had ravaged their territory; and the Acarnanians demonstrated in detail how the Aetolians had organized a plot against Thyrium and had even dared to assault the city while it was still night.

In addition to these, the Achaeans set out their case: that the Aetolians had seized Clarium in the territory of Megalopolis; that they had ravaged the lands of Patrae and Pharae as they passed through them; that they had thoroughly sacked Cynaetha; that they had plundered the temple of Artemis at Lusi; that they had besieged the people of Cleitor; and that they had made designs against Pylus by sea, and by land against the recently founded city of the Megalopolitans, striving together with the Illyrians to lay it utterly waste.

When the councillors of the allies had heard all this, they all with one accord resolved to declare war on the Aetolians.

Having set out the aforementioned grievances in the preamble of their resolution, they appended a decree, making the further declaration that they would help the allies recover any territory or city of theirs that the Aetolians had been holding since the time when Demetrius, the natural father of Philip, died;

and likewise, as for those who had been compelled by circumstances to take part unwillingly in the Aetolian confederacy, that they would restore all of these to their ancestral forms of government, in possession of their own land and cities, free of garrisons, free of tribute, and independent, living under their own constitutions and ancestral laws.

They also wrote that they would help the Amphictyons recover their laws and their authority over the temple, which the Aetolians had now taken away from them, since the Aetolians wished to control the affairs of the temple themselves.

## Book 4 Chapter 26: Opening of the Social War

When this resolution had been ratified, in the first year of the 140th Olympiad, the war called the Social War had taken its beginning—a beginning that was just and fitting in view of the wrongs that had been committed.

The councillors at once sent envoys to the allies, so that, once the resolution had been confirmed among each people by a vote of the citizens at large, all might join in declaring war on the Aetolians from their respective territories.

Philip also sent a letter to the Aetolians, making clear that, if they had anything just to say in their defense regarding the charges against them, they should even now come together and settle the matter through discussion;

but that if they supposed they could, without any common declaration of war, plunder and ravage everyone, while those they wronged would not defend themselves—and that, if these did defend themselves, they would be judged to have started the war—then they were the most simple-minded of all people.

When the magistrates of the Aetolians received this letter, at first they expected that Philip would not come, and so they fixed an appointed day on which they would meet him at Rhium.

But when they learned that he was actually on his way, they sent a courier explaining that they were not able, before the assembly of the Aetolians met, to settle anything on their own authority concerning matters of general policy.

The Achaeans, having gathered for their regular assembly, all ratified the resolution and proclaimed the right of reprisal against the Aetolians.

And when the king too came before the council at Aegium and spoke at some length, they received what he said with goodwill and renewed with Philip himself the friendly relations that had existed between their ancestors.

## Book 4 Chapter 27: Disingenuous Policy of the Spartans

At about the same time the Aetolians, the season of their elections having arrived, chose as their Strategus Scopas, the very man who had been responsible for all the wrongful acts just described.

About these matters I scarcely know how one ought to speak. For to pass a public decree against making war, and yet to march out in full force and plunder and carry off their neighbors' property; and to punish none of those responsible, but on the contrary to elect as generals and to honor the leaders in such deeds—this seems to me to be brimming with utter villainy.

For what else could one call such wickedness? My meaning will be made clear from the following examples.

When Phoebidas treacherously seized the Cadmeia in violation of the treaty, the Lacedaemonians penalized the man responsible, but did not withdraw the garrison—as though the wrong were undone by the punishment of the man who committed it, when in fact the opposite course was open to them; for it was the garrison that mattered to the Thebans.

Again, they proclaimed that they were leaving the cities free and self-governing in accordance with the peace concluded under Antalcidas, yet they did not withdraw their governors from the cities.

And when they had driven the Mantineans from their homes—men who were their friends and allies—they claimed they were doing no wrong, on the grounds that they had merely resettled them from one city into several;

acting with a folly that was joined to wickedness, plainly because they imagined that, if a man shuts his own eyes, his neighbors too cannot see.

For both peoples, then, this devotion to such a policy became the cause of the greatest disasters; and it is one that should in no way and on no account be emulated, either by individuals or by states, if they deliberate rightly.

King Philip, having concluded his business with the Achaeans, set out with his army for Macedonia, hastening to make his preparations for the war,

and through the aforementioned decree he held out fair hopes not only to the allies but to all the Greeks, of his mildness and royal magnanimity.

## Book 4 Chapter 28: The Wars in Italy, Greece, and Asia Become Interlaced

These events were taking place at the same time as when Hannibal, who had by now become master of all the territory south of the Iber river, was launching his attack on the city of Saguntum.

Now, if it had happened that Hannibal's first undertakings were from the very outset interwoven with affairs in Greece, it would clearly have been my duty to have given an account of them in the previous book, alternating and setting them side by side with the affairs in Iberia, following the order of the dates.

But since the wars in Italy, in Greece, and in Asia had their beginnings separately, while their conclusions were shared, I have decided to give my account of them separately as well, until we arrive at the point at which the aforementioned events became interwoven with one another and began to tend toward a single end—

for in this way both the account of the several beginnings will be clear and the interweaving evident (the very interweaving I indicated at the start, showing when, how, and for what causes it came about)—and from then on it remains to make my history of them all a single, common one.

The interweaving of events occurred near the conclusion of the war, in the third year of the 140th Olympiad. Therefore, from that point onward I shall give my account following the dates in a common narrative, but I shall treat the events before it separately,

as I have said, merely calling to mind those events of the same period that have been related in the previous book, so that my narrative may become not only easy to follow but also striking to those who attend to it.

## Book 4 Chapter 29: The Illyrians and Acarnanians Join

While wintering in Macedonia, Philip carefully enrolled his forces for the campaign ahead, and at the same time secured his position against the barbarians who lived above Macedonia.

After this he met with Scerdilaidas and, boldly placing himself in his hands, discussed terms of friendship and alliance with him.

Partly by promising to help him set in order his affairs in Illyria, and partly by denouncing the Aetolians—who gave ample grounds for denunciation—he easily persuaded him to agree to what he proposed.

For private wrongdoing differs in no real way from public wrongdoing, but only in the number and scale of what results from it. Indeed, in private life the tribe of cheats and thieves comes to ruin chiefly in this way: by failing to deal justly with one another, and in short through their treacheries toward each other. And this is exactly what happened in the case of the Aetolians at that time.

For they had agreed to give Scerdilaidas a share of the plunder if he would join them in invading Achaea; and when he had been won over and had done so,

they sacked the city of the Cynaetheans, and after driving off many captives and herds of cattle, they made Scerdilaidas no sharer at all in what had been taken.

Because of this, resentment was already smouldering in him, so that with only a brief reminder from Philip he quickly gave his assent and agreed to join the common alliance, on these terms: that he should receive twenty talents a year, and should put to sea with thirty galleys and wage war against the Aetolians by sea.

## Book 4 Chapter 30: The Acarnanians Enter the War

So Philip was occupied with these matters. Meanwhile the envoys who had been sent out to the allies came first to Acarnania and met with the people there.

The Acarnanians sincerely confirmed the decree on their part and carried the war from their own territory against the Aetolians—even though they, if anyone, would have been justified in being excused for postponing it, hesitating, and in general fearing a war with their neighbours.

For they lay directly alongside the Aetolians, sharing a common border with their country; and still more because, on their own, they were easy to overpower; and, most of all, because only a short time before they had had a taste of the most dreadful sufferings on account of their hostility with the Aetolians.

But it seems to me that men of genuine character, both in public and in private affairs, never set anything above their duty; and this principle the Acarnanians, on most occasions, are found to have maintained as well as any of the Greeks, even though they set out from but slender resources.

With such people one should not hesitate to share in undertakings when circumstances demand it, but should rather be eager to do so, more than with any other of the Greeks; for both in private and in public life they have something steadfast and devoted to liberty about them.

The Epirotes, by contrast, after listening to the envoys, confirmed the decree in much the same way, and voted to wage war against the Aetolians—but only once King Philip too should have begun it.

To the envoys who came from the Aetolians, however, they replied that the Epirotes had resolved to maintain peace with them—conducting their affairs in an ignoble and double-dealing manner.

Envoys were also sent to King Ptolemy to urge him neither to send money to the Aetolians nor to furnish them with any other aid against Philip and the allies.

## Book 4 Chapter 31: Mistaken Policy of the Messenians

As for the Messenians—on whose account the war had begun in the first place—they answered those who had come to them that, since Phigalia lay on their borders and was subject to the Aetolians, they would not enter into the war until that city had been wrested away from the Aetolians.

This decision was forced through, though the common people were by no means pleased with it, by the ephors Oenis and Nicippus and certain others of the oligarchical party, who feared the boldness of the Aetolians—men who, in my judgement at least, were gravely mistaken and wide of the mark of what was needed.

For I maintain that, while war is indeed a fearful thing, it is not so fearful that one should endure anything whatever in order to avoid taking it up.

Otherwise, why do we all make so bold with our talk of equality before the law, freedom of speech, and the very name of liberty, if nothing is to count for more than peace?

We do not, after all, praise the Thebans for their conduct in the Persian Wars, because out of fear they withdrew from the dangers undertaken on behalf of Greece and chose the side of the Persians; nor do we praise Pindar, who joined them in counselling that they keep quiet, in these verses:

"Let a man set the common good of the citizens in fair weather, and seek out the bright light of high-hearted peace." For though at the moment he seemed to have spoken persuasively,

it was not long before his pronouncement was found to be the most shameful and most harmful of all;

for peace, when it goes with justice and honour, is the fairest and most profitable of possessions, but when it goes with baseness or disgraceful cowardice it is the most shameful and most ruinous of all things.

## Book 4 Chapter 32: The Messenians Too Much Inclined to Peace

The oligarchs who led the Messenians, aiming at their own immediate private advantage, were always more eager for peace than was right.

Because of this, although they had many opportunities and favourable moments, they let them slip, and at times escaped from fears and dangers as well; but along with this policy the reckoning against them was always mounting up, and in the end they made their country wrestle with the gravest disasters.

For my own part, I think the cause was this: that they bordered on two of the most powerful peoples in the Peloponnese—indeed almost in all of Greece—I mean the Arcadians and the Laconians,

## Book 4 Chapter 39: The Black Sea

The sea called the Pontus has a circumference of close to twenty-two thousand stades, and two mouths lying diametrically opposite to each other, the one opening from the Propontis, the other from the Maeotic Lake, which itself by itself has a circumference of eight thousand stades.

Into the aforementioned basins many great rivers discharge from Asia, and still greater and more numerous ones from Europe; and so it comes about that the Maeotis, being filled up by these, flows into the Pontus through its mouth, and the Pontus into the Propontis.

The mouth of the Maeotis is called the Cimmerian Bosporus, which has a breadth of about thirty stades and a length of sixty, and is shallow throughout.

The mouth of the Pontus is likewise named the Thracian Bosporus, and its length is one hundred and twenty stades, while its breadth is not everywhere the same.

The mouth begins, on the Propontis side, at the channel between Calchedon and Byzantium,

which is fourteen stades across; and on the Pontus side, at the place called Hieron, where they say that Jason, on his return voyage from Colchis, first sacrificed to the twelve gods. This place lies on the Asiatic side, and is twelve stades distant from Europe, measured to the Sarapieum of Thrace, which lies directly opposite.

There are two causes of the continual outflow of both the Maeotis and the Pontus. The first is immediate and plain to everyone: as many streams fall into vessels of fixed circumference, the liquid keeps growing greater and greater;

and if there were no outlet, it would necessarily keep rising and take up an ever greater and larger area of the basin, whereas, since outlets do exist, the water that is added and grows excessive must overflow and run off and be carried away continuously through the existing mouths.

The second cause is this: since the rivers carry much sediment of every kind into the aforementioned basins during heavy rains, the water, pressed out by the accumulating silt deposits, keeps rising and is carried away in the same manner through the existing outlets.

And since the silting and the inflow from the rivers are unceasing and continuous, the discharge through the mouths must necessarily be unceasing and continuous too.

These, then, are the true causes of the outflow of the Pontus, deriving their credibility not from merchants' tales, but from observation according to nature—

And since we have settled on this topic, we must leave nothing idle or resting on mere assertion—as most writers are accustomed to do—but must employ a more demonstrative manner of exposition, so that we leave none of the points under inquiry unresolved for our attentive readers.

For this is the peculiar mark of the present age, in which, all seas and lands having become navigable and passable, it would no longer be fitting to use poets and myth-writers as witnesses concerning things unknown—

which is what our predecessors did about most matters, offering, as Heraclitus says, untrustworthy guarantors for disputed points. We must rather try, through history itself, to present sufficient credibility to our hearers.

We assert, then, that the Pontus has been silting up both in the past and now, and that in time both the Maeotis and the Pontus will be entirely filled up—provided the same arrangement of the regions remains, and the causes of the silting continue to operate without interruption.

For whenever time is infinite, and the basins are in every way wholly finite, it is plain that, even if only a trifling amount is brought in, they will be filled in time.

For by nature, a finite thing that is continuously being formed or destroyed in infinite time—even if it happens by the smallest degree (for let this now be supposed)—must inevitably reach completion according to the proposition.

And whenever it is not a trifling amount, but actually a very great deal of silt that is brought in, it is clear that what we are now describing will come to pass not at some distant time, but quickly.

Achaeus was a kinsman of the Antiochus who had inherited the kingdom of Syria, and he came to hold the dominion just mentioned through circumstances such as these.

For as soon as Seleucus the younger had succeeded to the kingdom and learned that Attalus had already brought the whole dominion on this side of the Taurus under his own control, he was spurred to defend his interests.

But after crossing the Taurus with a large army, he was treacherously murdered by Apaturius the Gaul and by Nicanor, and so lost his life.

Achaeus, as his kinship required, immediately avenged the murder by killing Nicanor and Apaturius, and took charge of the army and of affairs as a whole with prudence and magnanimity.

For although the opportunity lay open to him, and the impulse of the troops favored his placing the diadem on his own head, he chose not to do so; instead he kept the kingdom safe for Antiochus, the younger of the sons, and pressed on energetically to recover the whole region on this side of the Taurus.

But when affairs flowed his way beyond all expectation—since he had shut Attalus up within Pergamum itself and had become master of all the rest—he was carried away by his successes and at once ran off course.

Placing the diadem on his head and proclaiming himself king, he was at that time the weightiest and most formidable of all the kings and princes on this side of the Taurus.

And it was above all in him that the Byzantines placed their trust at that time when they took on the war against the Rhodians and Prusias.

## Book 4 Chapter 49: Complaints of Prusias

Prusias had grievances against the Byzantines that went back some time. In the first place, he complained that, although they had voted to set up certain statues of him, they had never actually erected them, but instead let the matter drift into neglect and oblivion.

He was also displeased with them for throwing all their energy into reconciling Achaeus and Attalus and bringing their feud and war to an end, since he reckoned that a friendship between those two would, in many respects, work against his own interests.

He was further irritated by the fact that the Byzantines, as it appeared, had sent delegates to Attalus to take part in the sacrifices at the festival of Athena, but had sent no one to him for the Soteria.

And so, with his anger quietly smoldering on account of all these things, he gladly seized upon the opening the Rhodians offered him. Coming to terms with their ambassadors, he agreed that they should wage the war by sea, while he himself reckoned he would do the enemy no less harm by land.

Such, then, was the war of the Rhodians against the Byzantines.

## Book 4 Chapter 50: The War between Rhodes and Byzantium Begins

This was how it came about and how it began. At first the Byzantines fought the war vigorously, convinced that Achaeus would come to their aid, and that they themselves, by bringing Tiboetes over from Macedonia, would in turn confront Prusias with fears and dangers.

But Prusias, prosecuting the war with the determination already described, took from them the place called Hieron at the mouth of the strait,

which the Byzantines had purchased at great expense a little while before and made their own because of the convenient position of the spot, for they wished to leave no one any base from which to operate—neither against the merchants sailing into the Pontus, nor with respect to the slave trade and the fisheries of that very sea.

He also took from them the territory in Asia, in Mysia, which the Byzantines had held for many years already.

The Rhodians, for their part, manned six ships and, together with these, took on four more from their allies, and, having appointed Xenophantus as admiral, sailed for the Hellespont with ten ships in all.

With the rest they lay at anchor off Sestos and barred those sailing into the Pontus, while the admiral, putting out with a single ship, tested the Byzantines, to see whether they had by now had a change of heart, cowed by the war.

When they paid him no attention, he sailed off, took up the remaining ships, and withdrew with the whole fleet to Rhodes.

The Byzantines meanwhile sent to Achaeus, asking him to help, and dispatched men to Tiboetes to bring him from Macedonia,

for it was thought that the rule of Bithynia belonged no less to Tiboetes than to Prusias, since he was Prusias' uncle on his father's side.

But the Rhodians, observing the firm stand of the Byzantines, set about practically achieving their aim.

## Book 4 Chapter 51: Byzantium, Rhodes, and Prusias Treaties

For seeing that the mainstay of the Byzantines' endurance in the war lay in their hopes resting on Achaeus, and observing that Achaeus' father was being held at Alexandria while Achaeus himself set the highest store by his father's safety, they resolved to send an embassy to Ptolemy and to ask for the release of Andromachus.

They had made this request before only as a side matter, but now they pressed for it in earnest, so that by rendering Achaeus this favor they might put him under such an obligation to them that he would grant whatever they asked.

When the ambassadors arrived, Ptolemy at first considered keeping Andromachus back, in the hope of using him at an opportune moment, both because his own disputes with Antiochus remained unresolved and because Achaeus, having recently proclaimed himself king, now controlled a domain of considerable importance—

for Andromachus was the father of Achaeus and the brother of Laodice, the wife of Seleucus.

Nevertheless, since Ptolemy was inclined toward the Rhodians in his whole policy and was eager to oblige them in everything, he gave his consent and handed Andromachus over to them to convey to his son.

Having accomplished this, and having added some honors for Achaeus besides, they stripped the Byzantines of their most substantial hope.

And another awkward thing also befell the Byzantines: Tiboetes, while being brought down from Macedonia, wrecked their plans by departing this life.

When this happened, the spirits of the Byzantines collapsed, while Prusias, emboldened in his hopes for the war, at once pressed it on himself energetically from the Asian side, and at the same time hired Thracians who kept the Byzantines from passing through their gates on the European side.

So the Byzantines, cheated of their own hopes and hard pressed by the war on every side, began to look about for a way out—

## Book 4 Chapter 52: Cavarus, Gallic King, Negotiates Peace

—a respectable way out of their situation. And when Cavarus, the king of the Gauls, arrived at Byzantium and showed himself eager to put an end to the war, ambitiously extending his hands in mediation, both Prusias and the Byzantines agreed to his proposals.

When the Rhodians learned of Cavarus' eagerness and of Prusias' compliance, and being themselves anxious to bring their own design to completion,

they appointed Aridices as ambassador to the Byzantines and sent off along with him Polemocles in command of three triremes,

wishing, as the saying goes, to send the Byzantines both the spear and the herald's staff at once. On their arrival the settlements were concluded, in the year when Cothon, son of Calligeiton, was Hieromnemon at Byzantium.

With the Rhodians the terms were straightforward: the Byzantines were to levy no toll on those sailing into the Pontus, and, this being done, the Rhodians and their allies were to be at peace with the Byzantines.

With Prusias the terms were roughly these: that there should be peace and friendship between Prusias and the Byzantines for all time, and that neither the Byzantines should make war on Prusias in any way nor Prusias on the Byzantines;

that Prusias should restore to the Byzantines their lands, their forts, their populations, and their prisoners of war without ransom, and, in addition to these, the vessels captured at the start of the war and the missiles seized in the strongholds, and likewise the timber, the stonework, and the roof tiles from the place called Hieron—

for Prusias, in his anxiety over the return of Tiboetes, had demolished all the forts that seemed conveniently situated for any purpose—

and that Prusias should also compel those among the Bithynians who held any property taken from the territory of Mysia that was subject to the Byzantines to give it back to the farmers.

Such, then, were the beginnings the war of the Rhodians and Prusias against the Byzantines took—

## Book 4 Chapter 53: War In Crete

…the end. At about this time the Cnossians sent envoys to the Rhodians and persuaded them to dispatch the ships under Polemocles' command, together with three more undecked vessels which they were to launch and send in addition.

When this had been done and the ships had arrived in Crete, the people of Eleutherna, suspecting that Polemocles and his men had put to death their fellow-citizen Timarchus as a favor to the Cnossians, first proclaimed a right of reprisal against the Rhodians, and after that went to open war.

The Lyttians too, a little before this time, met with an irreparable disaster. For the affairs of Crete as a whole were at that time in roughly the following state.

The Cnossians, having come to an understanding with the Gortynians, had brought the whole of Crete under their power except the city of the Lyttians; and since this city alone refused to submit, they set about making war on it, eager to destroy it utterly, as an example and a source of terror to the rest of the Cretans.

At first, then, all the Cretans together waged war against the Lyttians; but when rivalry arose out of trivial occasions—as is the way with the Cretans—they fell into faction against one another.

The Polyrrhenians, the Ceraïtae, and the Lappaeans, and with them the Horii together with the Arcadians, with one accord broke away from their friendship with the Cnossians,

and resolved to make common cause with the Lyttians. Among the Gortynians, the older men chose the side of the Cnossians, while the younger chose that of the Lyttians, and so they fell into faction with each other.

The Cnossians, since this upheaval among their allies had come upon them unexpectedly, called in a thousand men from Aetolia in accordance with their alliance.

As soon as this had happened, the older men of the Gortynians seized the citadel and brought in the Cnossians and the Aetolians; and some of the young men they expelled, and others they killed,

## Book 4 Chapter 54

and they handed the city over to the Cnossians. At about the same time, when the Lyttians had marched out in full force into enemy territory, the Cnossians, perceiving what had happened, seized Lyttos while it was stripped of its defenders.

They sent off the children and the women to Cnossus, and after burning the city, razing it to the ground, and ravaging it in every possible way, they returned home.

The Lyttians, arriving back at their city from the expedition and seeing what had befallen it, were so overcome with grief in their hearts that not one of those present had the courage even to enter his homeland;

but all of them, after marching around it in a circle, and after wailing aloud and lamenting again and again the fate of their fatherland and their own, turned back and withdrew once more to the city of the Lappaeans.

The Lappaeans received them kindly and with all eagerness; and these men, who in a single day had become cityless strangers instead of citizens, joined their allies in making war against the Cnossians.

Thus Lyttos—a colony and kinswoman of the Lacedaemonians, the most ancient of the cities of Crete, and by common consent the constant nurse of the bravest men among the Cretans—was utterly and unexpectedly swept away.

## Book 4 Chapter 55: Polyrrhen and Lappa Join the Alliance

The people of Polyrrhenia and Lappa, together with all their allies, observed that the Cnossians were holding fast to their alliance with the Aetolians, and they saw that the Aetolians were at war with both King Philip and the Achaeans. They therefore sent envoys to the king and to the Achaeans to ask for military aid and for admission into alliance.

The Achaeans and Philip received them into the common alliance and dispatched a relief force: four hundred Illyrians under the command of Plator, two hundred Achaeans, and a hundred Phocians.

These troops arrived and, after no long time, sailed home again, having greatly strengthened the position of the Polyrrhenians and their allies.

For in a very short span of time they penned the Eleuthernaeans and the Cydonians, and the people of Aptera as well, within their walls, and forced them to abandon the alliance with the Cnossians and share in the same hopes as the Polyrrhenians. Once this had been accomplished,

the Polyrrhenians and the allies who stood with them sent five hundred Cretans to Philip and the Achaeans, while the Cnossians, a little earlier, had sent a thousand to the Aetolians. Both contingents fought alongside their respective sides in the war then under way.

Meanwhile the Gortynian exiles also seized the harbor of Phaestus, and likewise held the harbor of Gortyn itself by a daring stroke; and using these places as their bases of operations, they made war on the party within the city.

## Book 4 Chapter 56: Raid of Aetolians In the Peloponnese

Such, then, was the state of affairs in Crete. Around this same time Mithridates also opened war against the people of Sinope, and this became, as it were, the beginning and the pretext of the misfortune that was ultimately brought upon the Sinopeans.

When, with a view to this war, they had sent an embassy to the Rhodians and called upon them for aid, the Rhodians resolved to appoint three men and to give them a hundred and forty thousand drachmas, with which those appointed were to procure the supplies needed by the Sinopeans.

The men so commissioned made ready ten thousand jars of wine, three hundred talents of dressed hair, a hundred talents of prepared sinew, a thousand suits of armor, three thousand stamped gold pieces, and besides these four catapults together with the men to operate them.

The Sinopean envoys took these and returned home; for the Sinopeans were in dread that Mithridates might undertake to besiege them both by land and by sea, and so they were directing all their preparations to that end.

Sinope lies on the right-hand side of the Pontus as one sails toward Phasis, and it is settled on a peninsula that juts out into the sea. The neck of this peninsula, joining it to Asia, is no more than two stades across, and the city, set upon it, effectively closes it off from sea to sea.

The remainder of the peninsula projects out into the open sea; it is level ground and entirely easy to approach toward the city, but all around, on the seaward side, it is sheer, hard to land upon, and offers altogether very few points of access.

For this reason the Sinopeans were anxious that Mithridates might construct siege works along the side facing Asia, and likewise, on the side opposite to it, make a landing from the sea onto the level ground and the heights overlooking the city, and so attempt to besiege them.

They therefore set about fortifying the seaward circuit of the peninsula, staking off and entrenching the approaches from the sea, while at the same time placing missile weapons and soldiers at the points of advantage.

For the whole extent of it is not large, and is altogether easy to control and of modest size.

## Book 4 Chapter 57: Philip Starts for Aetolia

So the affairs at Sinope stood as I have described. Meanwhile King Philip, having set out from Macedonia with his army — for it was at this stage of his undertakings that I broke off my account of the Social War — marched toward Thessaly and Epirus, eager to make his invasion of Aetolia by that route.

At about this same time Alexander and Dorimachus, having an intrigue under way against the city of the Aegiratans, gathered around twelve hundred Aetolians at Oeanthe in Aetolia, which lies directly opposite the aforesaid town, and, having made ready ferry boats for them, were watching for a chance to sail across in fulfillment of their plan.

For one of the deserters from Aetolia, who had spent a considerable time among the Aegiratans and had observed that the men guarding the gate facing Aegium got drunk and carried out their watch carelessly,

had crossed over again and again to Dorimachus and his party and invited them to attempt the venture, since they were thoroughly at home in enterprises of this kind.

The city of the Aegiratans is built on the Peloponnesian shore of the Corinthian Gulf, between the cities of Aegium and Sicyon. It sits upon rugged and hard-to-climb hills, faces by its position toward Parnassus and that part of the coast opposite, and lies about seven stades back from the sea.

When a chance to sail offered itself to Dorimachus and his men, they put out and came to anchor, while it was still night, at the mouth of the river that flows down past the city.

Alexander and Dorimachus, then, and with them Archidamus the son of Pantaleon, having the main body of the Aetolians about them, began advancing toward the city along the road that comes from Aegium.

But the deserter, taking the twenty fittest men, made his way over the cliffs by the trackless routes faster than the rest thanks to his familiarity with the ground, and slipping in through a certain water channel, caught the men at the gate still asleep.

Having cut them down while they were still in their beds, and having hacked through the bars with their axes, he opened the gates to the Aetolians.

These rushed in, but then handled the situation with conspicuous lack of forethought — which proved to be a contributing cause of the Aegiratans' salvation and of the Aetolians' destruction.

For supposing that getting inside the gates was the whole point of taking an enemy's city, in this manner

## Book 4 Chapter 58: The Aetolians Repulsed At Aegira

they handled their affairs. And so, after staying together only a very short time around the marketplace, they then, in their passion for plunder, drifted apart, broke into the houses, and began carrying off the people's property — and by now it was broad daylight.

The Aegiratans, the affair having befallen them utterly unhoped-for and unexpected, were thrown into panic and terror: those upon whose houses the enemy had descended all turned to flight out of the city, supposing that it was already firmly in the enemy's hands.

But all those who, hearing the shouting, came out to help from houses that had not yet been touched, ran together to the citadel.

These kept growing more numerous and more confident, while the body of Aetolians, on the contrary, grew smaller and more disordered, for the reasons already given.

Nevertheless Dorimachus and his men, seeing the danger now closing around them, rallied together and charged the men holding the citadel, supposing that by their boldness and daring they would strike panic into those who had assembled for the defense and put them to flight.

But the Aegiratans, cheering one another on, fought back and grappled with the Aetolians nobly.

Since the citadel was unwalled, and the fighting was hand to hand and man against man, the contest was at first what one would expect, the one side fighting for fatherland and children, the other for their own survival. In the end, however, the Aetolians who had broken in were routed.

The Aegiratans, seizing the opening of their enemies' wavering, pressed upon them vigorously and terrifyingly. As a result it came about that most of the Aetolians, in their panic, fleeing of their own accord, were trampled together at the gates.

Alexander fell in hand-to-hand fighting in the thick of the action, while Archidamus perished in the crush and suffocation at the gates.

Of the rest of the Aetolian host, part was trampled underfoot, and part, fleeing over the cliffs by the trackless ways, broke their necks.

What part of them did get through safely to the ships threw away their arms in utter disgrace and made their voyage back, as unexpectedly as they had come.

Thus the Aegiratans, having lost their fatherland through negligence, recovered it again, against all expectation, through their courage and gallantry.

## Book 4 Chapter 59: Euripidas

At about the same time Euripidas, who had been sent out by the Aetolians as general for the Eleans, overran the territory of Dyme, Pharae, and also Tritaea, and after rounding up a considerable quantity of plunder, began withdrawing toward Elis.

But Miccus of Dyme, who happened at that time to be deputy general of the Achaeans, came out to the rescue with the full levy of the Dymaeans and Pharaeans, and with the Tritaeans as well, and fell upon the enemy as they were retiring.

Pressing too eagerly upon the retreating force, however, he ran into an ambush and was checked, and lost many of his men: forty were killed, and about two hundred of the infantry taken prisoner.

Euripidas, then, having won this advantage and being elated at what had happened, marched out again a few days later and seized, near the Araxus, a well-placed fort of the Dymaeans called the Wall —

which the myths say Heracles built long ago, when he was at war with the Eleans, wishing to use it as a base of operations against them.

## Book 4 Chapter 60: Philip Advances Southward

The peoples of Dyme, Pharae, and Tritaea, having come off the worse in their attempt at relief and fearing what was to come from the seizure of the fort, at first sent messengers to the general of the Achaeans, reporting what had happened and asking him to help them; afterwards they dispatched envoys to make the same request.

But Aratus was unable to assemble the mercenary force, because during the Cleomenic War the Achaeans had fallen behind in some of the pay owed to the hired soldiers; and in general he conducted his undertakings — in a word, the whole business of the war — without daring and sluggishly.

For this reason Lycurgus took the Athenaeum of the Megalopolitans, and Euripidas, following up the successes already mentioned, took Gortyna in the territory of Telphusa.

And the people of Dyme, Pharae, and Tritaea, having lost hope in any help from the general, agreed among themselves not to pay the common contributions to the Achaeans,

but to raise mercenaries of their own — three hundred infantry and fifty cavalry — and through these to secure their territory.

In doing this they were thought to have deliberated reasonably enough regarding their own affairs, but the opposite regarding the common interest; for they seemed to have become the originators and leaders of a wicked approach and pretext for those who wished to break up the confederacy.

Yet the greatest share of the blame for this action one might justly lay upon the general, who was negligent, kept procrastinating, and continually abandoned those who needed him.

For every man who is in danger, so long as he can hold on to some hope from his kinsmen and allies, is inclined to rely on them; but when, in his straits, he gives up that hope, then at last he is forced to help himself as best he can.

Therefore the Tritaeans, Pharaeans, and Dymaeans are not to be blamed for raising mercenaries of their own when the leader of the Achaeans was dragging his feet; but for renouncing the contributions to the common treasury they deserve reproach.

For they ought not to have neglected their own need — being indeed in a good position and able to act — yet they should at the same time have kept up their just obligations toward the common commonwealth, all the more since recovery of such payment was guaranteed without fail under the common laws, and, above all, since they had been founders of the Achaean confederacy.

## Book 4 Chapter 61: Philip Arrives in Epirus

Such, then, was the state of affairs in the Peloponnese. King Philip, having passed through Thessaly, arrived in Epirus.

Taking up the Epirotes alongside the Macedonians in full force, together with the three hundred slingers from Achaia who had joined him, and in addition the five hundred Cretans sent by the Polyrrhenians, he advanced; and having passed through Epirus, he arrived in the territory of the Ambracians.

Now, if he had at once pressed straight on and thrust into the interior of Aetolia, falling upon it suddenly and unexpectedly with so heavy a force, he would have put an end to the whole business.

But as it was, persuaded by the Epirotes to reduce Ambracus first by siege, he gave the Aetolians a breathing space in which to make a stand, to take some forethought, and to prepare for what lay ahead.

For the Epirotes, placing their own interest above the common interest of the allies and being intensely eager to bring Ambracus under their own control, begged Philip to lay siege to the stronghold and to capture this first,

since they counted it of the greatest importance to recover Ambracia from the Aetolians, and hoped that this could be accomplished only if, having gained mastery of the aforementioned place, they should then settle down to besiege the city.

For Ambracus is a stronghold well fortified with both outworks and a wall, and it lies amid marshes, having a single narrow, raised causeway as its approach from the land; and it is conveniently situated so as to command both the territory of the Ambracians and the city.

Philip, then, won over by the Epirotes and having encamped around Ambracus, set about

## Book 4 Chapter 62: Scopas Destroys Dium

the preparations for the siege. Scopas, meanwhile, at this same time took up the Aetolians in full force, and making his march through Thessaly, invaded Macedonia; he advanced and destroyed the grain throughout Pieria, and after gathering a great quantity of plunder, he set off back, making his march toward Dium.

When the inhabitants had abandoned the place, he entered it and razed the walls, the houses, and the gymnasium; and besides these he burned the colonnades around the sacred precinct and destroyed the rest of the offerings—all that served for adornment or for the use of those gathering for the festivals; and he overturned all the statues of the kings as well.

This man, then, having from the very outset of the war and in his very first action carried on war not only against men but also against the gods, made his way back.

And on arriving in Aetolia, he was honored and admired—not as one who had committed sacrilege, but as a man who had proved himself good in the public service—having filled the Aetolians with empty hopes and irrational arrogance.

For from these events they conceived the notion that no one would dare even to come near Aetolia, while they themselves would fearlessly plunder not only the Peloponnese, as had been their custom, but Thessaly and Macedonia as well.

## Book 4 Chapter 63: Philip's Aetolian Campaign

Philip, however, on hearing of the events in Macedonia—and having at once reaped the consequences of the Epirotes' ignorance and contentiousness—was besieging Ambracus.

Making vigorous use of his earthworks and the rest of his siege apparatus, he quickly cowed those within, and took the stronghold in forty days all told.

He let the garrison—some five hundred Aetolians—depart under truce, and so fulfilled the Epirotes' desire by handing Ambracus over to them.

He himself, taking up his force, advanced past Charadra, eager to cross the so-called Ambracian gulf where it is narrowest, near the Acarnanian temple called Actium.

For the aforementioned gulf issues from the Sicilian sea between Epirus and Acarnania through an altogether narrow mouth—less than five stades wide—

but as it advances inland it stretches to a breadth of a hundred stades, while in length it runs in from the sea about three hundred stades; and it forms the boundary between Epirus and Acarnania, having Epirus on the north and Acarnania on the south.

Having ferried his force across at the aforementioned mouth, and having passed through Acarnania, he came to the Aetolian city called Phoeteiae, having taken along on the way two thousand Acarnanian infantry and two hundred cavalry.

Having encamped around the aforementioned city and made vigorous and terrifying assaults upon it for two days, he took it by capitulation, releasing the Aetolians within under truce.

But on the following night, since the city was thought to be still untaken, five hundred Aetolians came to its aid; the king, learning of their approach beforehand, set ambushes at certain well-chosen spots, killed most of them, and took the rest captive, except for a very few.

After this, having measured out grain rations for thirty days to his force from the captured stores—for a great quantity had been found amassed at Phoeteiae—he advanced, making his march into the territory of Stratus.

At a distance of about ten stades from the city he encamped beside the river Achelous, and from there he set out and fearlessly ravaged the country, since none of the enemy dared to come out against him.

## Book 4 Chapter 64: Philip Marches Toward Metropolis

At this time the Achaeans, hard pressed by the war and learning that the king was close at hand, sent ambassadors asking him to come to their aid.

These men, meeting Philip while he was still near Stratus, discussed the rest of their business according to their instructions, and, pointing out to the army the spoils to be had from the enemy's country, tried to persuade him to cross the Rhium and invade Elis.

The king, having heard them out, detained the ambassadors, saying that he would deliberate about their requests; he himself broke camp and advanced, making his march toward Metropolis and Conope.

The Aetolians held the citadel of Metropolis but abandoned the town. Philip, having set fire to Metropolis, pressed straight on toward Conope.

When the Aetolian cavalry had gathered and dared to confront him at the crossing of the river, which lies twenty stades in front of the city, and were confident that they would either stop him altogether or do much harm to the Macedonians during the crossing,

the king, perceiving their design, ordered his peltasts to enter the river first and to make the crossing in a dense body, locked together unit by unit with shields interlocked.

His men obeyed; and as soon as the first company had crossed, the Aetolian cavalry made a brief trial against it, but since this company stood fast with shields locked, and the second and third, as they crossed, joined their arms to the one already in place, the cavalry, accomplishing nothing and finding themselves in difficulty, withdrew toward the city.

And from then on the spirit of the Aetolians, having taken refuge in their cities, held itself quiet.

Philip, having crossed over with his army and fearlessly ravaged this district too, advanced, making his march toward Ithoria. This is a stronghold that lies squarely on the pass and is remarkable for its strength, both natural and man-made.

As he drew near, the garrison, terror-stricken, abandoned the place; and the king, having gained mastery of it, razed it to the ground.

In the same way he ordered his foraging parties to deal likewise with the remaining towers throughout the country

## Book 4 Chapter 65: Philip Takes Paeanium

...to bring it down. Once he had passed through the narrows, he made the rest of the march at an easy, unhurried pace, allowing his army the chance to gather plunder from the countryside.

When the camp was fully stocked with every kind of provision, he came to Oeniadae.

Pitching his camp near Paeanium, he decided to capture this place first. By mounting continuous assaults he took it by storm—a city not large in its perimeter, for it measured less than seven stades, but in the whole construction of its houses, walls, and towers inferior to none.

He razed the entire wall to the ground, and, dismantling the houses, he fitted the timber and roof-tiles onto rafts and kept floating them down the river to Oeniadae with great enthusiasm.

At first the Aetolians set out to hold the citadel at Oeniadae, having secured it with walls and the rest of their works; but as Philip drew near they took fright and abandoned it.

The king, having taken over this city as well, advanced from it and encamped in Calydonia at a stronghold called Elaeus, which had been made exceptionally secure with walls and the other defenses—Attalus having undertaken to build up its fortifications for the Aetolians.

Carrying this place too by storm, the Macedonians ravaged the whole of Calydonia and then returned again to Oeniadae.

Philip, observing how advantageously the place was situated, both for other purposes and especially for the crossings into the Peloponnese, set about walling the town.

For Oeniadae happens to lie by the sea, at the edge of Acarnania where it borders on the Aetolians, around the mouth of the Corinthian Gulf.

The town is positioned opposite the coastline of the Dymaeans in the Peloponnese, and lies nearest to the region around Araxus, for it is no more than a hundred stades distant. With his eye on these advantages, he fortified the citadel by itself,

and, throwing a wall around the harbor and the dockyards together, he undertook to connect them to the citadel, using the materials from Paeanium to manage the work.

## Book 4 Chapter 66: Philip Recalled To Macedonia

While the king was still occupied with this work, a messenger arrived from Macedonia making it clear that the Dardani, suspecting his intended expedition into the Peloponnese, were gathering forces and making great preparations, having resolved to invade Macedonia.

On hearing this, and judging it necessary to come quickly to the aid of Macedonia, he dismissed the envoys from the Achaeans, giving them the reply that, once he had dealt with what had just been reported to him, he would after that make nothing more pressing than to help them to the best of his power.

He himself broke camp and made his return march in haste, by the same route as that by which he had come.

Just as he was about to cross the Ambracian Gulf from Acarnania into Epirus, Demetrius of Pharos appeared, sailing in a single boat, having been driven out of Illyria by the Romans—matters I have made clear in the earlier parts of this work.

Philip received him kindly and told him to sail toward Corinth and from there to come through Thessaly into Macedonia; while he himself crossed into Epirus and pressed steadily onward.

When he reached Pella in Macedonia, the Dardani—learning of Philip's arrival from some Thracian deserters—took fright and at once broke up their expedition, even though they were already close to Macedonia.

Philip, on learning of the Dardani's change of heart, dismissed all the Macedonians to gather in the autumn harvest, while he himself went to Thessaly and spent the remaining part of the summer at Larisa.

It was around this time that Aemilius brought his triumph into Rome in splendid style for his Illyrian campaign, and that Hannibal, having taken Saguntum by storm, dismissed his forces into winter quarters;

while the Romans, when news of the capture of Saguntum reached them, sent envoys to demand the surrender of Hannibal from the Carthaginians, and at the same time made their preparations for the war, appointing as consuls Publius Cornelius and Tiberius Sempronius.

I have set out the particulars of these matters in the previous book; but I have now brought them forward by way of reminder, in keeping with my original undertaking, so that the corresponding events may be known.

## Book 4 Chapter 67: Philip Returns To the Peloponnese

So this Olympiad year was drawing to a close. Among the Aetolians, the time of the elections having now arrived, Dorimachus was chosen general. He at once took up his office, gathered the Aetolians under arms, and invaded the upland regions of Epirus, ravaging the country and pursuing its destruction with unusual ferocity.

For in most of what he did his aim was not so much his own profit as the injury of the Epirotes.

And when he reached the sanctuary at Dodona, he burned the colonnades, destroyed many of the votive offerings, and even razed the sacred building to the ground,

so that the Aetolians observed the bounds neither of peace nor of war, but in both circumstances alike acted on their designs in defiance of the common customs and laws of mankind.

Having accomplished these and similar deeds, Dorimachus then withdrew once more to his own country.

The winter was still advancing, and everyone had given up expecting Philip to appear because of the season. But the king took up three thousand bronze-shielded troops, two thousand peltasts, three hundred Cretans, and in addition to these about four hundred horsemen of the royal guard, and set out from Larisa.

He moved them across from Thessaly into Euboea, and from there to Cynos, and came through Boeotia and the Megarid to Corinth about the time of the winter solstice, having made his approach so swift and secret that not one of the Peloponnesians suspected what had happened.

He closed the gates of Corinth, posted guards to control the roads, and on the very next day summoned Aratus the elder to come to him from Sicyon. He also sent off dispatches to the general of the Achaeans and to the cities, in which he made clear when and where they must all assemble under arms.

## Book 4 Chapter 70: Philip In Arcadia

Marching through Arcadia and enduring heavy snow and great hardship in the passes around Mount Olygyrtus, he came down on the third day, by night, to Caphyae.

After resting his force there for two days and taking on Aratus the younger together with the Achaeans he had gathered—so that the whole force came to about ten thousand men—he advanced through the territory of Clitor toward Psophis, collecting missiles and scaling ladders from the towns he passed through.

Psophis is, by common agreement, an ancient foundation of the Arcadians, belonging to the district of Azanis. With respect to the Peloponnese as a whole it lies in the interior, but with respect to Arcadia itself it stands on the western frontier, bordering on those who dwell at the farthest edges of the western Achaeans.

It is also well placed to overlook the territory of the Eleans, with whom at that time it happened to share a political alliance.

Reaching it on the third day from Caphyae, Philip encamped on the hills that rise opposite the city, from which he could safely survey both the whole town and the country round about it.

But when the king observed the strength of Psophis, he was at a loss what to do.

Along its western side rushes a violent winter torrent, which for the greater part of the winter is impassable, and which in any case makes the city secure and hard to approach because of the size of the ravine that its waters have gradually carved out over time as they sweep down from the higher ground.

Along its eastern side it has the Erymanthus, a great and violent river about which much has been said by many writers.

Since the torrent flows into the Erymanthus on the southern side of the city, the result is that three of its faces are enclosed by the rivers and protected in the manner described.

On the remaining side, that toward the north, there rises a strong fortified hill, which serves as a well-placed and serviceable citadel; and the city also has walls remarkable for their size and construction.

On top of all this, a relieving force from the Eleans had come into the town, and Euripidas, having survived his flight, was now inside it.

## Book 4 Chapter 71: Philip Captures Psophis

Taking all of this in and weighing it up, Philip was in part dissuaded by his calculations from forcing and besieging the city, yet in part eager for it, seeing the strategic advantage of the place.

For in proportion as it then bore down upon the Achaeans and Arcadians and served the Eleans as a secure base of war, so, if it were taken in turn, it would shield the Arcadians and become a well-placed jumping-off point for the allies against the Eleans.

Having therefore set his mind on this course, he ordered all the Macedonians to take their breakfast at first light and to stand ready in full equipment.

After this he crossed the bridge over the Erymanthus, and since no one stood in his way—so unexpected was the move—he came right up to the city itself with energy and to terrifying effect.

Euripidas and all those in the city were thrown into confusion by what was happening, for they had been convinced that the enemy would neither dare to attack and storm so strong a city in a single onset, nor be able to mount a prolonged siege, given the difficulty of the season.

And as they reasoned thus, they grew distrustful of one another, fearing that Philip had arranged some treachery against the city through men within it.

But when they saw nothing of the kind arising among themselves, the greater part rushed to the walls to defend them, while the Elean mercenaries went out by a gate on the higher ground, meaning to fall upon the enemy.

The king arranged the men who were to bring the ladders against the wall at three points, distributed the rest of the Macedonians likewise among them, and then, giving each party the signal by trumpet, launched the assault on the walls from every side at once.

At first the defenders of the city resisted gallantly and hurled many men down from the ladders.

But when the supply of missiles and of the other things needed ran short—as was bound to happen, since the preparations had been made on the spur of the moment—and the Macedonians, far from being daunted by what was happening, kept replacing each man thrown from the ladders, the next coming up without hesitation into his place,

at last the men of the city broke and all fled toward the citadel. Of the king's troops, the Macedonians mounted the wall, while the Cretans closed with the mercenaries who had sallied out by the upper gate and forced them to throw down their arms and flee in utter disorder.

Pressing upon them and laying hands on them, the Cretans burst in through the gate along with them; and so it came about that the city was taken from every side at once.

The Psophidians, then, withdrew with their children and wives into the citadel, and with them Euripidas and his men, and likewise the rest of the survivors.

## Book 4 Chapter 72: The People of Psophis Surrender

The Macedonians, pouring in, at once plundered all the household goods from the houses, and after that quartered themselves in the dwellings and held the city.

Those who had taken refuge in the citadel, having no provisions of any kind, foresaw what was coming and decided to surrender themselves to Philip.

So they sent a herald to the king, and having obtained leave to send an embassy, they dispatched their magistrates and Euripidas along with them; these men made a truce and secured safety for all who had taken refuge there, foreigners and citizens alike.

The envoys then returned to the place from which they had set out, carrying instructions to stay where they were until the army should march off, so that none of the soldiers might disobey and plunder them.

The king, however, was forced by a fall of snow to remain on the spot for some days. During this time he gathered the Achaeans who were present and first pointed out to them the strength and strategic value of the city for the present war.

He then recounted the goodwill and devotion he felt toward their nation, and ended by declaring that he now ceded and gave the city to the Achaeans; for it was his settled aim to grant them what favors he could and to fall short in nothing of his zeal for them.

When Aratus and his men, along with the rest of the gathering, thanked him for this, Philip dismissed the assembly, set his army in motion, and marched toward Lasion.

The Psophidians came down from the citadel and recovered their city, each man his own dwelling, while Euripidas and his men departed to Corinth and from there to Aetolia.

Of the Achaean magistrates who were present, they set Prolaus of Sicyon over the citadel with an adequate garrison, and Pythias of Pallene over the city.

Such was the conclusion of the affair at Psophis.

Having settled these matters, he reached Olympia on the fifth day. There he sacrificed to the god, gave a banquet for his officers, and at the same time rested the remainder of his army for three days, after which he broke camp once more.

Advancing into Elean territory, he loosed his foraging parties upon the countryside while he himself pitched camp near the place called the Artemisium.

After taking in the plunder there, he moved on again to the Dioscurium. As the land was being ravaged, the number of those captured was large, but still larger was the number of those who fled together into the neighboring villages and the fortified places.

For it happens that the territory of the Eleans is exceptionally well populated and full of people and property, beyond the rest of the Peloponnese.

Indeed, some of them are so devoted to life on the land that there are men who, for two and three generations, though possessing ample estates, have never once set foot in a public assembly.

This comes about because those who manage public affairs take great care and forethought for the people living in the countryside, so that justice may be administered to them on the spot and they may lack nothing for the necessities of life.

It seems to me that they devised and enacted all these arrangements in ancient times both because of the great extent of their land and, above all, because of the sacred manner of life they once enjoyed,

when, having received from the Greeks a concession on account of the contest of the Olympic Games, they inhabited Elis as a land sacred and inviolate, untouched by any...

## Book 4 Chapter 74: Peace the Only Unquestioned Blessing

...terror or any circumstance of war. But afterward, when the dispute with the Arcadians over Lasion and the whole of Pisatis forced them to defend their territory and to change their ways of living,

they no longer took even ordinary trouble to recover from the Greeks their ancient and ancestral immunity from pillage, but remained as they were—not, in my judgment at least, making correct provision for the future.

For consider: there is one thing that we all pray to the gods to obtain, and for the sake of which we endure anything in our longing to share in it—the one thing among our reckoned goods that is beyond all dispute among men—I mean peace. If, then, certain people who are able to acquire this from the Greeks, with justice and propriety and uncontested for all time, neglect it or treat something else as more important than it, how could they fail to seem, by common consent, to be blind to their own interest? "Yes, by Zeus,"

someone may object, "but perhaps by such a manner of life they become an easy target for those who set out to make war and to violate treaties."

Yet such a thing is rare, and even if it should ever occur, it would be eligible for the common protection of the Greeks.

And as for the lesser, particular injustices, given the means that would have accrued to their estates—as would naturally be the case for people living always in peace—it is plain that they would not have lacked foreign troops and mercenaries to stand on guard at the various places and occasions.

As it is, fearing the rare and the unexpected, they hold their land and their livelihoods amid continual wars and devastations.

Let this, then, be said by us for the sake of admonishing the Eleans, since the circumstances of the times have never before offered a more favorable disposition than the present for them to acquire, by the agreement of all, their immunity from pillage. The greatest quantity of movable goods and the greatest throng of people and livestock had been gathered into the place they call Thalamae, because the country around it was narrow and hard to penetrate, and the place itself was inaccessible and difficult to approach.

## Book 4 Chapter 76: Popularity of Philip In the Peloponnese

Now Apelles—who was one of the guardians of the boy left in charge by Antigonus, and who at that time happened to have more influence than anyone else with the king—wishing to bring the Achaean nation into a condition like that of the Thessalians, set about doing a wicked thing.

For the Thessalians were thought to govern themselves by their own laws and to differ greatly from the Macedonians, but in fact they differed not at all: they suffered everything just as the Macedonians did and carried out every order given by the royal officials.

And so, fitting his action to this design, the man just mentioned set out to make trial of his fellow soldiers.

First, then, he permitted the Macedonians to evict from their quarters, on every occasion, those Achaeans who had taken possession of lodgings first, and likewise to strip them of their plunder.

After that, through his subordinates he laid hands on them on any chance pretext, and those who shared the others' indignation, or came to the aid of the men being flogged, he himself, being present, hauled off in chains,

convinced that by such a method he would, little by little and unnoticed, bring them into the habit of regarding nothing as outrageous, whatever a man might suffer at the king's hands;

and this although only a short time before he had campaigned alongside Antigonus and had seen that the Achaeans had endured the trial of every terror rather than do what Cleomenes commanded.

Nevertheless, when a group of young Achaeans banded together and approached Aratus and his circle and made clear to them the intention of Apelles, Aratus and his men went to Philip, having decided to take a stand on such matters from the outset and not to delay. ## Book 4 Chapter 77: Phillidas and the Aetolian Troops Arrive

Philip, then, was winning a high reputation—both for his conduct in his dealings with the men who shared the open-field life of the camp, and for his action and daring in matters of war—not only among the soldiers serving with him, but among all the rest of the Peloponnesians as well.

For it would not be easy to find a king more abundantly furnished by nature with the resources needed for acquiring power.

He possessed, indeed, a keen intelligence, a strong memory, and an exceptional charm, and on top of these a kingly bearing and authority—and, most important of all, capacity and boldness in war.

But what it was that in the end overcame all these qualities, and turned a king of fine natural gifts into a savage tyrant, is not easy to explain in a few words. For that reason it will be more fitting to examine and work through these questions at some other occasion than the present one.

Philip, having broken camp at Olympia, took the road toward Pharae and came to Telphusa, and from there to Heraea. There he sold off the plunder as spoils, and set about repairing the bridge over the Alpheus, intending to make his invasion of Triphylia by that route.

At about this same time Dorimachus, the general of the Aetolians, in response to the appeal of the Eleans for help against the ravaging of their land, sent them six hundred Aetolians under the general Phillidas.

When Phillidas arrived in Elis, he took over the Elean mercenaries, who numbered about five hundred, and a thousand citizen soldiers, and along with these the Tarentine cavalry, and came to the relief of Triphylia.

This district has received its name from Triphylus, one of the sons of Arcas. It lies in the Peloponnese along the sea, between the territories of Elis and Messenia, faces toward the Libyan Sea, and forms the farthest edge of Arcadia in the direction of the winter sunset (the southwest).

It contains the following cities: Samicum, Lepreum, Hypana, Typaneae, Pyrgos, Aepium, Bolax, Stylangium, and Phrixa.

A short time before this the Eleans had gained control of these and had also annexed the city of the Aliphereans, which had originally belonged to Arcadia [and Megalopolis], after Lydiadas the Megalopolitan, during his tyranny, had handed it over to the Eleans in an exchange to serve certain private purposes of his own.

## Book 4 Chapter 78: Continued Success of Philip

Phillidas, however, sent the Eleans off to Lepreum and the mercenaries to Aliphera, while he himself, keeping the Aetolians with him at Typaneae, waited to see what would come of it.

The king, meanwhile, having set aside his baggage train and crossed the river Alpheus by the bridge—the river that flows right past the city of the Heraeans—

came to Aliphera. This town lies on a hill that is sheer on every side, with an ascent of more than ten stades, and it has a citadel on the very summit of the whole hill, together with a bronze statue of Athena, outstanding in beauty and size.

As for this statue—from what intention or with what funding the work was first undertaken—it happens to be a matter of dispute even among the local people, for it cannot be clearly established either from where or by whom it was dedicated.

The finished work of art, however, is acknowledged by everyone to be among the most magnificent and skillfully executed of works, made by Hypatodorus and Sostratus.

Be that as it may, when a clear and bright day had dawned, the king at first light drew up at several points the men carrying the scaling ladders, with the supporting detachments of mercenaries stationed in front of them,

and, posting the Macedonians divided up behind each of these forces, gave orders to all, the moment the sun came up, to advance against the hill.

As the Macedonians carried out the order with spirit and to terrifying effect, it came about that the Aliphereans kept rushing and running together against them to whatever spots they saw the Macedonians most closely approaching.

At this moment the king himself, with the most suitable men, climbed up unnoticed by way of certain cliffs to the suburb of the citadel.

When the signal had been given, all at once set their ladders against the walls and made their attempt on the city.

The king was the first to seize the suburb of the citadel, finding it deserted. As this was being set ablaze, the men defending the walls, foreseeing what was about to happen and terrified that, if the citadel were seized first, they would be robbed of their last hope as well, abandoned the walls and rushed to flee toward the acropolis.

When this happened, the Macedonians at once gained control of both the walls and the city.

After this the men on the citadel sent envoys to Philip, and he, granting them safe conduct, took possession of it too by agreement.

## Book 4 Chapter 79: Surrender of Typanae and Phigalia

When these things had been accomplished, all the people throughout Triphylia were struck with alarm and began to deliberate about their own safety and that of their respective home cities.

Phillidas, for his part, abandoned Typanae—after first plundering some of the houses there—and withdrew to Lepreum.

This, indeed, was the kind of reward that the allies of the Aetolians regularly received at that time: not only to be openly deserted at the moments of greatest need, but, on top of that, to be plundered or betrayed and so to suffer at the hands of their own allies precisely what is the due lot of those defeated by an enemy.

The people of Typanae handed their city over to Philip, and the inhabitants of Hypana did much the same.

At the same time, the people of Phigalia—hearing of what had happened throughout Triphylia and being discontented with the alliance with the Aetolians—seized, in arms, the area around the polemarchium.

The Aetolian pirates who were lingering in this city for the sake of the plunder to be had from Messenia were at first able to lay hands on the Phigalians and bully them;

but when they saw the citizens gathering with one accord to defend themselves, they gave up the attempt, came to terms, took up their own belongings, and departed from the city.

The Phigalians then sent an embassy to Philip and put themselves and their city into his hands.

## Book 4 Chapter 80: The People of Lepreum Evict the Garrisons

While these affairs were still in progress, the people of Lepreum seized a certain quarter of their city and demanded that the Eleans, the Aetolians, and likewise the troops from the Lacedaemonians evacuate both the citadel and the city—for a relief force had come to them from the Lacedaemonians as well.

At first Phillidas and his men paid no heed but stayed put, expecting to overawe those in the city;

but when the king dispatched Taurion with soldiers to Phigalia and himself advanced toward Lepreum, drawing close to the city, Phillidas and his men, realizing the situation, were cowed, while the Lepreates were further emboldened in their resolve.

It was indeed a fine feat that the Lepreates accomplished: although there were within the walls a thousand Eleans, a thousand Aetolians together with the pirates, five hundred mercenaries, and two hundred Lacedaemonians, and although the citadel was held by these troops, they nonetheless made a stand for their own homeland and refused to give up their hopes.

Phillidas, seeing the Lepreates holding their ground like men and the Macedonians drawing near, withdrew from the city along with the Eleans and the troops from the Lacedaemonians.

So the Cretans sent by the Spartans returned home through Messenia, while Phillidas and his men made their retreat in the direction of Samicum. The mass of the Lepreates,

now in control of their homeland, sent out ambassadors to put their city into Philip's hands.

The king, on hearing what had happened, sent the rest of his force on to Lepreum, but took the peltasts and the light-armed troops and led them himself, eager to catch up with Phillidas and his men.

He overtook them and gained possession of all their baggage, but Phillidas and his men hurried on and managed to slip into Samicum.

Encamping before the place and bringing up the rest of his force from Lepreum, he made it appear to those inside that he intended to besiege the position.

The Aetolians, together with the Eleans, having nothing ready for withstanding a siege except their bare hands, were dismayed at their predicament and entered into talks with Philip about their safety.

Having obtained permission to march out under arms, they set off for Elis; and the king at once became master of Samicum.

After this, when the other towns also came over to him bearing the suppliant's olive branch, he took over Phrixa, Stylangium, Aepium, Bolax, Pyrgos, and Epitalium.

Having accomplished all this, he returned again to Lepreum, having brought all of Triphylia under his control in six days.

After exhorting the Lepreates as the occasion required and installing a garrison in the citadel, he set out with his army for Heraea, leaving Ladicus the Acarnanian as overseer of Triphylia.

On reaching the aforementioned city, he distributed all the spoils, then took up his baggage and, leaving Heraea, arrived at the height of winter at Megalopolis.

## Book 4 Chapter 81: Chilon's Fruitless Attempts In Sparta

At about this same time, while Philip was occupied with affairs in Triphylia, Chilon the Lacedaemonian—holding that the kingship belonged to him by right of birth, and bitterly resenting the slight that the Ephors had shown him in the decision regarding the kingship, when they awarded it to Lycurgus—set out to overturn the established order.

He reasoned that if he followed the same path as Cleomenes and held out to the common people the hope of land allotments and a redistribution of property, the masses would quickly fall in behind him; and so he launched into the enterprise.

Having conferred about the matter with his friends and won as many as two hundred men to share in the venture, he set about carrying his plan to completion.

But seeing that the greatest obstacle to his design was Lycurgus and the Ephors who had conferred the crown upon him, he directed his first attack against these men.

He caught the Ephors at dinner and slaughtered every one of them on the spot—Fortune thus exacting from them the penalty they had earned; for whether one considers the man at whose hands, or the man on whose account, they suffered this fate, one would have to admit that they suffered it justly.

After finishing with these men, Chilon went to the house of Lycurgus and found him inside, but was unable to lay hold of him.

For Lycurgus was smuggled out by some of his neighbors and slipped away unnoticed. He then withdrew by back-country tracks to the place in Tripolis called Pellene.

Chilon, having been foiled in the most crucial point of his attempt, was thoroughly dispirited, yet he was forced nonetheless to press on with what he had begun.

So he burst into the marketplace, laid violent hands on his enemies, appealed to his kinsmen and friends, and held out to the rest the hopes just described.

But when no one paid him any heed, and the people instead began to band together against him, he grasped what was happening, slipped away in secret, and making his way across the country arrived alone in Achaia, an exile. As for the Lacedaemonians,

terrified at the approach of Philip, they cleared away everything from the countryside, and after demolishing the Athenaeum of the Megalopolitans, abandoned it.

So then the Lacedaemonians—

who from the time of Lycurgus's legislation had enjoyed a most admirable constitution and held very great power down to the battle of Leuctra, but after whom Fortune turned against them, their political condition steadily and ever more sliding toward the worse—

in the end experienced a great many hardships and civil factions, struggled through countless redistributions of land and banishments, and tasted the most bitter servitude down to the tyranny of Nabis; they who in former days could scarcely bear to hear even the word "tyrant."

Now the ancient history of Sparta, and most of its later history, has been recounted by many writers on both sides of the question. But the clearest part of it begins from the point at which Cleomenes utterly abolished the ancestral constitution; and this I shall relate at the appropriate moments as they arise.

## Book 4 Chapter 82: Intrigue of Apelles Against Aratus

Philip, breaking camp from Megalopolis and marching by way of Tegea, arrived at Argos, where he spent the remainder of the winter, having won admiration beyond his years both for his general conduct and for his achievements in the campaigns described above.

Apelles, however, even so did not abandon his scheme, but was bent on bringing the Achaeans gradually under the yoke.

Seeing that Aratus and his circle stood in the way of such a design, and that Philip listened to them—and especially to the elder Aratus, both because of his earlier connection with Antigonus and because he wielded the greatest influence among the Achaeans, and above all because of the man's resourcefulness and good sense—he set about working against them and scheming in something like the following way.

Investigating who the political opponents of Aratus were, he drew each of them out from their cities, and once he had them in his grasp he flattered them and urged them into friendship with himself.

He also presented them to Philip, pointing out to him at every turn that, if he followed Aratus, he would have to deal with the Achaeans according to the written terms of the alliance; but that if he listened to Apelles and took on friends of this sort, he would be able to handle all the Peloponnesians as he pleased.

He also at once threw himself into the matter of the elections, wanting to secure the office of Strategus for one of his own party and to drive Aratus and his people out of their position of power.

For these reasons he persuaded Philip to come to the Achaean elections at Aegium, on the pretext that he was at the same time making his way toward Elis.

The king having agreed, Apelles was present himself at the critical moment, and partly by entreaty, partly by intimidation, he barely managed it—but in the end he prevailed in having Eperatus of Pharae made Strategus, and in having Timoxenus, the candidate put forward by Aratus and his circle, rejected.

## Book 4 Chapter 83: Philip Captures the Wall

After this the king broke camp and, marching by way of Patrae and Dyme, came to the fortress called the Wall, which lies on the frontier of the territory of Dyme; this had been seized a short time before, as I mentioned earlier, by Euripidas and his men.

Eager to recover it for the Dymaeans by any means, he encamped before it with his whole army.

The Elean garrison, struck with alarm, surrendered the fortress to Philip—a place not large, but exceptionally well fortified:

for its circumference was no more than a stade and a half, while the height of its wall was nowhere less than thirty cubits.

Having handed it over to the Dymaeans, he advanced and ravaged the territory of the Eleans; and after devastating it and carrying off a great quantity of plunder, he returned with his army to Dyme.

## Book 4 Chapter 84: Aratus Defends Himself

Meanwhile Apelles, supposing that he had accomplished part of his purpose now that the Achaean Strategus had been installed through his own efforts, again set to work against Aratus and his circle, wishing to detach Philip entirely from his friendship with them. He set about fabricating his slander by means of a contrivance of the following kind.

Amphidamus, the Strategus of the Eleans, who had been captured at Thalamae together with those who had taken refuge there—as we related earlier concerning these events—when he arrived at Olympia, brought along with the other prisoners, made urgent efforts through certain intermediaries to obtain an interview with the king.

Having secured this, he stated that he was able to bring the Eleans over into friendship and alliance with the king. Philip, convinced, sent Amphidamus off without ransom,

instructing him to promise the Eleans that, if they chose friendship with him, he would return all their captives without ransom, would himself guarantee the security of their territory against every outside attack,

and besides this would preserve them free, ungarrisoned, exempt from tribute, and governed by their own institutions.

The Eleans, however, when they had heard these terms, paid them no heed, attractive and substantial though the offers seemed to be.

Apelles then, fashioning his slander out of this affair, brought it before Philip, claiming that Aratus and his circle did not bear genuine friendship toward the Macedonians, nor were they truly well disposed toward the king; for it was they who had been responsible even now for the estrangement of the Eleans.

He alleged that at the very time when the king dispatched Amphidamus from Olympia to Elis, these men had taken the fellow aside privately and worked on him, telling him that it was in no way to the advantage of the Peloponnesians for Philip to become master of the Eleans;

and that for this reason the Eleans had spurned all the offers made to them, choosing instead to keep up their friendship with the Aetolians and to persist in their war against the Macedonians.

## Book 4 Chapter 85: Apelles Repeats his Accusation to Aratus Before Philip

At first Philip, having accepted these arguments, ordered that Aratus and his party be summoned, and that Apelles state these same charges in their presence.

When they had arrived, Apelles repeated what he had said before, boldly and in a menacing manner; and while the king still kept silent, he added something further of this kind:

"Since the king finds you, Aratus, so ungrateful and so utterly unreasonable, he has resolved to call the Achaeans together, to render an account of these matters before them, and then to withdraw again to Macedonia."

But the elder Aratus took him up and urged Philip, as a general principle, never to believe anything that was said hastily or without examination,

and that whenever some report reached him against one of his friends or allies, he should make a more careful investigation before accepting the slander; for such conduct was both kingly and advantageous in every respect.

Therefore he now demanded that, concerning the things alleged by Apelles, the king should summon those who had heard them, bring forward into the open the man who had reported them to him, and leave nothing undone of what was possible toward learning the truth, before making any statement about these matters to the Achaeans.

## Book 4 Chapter 86: Aratus is Cleared

The king was satisfied with what was said, and declared that he would not treat the matter lightly but would investigate it; and so for the time being the meeting was dissolved.

In the following days Apelles brought forward no proof of his allegations, while for Aratus and his party there occurred a coincidence of the following kind.

For the Eleans, at the time when Philip was ravaging their country, grew suspicious of Amphidamus and set out to arrest him and, after binding him, to send him off to Aetolia.

But he, perceiving their intention beforehand, withdrew at first to Olympia, and afterward, learning that Philip was occupied at Dyme with the disposal of the spoils, hurried to make his way across to him.

So Aratus and his friends, when they heard that Amphidamus had been driven out of Elis and was present, were overjoyed—since they were conscious of no wrongdoing—and, approaching the king, urged that he ought to summon Amphidamus;

for that man, to whom the words had been spoken, would know best about the charges and would make the truth plain, since he had fled from his home on Philip's account and now placed his hopes of safety, for the present, in the king.

Persuaded by these arguments, the king sent for Amphidamus and found that the accusation was false.

For this reason, from that day onward, he received and esteemed Aratus ever more and more, while toward Apelles he grew more distrustful; yet, being still held in the grip of his thorough regard for him, he was forced to overlook much of what Apelles did.

## Book 4 Chapter 87: Further Intrigues of Apelles

But Apelles in no way abandoned his purpose. Rather, he set about slandering Taurion, who had been appointed over affairs in the Peloponnese—

not by faulting him, but by praising him and asserting that he was the right man to spend his time in the field at the king's side; his real aim being to have someone else, of his own choosing, set over affairs in the Peloponnese.

For this is indeed a novel kind of slander that has been devised: to injure those around one not by blaming them, but by praising them.

And such malice, spite, and cunning have been invented above all and first by those who pass their lives about the courts, springing from their jealousy and greed toward one another.

In the same way he also kept biting at Alexander, who had been appointed over the king's personal attendants, whenever he found an opportunity, wishing both to bring the guard of the king's person under his own control and, in general, to overturn the arrangement left in place by Antigonus.

For Antigonus, while he lived, presided nobly over both the kingdom and his son, and at his death he likewise made good provision beforehand, with respect to the future, for all his affairs.

For he left a will in which he wrote to the Macedonians about how matters had been administered, and likewise gave instructions about the future—how and through whom each thing was to be handled—wishing to leave no occasion for the courtiers to engage in rivalry and faction with one another.

Among those who were then on campaign with him, Apelles himself had been left as one of the guardians, Leontius over the peltasts, Megaleas over the secretariat, Taurion over affairs in the Peloponnese, and Alexander over the king's personal attendants.

Now Leontius and Megaleas he had wholly under his own control, but Alexander and Taurion he sought to remove from their posts, eager to manage both these offices and all the others through himself and his own friends.

And this he would easily have accomplished, had he not raised up Aratus as an adversary to himself; but as it was, he quickly got a taste of his own folly and greed.

For the very thing he set out to do against those around him, this he himself suffered, and that in a very short time indeed.

But how and in what manner this came to pass we shall postpone for the present, and shall bring this book to a close; in what follows we shall try to report clearly on each of these events.

Philip, having made the arrangements described above, returned to Argos, and there spent the winter quarters with his friends, while he dismissed his forces to Macedonia.
