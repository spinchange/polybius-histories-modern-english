# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXIII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 23 Chapter 1: Demetrius Before the Senate

Olympiad 149, year 1. Affairs in Italy.

In the 149th Olympiad an exceptionally large number of embassies gathered at Rome from Greece, more than had usually been seen before.

For Philip had been compelled, under the treaty, to submit disputes with his neighbors to legal arbitration; and when it became known that the Romans were ready to hear accusations against him and were taking care for the safety of those who had claims against the king,

all the peoples bordering on Macedonia appeared, some in a private capacity, some representing cities, and some entire ethnic groups, bringing charges against Philip.

Along with them came envoys from Eumenes, accompanied by Athenaeus, the king's brother, to accuse Philip concerning the cities in Thrace and the military aid sent to Prusias.

Demetrius, Philip's son, also arrived to answer all these complainants, bringing with him Apelles and Philocles, who were then considered the foremost of the king's friends.

Envoys came from the Spartans as well, one from each political group in the city.

The senate first summoned Athenaeus. It accepted the crown he brought, worth fifteen thousand gold staters, warmly praised Eumenes and his brothers in its reply, and urged them to remain steadfast in the same policy.

After that the magistrates brought in Demetrius and invited all Philip's accusers to speak, calling them forward one embassy at a time.

But the embassies were so numerous, and their appearances lasted for three days, that the senate found itself at a loss over how each individual case should be handled.

Envoys had come from Thessaly both collectively and separately from each city; so too from the Perrhaebians, the Athamanians, the Epirotes, and the Illyrians.

Some were contesting territory, some persons, some livestock, and others contracts or private wrongs committed against them.

Some said they could not obtain justice under the treaty because Philip blocked the legal process, while others attacked the judgments already given as corrupted, alleging that Philip had bribed the judges.

Altogether the accusations were so varied and tangled that they were difficult to sort out.

## Book 23 Chapter 2: Demetrius in the Senate

So the senate, unable itself to disentangle the issues and judging that Demetrius ought not to be required to answer each charge one by one,

especially since it felt kindly toward him and saw that he was still very young and far from equal to such a knot of complicated business,

and since above all it wanted not to hear Demetrius' advocacy but to learn Philip's real intentions,

released Demetrius from pleading the cases and instead asked the young man and the friends with him whether they had any written memorandum from the king about these matters.

Demetrius said that they did, and when he produced a small document the senate ordered him to state the substance of the written instructions, giving a summary of Philip's answer to each accusation.

In it Philip claimed either that he had done what the Romans ordered, or that, if something had not been done, the blame lay with his accusers.

To most of the responses he had also added phrases such as, "and yet Caecilius and the envoys with him did not treat us fairly in this matter," or again, "and yet we are suffering this unjustly."

Since this attitude of Philip's ran through all the replies, the senate, after hearing the men who had come, adopted a single decision covering the whole business.

Through the presiding magistrate it received Demetrius with great honor and kindness, addressed him in many encouraging words, and replied that it trusted that in all the matters mentioned by him, whether spoken aloud or read from the memorandum, what was right had been done or would be done.

But, it added, so that Philip too might understand that the senate was granting this favor for Demetrius' sake, it would send envoys to inspect whether everything was being carried out in accordance with the senate's wishes, and at the same time to make clear to the king that he was receiving this considerate treatment because of Demetrius.

That was how this affair was settled.

## Book 23 Chapter 3: Philip's Jealousy Roused

After these men, the envoys from Eumenes were admitted. They accused Philip both for the aid he had sent to Prusias and for his conduct in Thrace, saying that even now he had not withdrawn his garrisons from the cities there.

Philocles wished to reply on these points, since he had served as envoy to Prusias and had now been sent by Philip to the senate on this business.

But the senate listened only briefly and answered that, unless the envoys found everything in Thrace arranged according to the senate's decision and all the cities handed back under the control of Eumenes, it could no longer tolerate continued disobedience in that region.

Thus the growing tension between Philip and Rome was for the moment checked by Demetrius' presence.

Yet this embassy of the young prince contributed no little to the eventual ruin of the royal house.

For the senate, by placing its favor so openly in Demetrius, lifted the young man up, but deeply offended both Perseus and Philip, since it seemed that the Romans were showing kindness not for their sakes but for Demetrius'.

Titus too, by drawing the young man aside and advancing him through private conversations, greatly helped the same development.

He flattered Demetrius by suggesting that the Romans would soon establish him on the throne, and he irritated Philip's circle by writing that Demetrius should be sent again to Rome with as many of his friends as possible, and those of the greatest usefulness.

By taking advantage of these openings Perseus soon persuaded his father to consent to Demetrius' death.

I will explain in the following narrative how all this was managed in detail.

## Book 23 Chapter 4: The Spartan Ambassadors

After this the envoys from Sparta were called in. There were four different parties among them.

The group led by Lysis spoke on behalf of the old exiles, arguing that they should recover all the property from which they had originally gone into exile.

Areus and Alcibiades, by contrast, proposed that each exile should receive property worth one talent out of his former holdings, while the rest should be distributed among those worthy of citizenship.

Serippus argued that the existing constitutional arrangement should remain in place, the same one under which Sparta had once shared citizenship with the Achaeans.

Representatives of those who had been executed or banished under decrees of the Achaeans, headed by Chaeron, appeared as well, demanding permission to return and the restoration of the constitution in that form, ***

and they all addressed arguments against the Achaeans suited to their own particular causes.

Since the senate could not reach a clear judgment on the separate disputes, it appointed three men who had already before this been sent to the Peloponnese on these matters: Titus, Quintus Caecilius, and Appius Claudius.

After considerable discussion before them, all agreed that the exiles and the men condemned to death should be restored, and that Sparta should remain associated with the Achaean League.

But they disputed with one another over the property, namely whether each exile ought to select from his former estate goods worth a talent, ***

and so that they might not begin the whole controversy over again from the start, a written record was drawn up of the points agreed on, *** and all affixed their seals to it.

Titus and his colleagues, wishing also to bind the Achaeans into the agreement, summoned Xenarchus and those with him.

These men were then acting as envoys from the Achaeans, partly to renew the alliance and partly to attend to the Spartan dispute.

Unexpectedly asked whether they assented to what had been written, they found themselves in difficulty.

For they were displeased at the restoration of the exiles and the condemned, since this ran contrary to Achaean decrees and to the inscribed tablet; yet they approved the document as a whole because it stated that Sparta must remain united politically with the Achaeans.

So, torn between perplexity on the one side and awe of these Roman commissioners on the other, they too set their seal to it.

The senate then selected Quintus Marcius as envoy and sent him both to Macedonia and to the Peloponnese.

## Book 23 Chapter 5: Deinocrates of Messene

When Deinocrates the Messenian arrived at Rome as an envoy and found that Titus had been appointed by the senate as envoy to Prusias and Seleucus, he was delighted, thinking that Titus, both because of their friendship—

for the two had become close during the war in Laconia—and because of his quarrel with Philopoemen, would come to Greece and manage all Messenian affairs according to Deinocrates' own wishes.

So he put everything else aside, attached himself closely to Titus, and rested all his hopes on him.

This Deinocrates of Messene was by practice, and also by nature, a man fit for camp and court.

He gave the appearance of complete political ability, but the appearance was false and superficial.

In military matters, through readiness and boldness, he surpassed most men and was brilliant in personal danger.

In ordinary society too he was agreeable and easy in conversation, lively and urbane in company, and at the same time much given to love affairs.

But in public or political business—fixing his attention firmly, foreseeing the future with confidence, making preparations, and speaking effectively before a crowd—he was incapable from start to finish.

Even then, though he had set great evils in motion for his homeland, he thought he was doing nothing of real consequence. He went on living in the same way as before, foreseeing nothing, carried away by romance, drinking-parties, and entertainments day after day.

Titus, however, forced him to catch at least some brief glimpse of the seriousness of the crisis.

For once, seeing him at a drinking-party dancing in long robes, Titus said nothing at the time; but the next day, when Deinocrates met him and made some request concerning his country,

he replied, "Deinocrates, I will do everything I can. But I am amazed that you can dance after drinking, when you have set such serious troubles in motion among the Greeks."

At that moment he seems to have drawn himself together a little and to have learned that he was displaying habits and tastes ill suited to the situation.

Even so, he came to Greece with Titus convinced that, in a single stroke, Messenian affairs would be arranged according to his own wishes.

But Philopoemen and his circle clearly understood that Titus had received no instructions from the senate concerning Greek affairs, and so they remained quiet, simply waiting for his arrival.

After Titus landed at Naupactus he wrote to the Achaean general and the demiourgoi, ordering them to summon the Achaeans to an assembly.

They wrote back that they would do so if he first stated in writing what subjects he wished to discuss with the Achaeans, since the laws required magistrates to specify this.

As Titus did not dare to write that down, the hopes of Deinocrates and of the so-called old exiles—who had in fact only recently been driven out of Sparta—and in general the whole expectation raised by Titus' arrival, all collapsed in this way.

## Book 23 Chapter 6: Ambassadors from the Spartan Exiles

Affairs in Greece.

At about the same time envoys were sent to Rome by the Spartan exiles, among them Arcesilaus and Agesipolis, who, though still a boy, had once become king at Sparta.

Some pirates fell upon these men at sea and killed them,

but the others appointed with them made their way safely to Rome.

## Book 23 Chapter 7: Philip and Perseus are Jealous of Demetrius

Affairs in Macedonia.

When Demetrius returned from Rome to Macedonia bringing the Roman replies, in which the Romans placed all their favor and confidence in him and declared that whatever they had done or would do was being done for his sake,

the Macedonians welcomed Demetrius warmly, believing that they had been released from great fears and dangers—

for they expected the war from Rome to break upon them very soon because of Philip's obstructive conduct—

but Philip and Perseus were not pleased by what was happening. They did not like it that the Romans seemed to pay no attention to them, but to ascribe all their goodwill to Demetrius.

Philip concealed his displeasure, but Perseus, who was far inferior to his brother not only in his goodwill toward Rome but in almost every other respect as well, both by nature and by training, took it badly.

What weighed on him most was his fear for the succession, lest, despite being the elder, he should be pushed aside for the reasons already mentioned.

Therefore he began to corrupt the friends of Demetrius ***.

## Book 23 Chapter 8: Death of Demetrius

When Quintus Marcius and his colleagues came on embassy to Macedonia, Philip withdrew entirely from the Greek cities in Thrace and removed his garrisons, though he did so in bitterness and with many groans.

He also set in order all the other matters on which the Romans were giving commands, wanting to show them no sign of estrangement while gaining time to turn back to preparations for war.

In pursuit of this design he led an army out against the barbarians.

Marching through the heart of Thrace, he invaded the lands of the Odrysae, the Bessi, and the Dentheletae.

When he reached the city called Philippopolis, the inhabitants fled up into the high ground, and he seized the city in a sudden attack.

After this he overran the whole plain, plundering some places and taking pledges from others, and then returned, leaving a garrison in Philippopolis.

But after some time that garrison was expelled by the Odrysae, who broke the pledges they had given the king.

## Book 23 Chapter 9: The Senate Refuses to Help Either Messene or Achaia

Olympiad 149, year 2. Affairs in Italy.

In the second year the senate, after embassies had arrived from Eumenes, Pharnaces, Philip, the Achaean nation, the Spartans who had been expelled from their city, and also those who still held Sparta, dealt with their business.

Envoys from Rhodes also came on behalf of the misfortune that had befallen the people of Sinope.

To them, and also to the envoys of Eumenes and Pharnaces, the senate replied that it would send representatives to inspect the situation at Sinope and the matters disputed between the kings.

Since Quintus Marcius had recently returned from Greece and had already made matters clear concerning Macedonia and the Peloponnese, the senate had no need of many more speeches.

So, after calling in the envoys from the Peloponnese and Macedonia, it listened to what they said, but based its replies and settlement of the issues not on the ambassadors' speeches, but on Marcius' report.

For with regard to King Philip, he had reported that Philip had indeed carried out the commands laid upon him, but had done everything resentfully, and that, if he got the chance, he would do anything against Rome.

Therefore the senate gave Philip's envoys a reply of this sort: it praised Philip for what he had done, but thought he must be careful from now on not to seem to act in any way contrary to Roman interests.

About Peloponnesian affairs Marcius had reported that the Achaeans did not want to refer anything to the senate, but carried themselves proudly and undertook to manage everything on their own.

He said that if the Romans would for the moment merely disregard their wishes and show even a slight sign of displeasure,

Sparta would quickly align itself with Messene, and then the Achaeans would come running to Rome with great eagerness.

Accordingly, to the Spartans under Serippus the senate replied—wanting to keep the city in suspense—that it had done everything it could for them, but did not at present think this matter belonged before itself.

And when the Achaeans asked either, if possible, for help under the alliance against the Messenians, or at least for precautions to prevent anyone from Italy from importing arms or grain into Messene, the senate paid attention to neither request.

Instead it answered that, even if the people of Sparta or Corinth or Argos should break away, the Achaeans should not be surprised if they were not conducting their leadership in a way acceptable to Rome.

By publishing this reply, which amounted to a public invitation to anyone wishing, under Roman protection, to revolt from the Achaean constitution, the senate then kept the envoys in Rome, waiting to see how matters at Messene would turn out for the Achaeans.

Such was the situation in Italy.

## Book 23 Chapter 10: Philip's Desperate Measures

Affairs in Macedonia.

At this time some dreadful beginning of troubles fell upon King Philip and upon all Macedonia, something well worth observing and remembering.

It was as though Fortune had resolved at the proper moment to exact from him punishment for all the impieties and crimes he had committed in the course of his life, and therefore had set before him avenging spirits, penalties, and the ghosts of those ruined through him.

Staying with him night and day, these torments punished him until life left him, so that everyone was compelled to admit the truth of the proverb that Justice has an eye, and that human beings should never despise it.

First they planted in him the idea that, if he meant to go to war with Rome, he should uproot the leading men from the most distinguished and coastal cities, together with their wives and children, and transfer them inland to the region then called Emathia, but formerly Paeonia,

and should fill those cities with Thracians and other barbarians, on the assumption that their loyalty would be firmer in a crisis.

As this was carried out and people were torn from their homes, there arose such grief and such an uproar that the whole country seemed like a land taken by the spear.

Curses and appeals to heaven against the king were uttered not only in secret now, but openly.

After this, wanting to leave nothing alien or hostile in the kingdom, he wrote to the officials set over the cities ordering them to search out the sons and daughters of the Macedonians he had put to death and place them under guard,

especially meaning those connected with Admetus, Pyrrhichus, Samus, and the others who had perished with them.

At the same time he included all the rest who had been deprived of life by royal command, quoting, it is said, this verse:

"A fool, who kills the father and leaves the sons behind."

Since most of these were prominent because of their fathers' rank, their misfortune too became conspicuous and pitiful to all.

Fortune introduced a third act into the drama at the same period, namely the matter of Philip's sons.

As the young men plotted against one another, and reports of this were brought before him, he was tortured by having to decide which of his sons he must become the killer of, and which one he had more reason to fear for the rest of his life, lest in old age he should suffer something similar himself.

With his mind in such distress and turmoil, who would not reasonably conclude that some divine wrath had descended upon his old age because of the lawless deeds of his earlier life?

This will become even clearer from what follows.

Philip king of the Macedonians, after killing many Macedonians, also took action against their sons, repeating, they say, the same verse:

"A fool, who kills the father and leaves the sons behind."

***

And because his soul was driven almost mad by these things, the quarrel between his sons was enflamed along with all the troubles already described, Fortune seeming intentionally to bring all these disasters onto the stage at one and the same time.

The Macedonians make offerings to Xanthus and carry out a purification with horses in full armor.

## Book 23 Chapter 11: Philip Addresses his Sons

"One must not merely read tragedies, myths, and histories, but also understand them and apply one's mind to this lesson.

In all of them one may see that brothers who fall into anger and rivalry against each other and let it run too far destroy not only themselves, but utterly ruin their households, their children, and their cities.

But all those who have had the moderation to cherish one another and to bear with each other's failings prove the preservers of all these things and live out their lives with the noblest reputation and glory.

I have often directed your attention to the kings at Sparta, telling you that they preserved for their fatherland the leadership of Greece for so long because, obeying the ephors as though they were parents, they endured sharing the kingship with one another.

But when they quarrelled and turned the government into monarchy, then they made Sparta taste every kind of evil.

And finally, as if showing you a visible example and placing it before your eyes, I continually pointed to Eumenes and Attalus,

who inherited a small and insignificant dominion and enlarged it until it was inferior to none, for no other reason than their harmony with one another, their agreement, and their ability to preserve mutual respect.

Yet, though you heard all this, you did not take it to heart; on the contrary, as it seems to me, you were sharpening your anger against each other."

## Book 23 Chapter 12: The Fall of Philopoemen

Affairs in Greece.

Polybius says that he rose and went on ahead, weighed down partly by sickness and partly by age, for he was in his seventieth year.

But mastering his weakness by force of his old habit, he came from Argos to Megalopolis in a single day. —

Philopoemen, general of the Achaeans, was captured by the Messenians and killed with poison, a man inferior to none of his predecessors in virtue, though weaker than Fortune, even though throughout his earlier life she had seemed to work with him.

It seems to me that the common proverb is right: a human being may meet with success, but to be successful to the very end is impossible.

Therefore we should call the men of old fortunate, not as though any of them enjoyed unbroken good fortune—for why should we vainly flatter Fortune with a false phrase?—

but rather those who have had her favorable for the longest part of life, and who, when she does change her mind, encounter only moderate disasters. —

... Lycortas, who was in no way inferior to him. —

Philopoemen, after seeking distinction continuously for forty years in a democratic and many-sided political system, completely escaped the envy of the masses in every respect,

though for the most part he conducted public affairs not by courting favor, but with frankness. And that is something one would rarely find. —

## Book 23 Chapter 13: Character of Hannibal

It is remarkable, and the strongest possible sign that this man was born to command and was far superior to others in practical ability,

that although he spent seventeen years in the field, passed through numerous barbarian peoples, and employed many foreign and differently-speaking men in enterprises that seemed desperate and astonishing, not one of those who once shared his plans and entrusted themselves to him ever plotted against him or deserted him.

## Book 23 Chapter 14: Character of P. Cornelius Scipio Africanus

Publius, after pursuing distinction in an aristocratic constitution, won such goodwill from the masses and such trust from the senate that,

when someone undertook to prosecute him before the people according to Roman custom and made many harsh and bitter accusations,

he came forward and said only that it was not fitting for the Roman people to listen to anyone accusing Publius Cornelius Scipio, the man through whom the accusers themselves possessed the freedom to speak.

On hearing this, the crowd at once broke up and all left the assembly, leaving the accuser there alone. —

On another occasion, when in the senate there was a need for money for an urgent public purpose, and the quaestor said that because of a certain law he would not open the treasury on that day, Scipio said that he himself would take the keys and open it,

for he himself had been the cause of the treasury's being shut at all.

Again, when someone in the senate asked him to render an account of the money he had received from Antiochus before the treaty for paying the army, he said that he had the ledger, but that he ought not to submit to an audit from anyone.

When the man kept pressing him and told him to produce it, Scipio ordered his brother to bring the document. When it had been brought, he held it out and tore it to pieces before everyone's eyes, telling the man who demanded the account to look for it among those scraps,

and then asked the others how it was that they wanted an accounting of how the three thousand talents had been spent and through whose hands,

but did not ask at all how the fifteen thousand talents they were receiving from Antiochus were entering Rome, through whose agency, or how they had become masters of Asia, Libya, and Iberia.

As a result, not only were all the others struck silent with amazement, but even the man who had demanded the account said no more.

Let this be said by us both for the sake of the glory of these departed men and to stir later generations toward noble actions. —

## Book 23 Chapter 15: Submission of the Messenians

It is not right to destroy the crops of those opposed to you. For Polybius says, "For my part I never agree with those who carry their anger against their own people so far that they not only strip their enemies of the year's produce,

but even destroy the trees and the built works, leaving no room for repentance.

Those who do such things seem to me gravely mistaken. For to the same degree that they think they are terrifying their enemies by ravaging the land and taking away not only present means of life but future hopes as well, to that same degree they make men savage and turn the anger of those who have once gone astray into something unchangeable." —

## Book 23 Chapter 16: Lycortas Defeats Messene

Lycortas, the general of the Achaeans, had broken the spirit of the Messenians by the war. *

The Messenians had long been terrified of their leaders, but now at last some of them dared to speak out, trusting in the enemy reserve force, and said that envoys should be sent to negotiate a settlement.

Deinocrates and his associates, no longer able to face the people because they were hard-pressed *** by the turn of events, gave way and withdrew to their own homes.

The majority, encouraged by the older men and especially by the envoys from Boeotia,

who had already come earlier about a settlement—Epaenetus and Apollodorus—and who happened opportunely to be in Messene at that moment, quickly appointed envoys and sent them out, begging pardon for what had been done.

The Achaean general took his colleagues with him, heard the envoys out, and said that there was only one possible reconciliation between the Messenians and the League:

they must immediately hand over to him those responsible for the revolt and for the murder of Philopoemen, submit all other matters to the judgment of the Achaeans, and admit a garrison at once into the citadel.

When these terms were reported to the crowd, those who had long been bitterly disposed against the men responsible for the war were eager to surrender and arrest them; and those who were persuaded that they would suffer nothing severe from the Achaeans readily agreed to entrust the rest of the matter to their decision.

In short, having no real choice in the present circumstances, they unanimously accepted the proposals.

The general therefore immediately took possession of the citadel and led the peltasts into it.

After that he took selected men from the camp, entered the city, gathered the populace together, and addressed them in language suitable to the crisis, promising that they would never regret the confidence they were placing in him.

The final decision on the whole issue he referred to the nation—for by what was almost a coincidence the Achaeans were again assembling at Megalopolis for the second synod—

but as for those charged with responsibility, all who had taken part in the immediate killing of Philopoemen he ordered to remove themselves from life at once *** —

## Book 23 Chapter 17: Arguments For and Against Admitting Sparta

The Messenians, by their own ignorance, had brought themselves to the very edge of ruin, but through the magnanimity of Lycortas and the Achaeans they were restored to their original position in the federal union.

At this same time Abia, Thuria, and Pharae separated themselves from Messene and, after each set up its own inscribed stele, joined the common federation independently.

When the Romans learned that affairs at Messene had turned out in the proper way for the Achaeans, they ignored their earlier reply and gave a different answer to the same envoys, making it clear that they had taken measures to prevent anyone from Italy from bringing either arms or grain into Messene.

From this it became obvious to everyone that they were very far from neglecting outside affairs unless absolutely necessary; quite the opposite, they were annoyed whenever everything was not referred to them and carried out according to their judgment.

When the envoys from Rome arrived in Sparta bringing this answer, the Achaean general, as soon as he had finished settling affairs at Messene, summoned the people to the city of the Sicyonians.

Once the Achaeans had assembled, he laid before them a proposal to admit Sparta into the federation,

saying that the Romans had declined the arbitration previously entrusted to them concerning that city, for they had now answered that affairs at Sparta were no concern of theirs,

while those who at present controlled Sparta wished to take part in the federation.

Therefore, he urged them to admit the city, for this would be advantageous in two ways. First, they would be receiving men who had preserved their loyalty to the League.

Second, they would not have as partners in the constitution those old exiles who had behaved ungratefully and impiously toward them; rather, by confirming the policy of those who had excluded such men from the city, they would both uphold the decision already taken and pay them the gratitude they deserved, with the favor of the gods.

With these and similar arguments Lycortas urged the Achaeans to receive Sparta.

But Diophanes and certain others tried to support the exiles, urging the Achaeans not to join in crushing men who had been banished, and not, because of a handful of people, to strengthen those who had impiously and unlawfully driven them from their fatherland.

## Book 23 Chapter 18: Sparta Included In the League

Such were the arguments made by both sides. After hearing them both, the Achaeans decided to admit the city; and after an inscribed stele had been drawn up, Sparta was politically united with the Achaeans,

the men then in possession of the city agreeing to receive such of the old exiles as were not thought to have committed any outrageous act against the Achaean nation.

After ratifying these measures, the Achaeans sent envoys to Rome, headed by Bippus of Argos, to explain everything to the senate.

The Spartans likewise appointed envoys led by Chaeron.

And the exiles sent Cletis and Diactorius to appear in the senate in opposition to the envoys from the Achaeans.
