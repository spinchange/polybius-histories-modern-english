# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXI

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 21 Chapter 1: Embassy from Sparta

Olympiad 147, year 2. Affairs in Italy.

At this same time the embassy that the Spartans had sent to Rome returned with its hopes disappointed.

Their mission concerned the hostages and the villages.

The senate said that it would give instructions about the villages to the envoys it would send itself, but that it still wished to deliberate further about the hostages.

As for the old exiles, it said it was amazed that they had not yet been brought home, now that Sparta had been freed.

## Book 21 Chapter 2: Embassy from Philip

When news of the Roman victory in the naval battle had just reached Rome, they first proclaimed a nine-day public thanksgiving.

That is, the whole people were to suspend ordinary business and offer thank-offerings to the gods for their success.

After this they brought before the senate the envoys from the Aetolians and those sent by Manius.

When both sides had spoken at length, the senate resolved to offer the Aetolians two alternatives: either they were to leave all matters concerning themselves to Roman decision, or they were to pay a thousand talents at once and treat the same people as friends and enemies that Rome did.

But when the Aetolians asked the senate to state explicitly what issues they must submit for Roman decision, the senate refused to define them. That is why the war with the Aetolians continued for them alone.

## Book 21 Chapter 3: The Scipios In Greece

At about the same time the senate also dealt with the envoys sent by Philip.

They had come to explain the goodwill and zeal the king had shown toward the Romans in the war against Antiochus.

After hearing them, the senate immediately released his son Demetrius from hostage status, and also promised to remit the tribute, provided that Philip continued faithful in the present circumstances.

In the same way they also released the Spartan hostages, except Armenas the son of Nabis; he later died of illness.

## Book 21 Chapter 3b

Affairs in Greece.

An embassy came into Achaea from King Eumenes asking for alliance.

The Achaeans assembled in public meeting, ratified the alliance, and sent out young troops—one thousand infantry and one hundred cavalry—under the command of Diophanes of Megalopolis.

## Book 21 Chapter 4: The Athenians Intercede for the Aetolians

While Manius, the Roman general, was besieging the Amphissians, the Athenian people learned both of the distress of Amphissa and of Publius's presence, and at this moment sent envoys headed by Echedemus.

Their instructions were, first, to greet Lucius and Publius, and at the same time to test whether a settlement with the Aetolians might be arranged.

When they arrived, Publius gladly welcomed them and treated them with great courtesy, seeing that they could be useful for the plans before him.

For he wanted to put Aetolian affairs in good order if possible; but if the Aetolians would not cooperate, he had fully resolved to leave that matter aside and cross into Asia.

He clearly understood that the true end of the war and of the whole enterprise was not the reduction of the Aetolian nation, but the defeat of Antiochus and mastery of Asia after victory.

Therefore, the moment the Athenians mentioned a settlement, he readily accepted what they said and told them to approach the Aetolians in the same spirit.

Echedemus and his colleagues first sent men ahead and then went themselves to Hypata, where they discussed peace with the Aetolian magistrates.

The Aetolians also agreed readily, and representatives were appointed to meet the Romans.

When these men came to Publius and found him encamped sixty stades from Amphissa, they spoke at length, reminding him of the kindnesses the Aetolians had shown to Rome in the past.

Publius spoke in an even gentler and more humane way. He cited what he had done in Spain and in Libya, explained how he had treated those in those regions who put their trust in him, and concluded by saying that they ought to place themselves in his hands and trust him.

At first all who were present became hopeful that the settlement would immediately be brought to completion.

But when the Aetolians asked on what terms they were to make peace, Lucius explained that two choices lay before them.

They must either submit everything concerning themselves to Roman decision, or pay a thousand talents at once and accept the same friends and enemies as Rome.

At this the Aetolians present were deeply troubled, because the decision was so unlike the previous tone of the conversation; still, they said they would report back to the Aetolians on the demands laid upon them.

## Book 21 Chapter 5: Truce With the Aetolians

So these men went back to deliberate on what had been proposed.

Echedemus and his colleagues met with the Apocleti and joined in their discussions.

One of the Roman demands was impossible because of the sheer amount of money involved; the other was frightening because they had already once been deceived in connection with surrender, when they had very nearly fallen into chains.

Therefore, perplexed and distressed, they again sent the same envoys to ask either that some part of the money be remitted, so that they could pay it, or that political leaders and women be excluded from the terms of submission.

When the envoys met Publius and his circle, they explained these decisions.

Lucius said that the authority granted him by the senate extended only to the terms he had just stated. So the envoys returned once more.

Echedemus and his colleagues then followed them to Hypata and advised the Aetolians that, since peace was for the moment blocked, they should ask for a truce and thus win a postponement of their present miseries, and then send an embassy to the senate.

If they obtained what they requested, well and good; if not, they must wait on circumstances.

For their position could hardly become worse than it already was, while for many reasons it might yet become better.

Since Echedemus's advice seemed sound, the Aetolians decided to send an embassy to ask for a truce.

When they reached Lucius, they begged that for the present a six-month truce be granted, so that they might send envoys to the senate.

Publius, who had long been eager for the operations in Asia, quickly persuaded his brother to grant what they asked.

The agreements were drawn up in writing. Manius then raised the siege, handed over the whole army and the supplies to Lucius, and at once departed for Rome with his military tribunes.

## Book 21 Chapter 6: Asia: Factions at Phocaea

Affairs in Asia.

At Phocaea there was civil strife, partly because the Romans left on board the ships were quartered there, and partly because the people resented the orders laid upon them.

At the same time the magistrates of Phocaea, fearing both the impulse of the crowd because of the grain shortage and the agitation of the supporters of Antiochus, sent envoys to Seleucus, who was near their frontier.

They asked him not to approach the city, because they intended to remain quiet, wait for the issue of the whole conflict, and afterward obey whatever settlement was made.

Among the envoys, Aristarchus, Cassander, and Rhodon were personally attached to Seleucus and favored that policy, while Hegias and Gelias opposed it and leaned toward Rome.

When they met him, Seleucus at once gave his attention to Aristarchus and his party, while he ignored Hegias and his associates.

But when he learned how the crowd felt and how scarce grain was, he dropped further discussion and audience with the envoys and advanced on the city.

Then two Galli came out carrying their sacred emblems and breast-images and begged him not to take any irreparable step against the city.

## Book 21 Chapter 7: The Rhodian Firing Apparatus

The fire-throwing device used by Pausistratus, the Rhodian admiral, was a brazier.

On each side of the prow two hooked fittings were placed along the inner faces of the ship's sides, and into these were set poles projecting outward over the sea.

From the end of these the brazier, full of fire, was suspended by an iron chain.

In this way, when the ship charged or came alongside an enemy vessel, the fire could be flung onto the hostile ship, while remaining far from their own because of the outward slant of the poles.

It was also thought that Pamphilidas, the Rhodian admiral, was better suited than Pausistratus for every sort of crisis, since he was by nature deeper and steadier rather than simply bolder.

Most people, after all, do not form their judgments from reasoned principle, but from whatever result has happened.

So although they had only just chosen Pausistratus for command precisely because he had drive and daring, they immediately swung to the opposite opinion when fortune turned against him.

## Book 21 Chapter 8: The Aetolian Truce Announced

At this time letters reached Samos for Lucius and for Eumenes, both from Lucius the consul and from Publius Scipio,

announcing the arrangements made with the Aetolians about the truce and the march of the land army toward the Hellespont.

The same facts were likewise made known to Antiochus and Seleucus by the Aetolians.

## Book 21 Chapter 9: Antiochus At Pergamum

Diophanes of Megalopolis had great practical experience in warfare, because during the long war against Nabis the Megalopolitans, being a neighboring people, had spent all that time serving under Philopoemen and had thus gained real training in military action.

Besides this, the man was powerful and impressive both in appearance and in bodily vigor.

And most important of all, he was a brave soldier in war and exceptionally skillful in the use of arms.

## Book 21 Chapter 10: Antiochus Proposes Peace

King Antiochus invaded the territory of Pergamum; but when he learned that King Eumenes was present and saw that not only the naval but also the land forces were coming against him, he began to consider opening negotiations for peace at once with the Romans, Eumenes, and the Rhodians.

So he moved with his whole army to Elaea, occupied a hill opposite the city, stationed his infantry on it, and encamped his cavalry—more than six thousand strong—close by the city itself.

He himself took position between these forces and sent messengers into the city to Lucius about a settlement.

The Roman general then called together the Rhodians and Eumenes and asked each to state plainly what seemed best in the present situation.

Eudamus and Pamphilidas were not opposed to a settlement, but the king said that peace was at present neither honorable nor practicable.

“How,” he said, “could the outcome be honorable if we make peace while shut up behind walls?

And indeed how can it be practicable now? Without the consul being present, how could any agreed terms be valid without his approval?

Besides, even if some sign of agreement were reached with Antiochus, neither the fleet nor the army could properly go home before the people and senate had ratified the terms.

We would then have nothing left but to winter here waiting for their decision, doing nothing while consuming the supplies and resources of our allies.

Then, if the senate should refuse the settlement, we would have to begin the war afresh from the beginning, after letting the present opportunity pass—an opportunity in which, if the gods are willing, we may bring the whole matter to an end.”

Such was Eumenes' view. Lucius approved the advice and answered Antiochus's envoys that no settlement could be made before the proconsul arrived.

When Antiochus's people heard this, they at once began to ravage the territory of Elaea.

After this Seleucus remained in that district, while Antiochus continued his march and entered the plain called Thebe.

There, having moved into a rich and prosperous region full of resources, he filled his army with every kind of plunder.

## Book 21 Chapter 11: Prusias Refuses To Help Antiochus

When King Antiochus returned to Sardis from the expedition just mentioned, he kept sending messages to Prusias, urging him to join his alliance.

In earlier times Prusias had not been unwilling to make common cause with Antiochus, because he was very much afraid that the Romans were crossing into Asia in order to overthrow all monarchs.

But a letter reached him from the brothers Lucius and Publius. When he received it and read it, his thinking changed greatly, and he gained a reasonable foresight of what was coming.

For the men around Publius had written with great clarity, and had added many proofs to make their case convincing.

They did not merely defend their own policy,

but that of the Roman people as a whole, showing that they had not taken kingdoms away from the kings who originally held them; rather, in some cases they had themselves established rulers, and in others had enlarged their power and made their dominions many times greater.

They cited Indibilis and Colichas in Spain, Massanissa in Libya, and Pleuratus in Illyria.

All of these, they said, had been raised by Rome from small and insignificant dynasts into acknowledged kings.

In the same way they mentioned Philip and Nabis in Greece. Though they had defeated Philip in war and reduced him to hostages and tribute, after receiving from him only a slight proof of goodwill they had restored his son and the young men who had been hostages with him, released him from tribute, and returned many cities taken in war.

And although they could have removed Nabis altogether, they had not done so, but had spared him, tyrant though he was, after receiving the customary guarantees.

In view of all this they urged Prusias by letter not to be afraid for his throne, but confidently to choose the Roman side, since he would never regret such a decision.

After hearing these arguments Prusias changed his mind. And when envoys headed by Gaius Livius also arrived and met with him, he gave up all expectation of support from Antiochus.

Antiochus, having failed in this hope, came to Ephesus; and reflecting that only by mastering the sea could he prevent the crossing of the land army and in general keep the war away from Asia, he resolved to fight a naval battle and decide the issue by a contest at sea.

## Book 21 Chapter 12: Pirates

Polybius says that when the pirates caught sight of the Roman ships sailing toward them, they suddenly turned and made their retreat.

## Book 21 Chapter 13: Antiochus Sends an Envoy To Discuss Peace

After his defeat in the sea battle Antiochus stayed at Sardis, wasting time and delaying in the larger conduct of the war.

As soon as he heard that the enemy had crossed over, his spirit was crushed and he lost hope; so he decided to send to Lucius and Publius to negotiate a settlement.

He chose Heracleides of Byzantium and sent him out with instructions to cede the cities of Lampsacus and Smyrna, and also Alexandria, from which the war had originally taken its beginning.

Likewise, if the Romans wished to take away any other cities in Aeolis and Ionia that had chosen their side in the present war, he was prepared for that too.

In addition, he would pay half the expense they had incurred in the war against him.

These were the instructions Heracleides had for the public audience; but for Publius in private he had other proposals, which I shall explain in due course.

When this envoy reached the Hellespont and found the Romans still waiting in the camp where they had first encamped after crossing,

he was at first delighted, thinking that their remaining where they were and not yet moving on would help his negotiations.

But when he learned that Publius was still on the other side, he was troubled, because the greatest weight in the matter rested on Publius's judgment.

The reason why the army remained in its first camp and why Publius was apart from the forces was that he was one of the Salii.

As I said in my work on the constitution, this is one of the three priestly bodies by which Rome performs its most notable sacrifices to the gods.

During the season of that sacrifice, the law requires that these Salii not change their location for thirty days, wherever they may happen to be caught.

This happened to Publius then. Just as the army was about to cross over, that sacred period overtook him, so that he could not change place.

As a result, Scipio was separated from the army and remained in Europe, while the forces that had crossed stayed where they were and could do nothing further, awaiting the arrival of the man just mentioned.

## Book 21 Chapter 14: Antiochus Tries To Negotiate

After some days, when Publius had arrived, Heracleides was summoned before the council and spoke on the instructions he carried.

He said that Antiochus would withdraw from Lampsacus and Smyrna and also from the city of Alexandria, as well as from any cities in Aeolis and Ionia that had chosen the Roman side.

In addition, he would undertake to pay half the expense they had incurred in the present war.

He also said much else on the same subject, urging the Romans not to test fortune too far, since they were men, and not to make the scope of their power boundless, but to set limits to it—if possible, the limits of Europe.

Even that, he said, would be a great and astonishing dominion, since none of their predecessors had attained it.

But if they were determined to lay hold of some part of Asia as well, let them define what parts those were, and the king would go to the utmost of his power to satisfy them.

After this had been said, the council decided that the general should reply that Antiochus ought to pay not half the cost of the war but all of it, since the war had arisen originally not because of Rome but because of him.

As for the cities, he must not free only those in Aeolis and Ionia, but give up all his dominion on this side of Taurus.

When the envoy heard this answer from the council, and saw that the demands went far beyond his instructions, he made no further attempt in the public meeting but instead devoted himself to courting Publius privately.

## Book 21 Chapter 15: Scipio Scorns Antiochus's Secret Proposal

When he found a suitable opportunity, he spoke of the proposals entrusted to him.

First, the king would restore Publius's son without ransom.

For at the beginning of the war Scipio's son had fallen into the hands of Antiochus's people.

Second, the king was ready even now to give whatever sum of money Publius might name, and after that to make the resources of his kingdom common with him, if only he would support the peace terms proposed by the king.

Publius said that he accepted the offer concerning his son and would be deeply grateful if Antiochus fulfilled that promise.

But as for the rest, he said the king entirely failed to understand his own interest, not only in this private approach to him, but in the public negotiations as well.

If Antiochus had made these offers while still master of Lysimacheia and of the entrance into the Chersonese, he would quickly have obtained his aim.

Likewise, even after giving up those places, if he had come to the Hellespont with his army, clearly prepared to prevent our crossing, and had then negotiated on these same points, he might still have achieved what he wanted.

But now, after allowing our forces to set foot in Asia and accepting not only the bridle but even the rider, he comes to propose equal terms of peace. Naturally he must fail and be disappointed in his hopes.

Therefore Publius advised him to take better counsel about the present and to look truly at the situation.

In return for the king's promise about his son, he said he would give advice worth the favor offered: the king should yield on every point, but under no circumstances fight the Romans.

After hearing this, Heracleides returned and reported the details to the king.

Antiochus, believing that no harsher terms could be laid on him if he were defeated in battle than those already being demanded, gave up the business of negotiation and made every kind of preparation for a decisive struggle.

## Book 21 Chapter 16: Zeuxis and Antipater Sent to Negotiate Peace

After the victory the Romans took possession of Sardis and its citadel, and then Musaeus came from Antiochus under flag of truce.

Publius and his associates received him graciously, and he said that Antiochus wished to send envoys to discuss the whole settlement.

Accordingly he asked that safe conduct be given to those who came. When this was granted, he returned.

After some days envoys came from King Antiochus: Zeuxis, formerly satrap of Lydia, and Antipater his nephew.

Their first concern was to meet King Eumenes, because they feared that, owing to the earlier friction, he might be especially eager to harm them.

But contrary to expectation they found him moderate and mild, and so at once moved on to the general audience.

Summoned into the council, they said much else besides, urging the Romans to use their success with gentleness and magnanimity.

They declared that this was not so much to Antiochus's advantage as to that of the Romans themselves, since fortune had now handed them mastery and dominion over the inhabited world.

In the end they simply asked what Antiochus must do to obtain peace and friendship with Rome.

The men in the council had already sat together and deliberated about these matters, and now instructed Publius to set out the decision.

## Book 21 Chapter 17: The Roman Terms To Antiochus

Publius said that the Romans never became harsher after victory.

Therefore the envoys would receive from Rome the same answer that they had already received before the battle, when they came to the Hellespont.

Antiochus must withdraw from Europe and from all Asia on this side of Taurus.

In addition, he must pay the Romans fifteen thousand Euboic talents as compensation for the cost of the war.

Of this, five hundred were to be paid immediately, another twenty-five hundred when the people ratified the settlement, and the rest over twelve years, one thousand talents each year.

He must also pay Eumenes the four hundred talents still owed to him and the grain still lacking under the treaty made with Eumenes' father.

Along with this he must surrender Hannibal the Carthaginian, Thoas the Aetolian, Mnasilochus the Acarnanian, and the Chalcidians Philo and Eubulidas.

As security for all this, Antiochus must at once provide twenty hostages of the sort specified.

Publius announced these terms on behalf of the whole council. Since Antipater and Zeuxis agreed to them, everyone resolved to send envoys to Rome to ask the senate and people to ratify the treaty.

So they separated on those terms, and in the following days the Romans divided their forces.

A few days later, when the hostages had arrived at Ephesus, Eumenes and the envoys from Antiochus immediately began preparations to sail to Rome.

In the same way envoys also went to Rome from the Rhodians, the Smyrnaeans, and almost all the peoples and states dwelling on this side of Taurus.

## Book 21 Chapter 18: Eumenes Persuaded to Speak

Olympiad 147, year 3. Affairs in Italy.

At the opening of summer, after the Roman victory over Antiochus, King Eumenes was in Rome, together with Antiochus's envoys and those from Rhodes and many others.

Nearly everyone in Asia had sent ambassadors to Rome immediately after the battle, because at that time all hopes for the future rested for all alike with the senate.

The senate received everyone graciously, but gave King Eumenes the grandest reception of all, both in its formal meeting of him and in its hospitality; next after him came the Rhodians.

When the time for audience arrived, they first called in the king and asked him to speak openly about what he wanted from the senate.

Eumenes answered that if he had wished to obtain some favor from others, he would have used the Romans as counselors, so as neither to desire anything improper nor to ask for anything beyond what was fitting.

But since he now stood before the Romans themselves as a petitioner, he thought it best to place both his own interests and those of his brothers in their hands.

When one of the elder senators rose and urged him not to be afraid but to say what seemed best to him, since the senate was resolved to do all it could for him, Eumenes remained of the same opinion. After some time had passed, the king withdrew.

The senate then deliberated privately on what ought to be done.

It was resolved to invite Eumenes again and encourage him to speak freely about what had brought him there, since he knew Asian affairs and his own interests more exactly than anyone else.

After this decision he was called back in, and when one of the elder senators explained the resolution, he was compelled to speak on the matter.

## Book 21 Chapter 19: King Eumenes In the Senate

So he was forced to address the question. He said that he would add nothing further on his own concerns, but would continue entirely to leave those matters in Roman hands. One point only troubled him—the question of the Rhodians.

That was why he had now been brought to speak about the present situation.

For they had come no less eagerly to advance the interests of their own city than he had come to advance those of his own kingdom.

Yet, he said, their words would give an impression opposite to their true purpose, and this would be easy to recognize.

Once admitted, they would say that they had come neither to ask anything at all from the Romans nor in any way to injure him, but to plead for the freedom of the Greeks who lived in Asia.

They would further say that this was not so much a favor to themselves as something that befitted the Romans and was consistent with their past deeds.

Such would be the appearance created by their speech; but the real disposition behind it would be the opposite.

For if the cities were freed, as they urged, Rhodian power would greatly increase, while his own would in a way be ruined.

The very name of freedom and autonomy would draw away from him not only those cities now to be freed, but even those already subject to him from earlier times, once the Romans openly showed that this was their chosen policy; all these would be added to the Rhodians.

That is the natural course of events. Men who think they have been liberated through Rhodian action will be their allies in name, but in reality they will readily do whatever the Rhodians order, being bound to them by the greatest obligation.

Therefore, gentlemen, he urged them to watch this point carefully, lest they unknowingly strengthen one ally beyond measure while unreasonably weakening another,

and at the same time end up rewarding former enemies while overlooking and slighting true friends.

## Book 21 Chapter 20: Eumenes Has Always Been a Friend to Rome

As for everything else, I would willingly yield to my neighbors, if need be, without contention; but in friendship toward you and goodwill for you, I would never knowingly give way to any living man.

I think my father too, if he were alive, would have spoken in the same way.

For he, being the first in Asia and Greece to share your friendship and alliance, preserved that bond in the noblest fashion to the very end of his life, not only in intention but in action.

He joined you in all your wars in Greece, provided greater land and naval forces than your other allies, contributed more supplies, and endured greater dangers.

In short, he ended his life in the very midst of action during the war with Philip, while urging the Boeotians into friendship and alliance with you.

And I, when I succeeded to the throne, preserved my father's policy—for that I could not abandon—but in deeds I have gone even further.

For the times brought me to a fiercer test than they did him.

Antiochus was eager to give me his daughter and to bind me to himself by marriage and alliance; he offered at once to restore the cities that had been taken from me before, and after that promised to do anything, if only I would join him in the war against you.

Yet I was so far from accepting any of this that, more than any other of your allies, I fought alongside you against Antiochus with large land and naval forces, contributed abundant resources when you most needed them, and exposed myself without reserve to every danger under your commanders.

In the end I even endured being shut up in Pergamum itself, besieged and in danger alike for my life and for my kingdom, because of my loyalty to the Roman people.

## Book 21 Chapter 21: Conclusion of Eumenes' Speech

Therefore, men of Rome, since many of you have seen these things with your own eyes and all of you know that what I say is true, it is only just that you should show proper concern for us.

It would be a most shocking thing if you made Massanissa—who was not only once your enemy, but came over to you at the last with only a few horsemen—king over the greater part of Libya because he proved faithful in a single war against Carthage,

and if you raised Pleuratus, who had done nothing beyond keeping faith, to be the greatest ruler in Illyria,

but then treated as nothing us who, through our forefathers, have worked with you in the greatest and finest of your achievements.

What, then, is it that I ask, and what do I say we ought to receive from you?

I will speak openly, since you yourselves have called on me to say what I think.

If you decide to keep certain parts of Asia—lands that lie on this side of Taurus and formerly belonged to Antiochus—we would wish above all to see that happen.

For we believe our kingship will be safest if you are our neighbors, and especially if we share directly in your power.

But if you decide not to do this and instead to withdraw entirely from Asia, then we say that no one has a juster claim than we do to the prizes won in the war.

Yes, someone may say that it is nobler to free those who are enslaved—if indeed they had not dared to fight beside Antiochus against you.

But since they chose to do that, it is far nobler to repay true friends with fitting gratitude than to bestow benefits on former enemies.

## Book 21 Chapter 22: The Rhodians Address the Senate

When Eumenes had spoken effectively and withdrawn, the council received both the king himself and what he had said with favor, and was entirely ready to gratify him as far as possible.

After him they intended to call in the Rhodians, but because one of the Rhodian envoys was late, they summoned the men from Smyrna instead.

These gave many proofs of the goodwill and zeal they had shown the Romans in the present war.

But since everyone agreed that of all the free states in Asia they had been the most wholehearted, I do not think it necessary to set out their arguments in detail.

After them the Rhodians came in. After briefly mentioning what they themselves had done for Rome, they quickly turned to the question of their own policy.

They said that the greatest difficulty of this embassy was that circumstances had set them in opposition to a king to whom both publicly and privately they were closely attached.

Their own city, they said, believed that the finest course, and the one most fitting for the Romans, was that the Greeks in Asia should be set free and enjoy autonomy, the possession dearest to all men; but for Eumenes and his brothers this was the least advantageous outcome. For every monarchy naturally hates equality

and seeks to have all men—or at least as many as possible—subject and obedient.

Yet, though this was the state of things, they said they were convinced they would prevail, not because Eumenes had less influence with the Romans, but because their advice was more just and clearly more beneficial for all.

If there were no other way for the Romans to repay Eumenes except by handing over to him the autonomous cities, then there might well be a real difficulty in the present case.

They would have to choose either to neglect a true friend,

or to disregard what was noble and fitting for themselves and so dim and tarnish the final glory of their own achievements.

But if both ends can be adequately provided for, who could still feel uncertainty on this point?

The case, they said, was like a lavish banquet: there is enough for everyone, and more than enough.

Lycaonia, Hellespontine Phrygia, Pisidia, and besides these the Chersonese and the neighboring parts of Europe can be given by you to whomever you wish.

Added to the kingdom of Eumenes, these could make it ten times what it now is; and if all or most of them were assigned to him, his power would be inferior to no other monarchy.

## Book 21 Chapter 23: Conclusion of the Rhodians' Speech

So, men of Rome, it is possible both to strengthen your friends on a generous scale and not to lower the splendor of your own policy.

For the end you pursue is not the same as that of other men, but something different.

All other peoples go to war because they want to conquer and acquire cities, wealth, and ships.

But the gods have made you in need of none of these things, since they have placed everything in the inhabited world under your power.

What, then, do you still need, and for what should you take the strongest possible precautions?

Clearly praise and glory among men—things that are hard to win, and even harder to preserve once won.

You can recognize the truth of this from your war against Philip. In that war you endured everything for the freedom of the Greeks; that was your declared object, and that was the only prize you gained from the war, nothing else.

Yet you prize that achievement more highly than the tribute paid by Carthage—and rightly so.

Money is a possession common to all mankind; but what is noble, and what belongs to praise and honor, belongs to the gods and to men nearest to them.

Therefore the liberation of the Greeks is the most august of all your deeds. If you now add what properly follows, the glory you possess will be brought to completion; but if you neglect it, even your former achievements will plainly be diminished.

As for us, gentlemen, having deliberately chosen this course and having truly shared with you in the greatest contests and dangers, we do not now abandon the place of friends.

Rather, what we think befits you and serves your interests, we have not hesitated to remind you of openly, aiming at nothing else and valuing nothing above what duty requires.

When the Rhodians had spoken in this way, they seemed to everyone to have spoken with moderation and good sense on the issue before them.

## Book 21 Chapter 24: The Decision of the Senate

After this the senate introduced Antiochus's envoys, Antipater and Zeuxis.

They spoke with dignity and earnest appeal, and the senate approved the agreements already made with Scipio in Asia.

A few days later the people ratified them as well, and oaths were sworn on these terms with Antipater and his colleagues.

After that they also introduced the rest of the envoys who were present from Asia; they listened only briefly,

but gave them all the same answer: that they were sending ten commissioners to decide all disputed questions among the cities.

Having given this answer, they then appointed the ten commissioners, entrusting them with the details.

As for the main principles, they themselves decided that of the peoples dwelling on this side of Taurus, all who had formerly been subject to Antiochus should be given to Eumenes, except Lycia and Caria as far as the Maeander, which should belong to the Rhodians.

And among the Greek cities, those that had paid tribute to Attalus should continue to pay it to Eumenes, while those that had paid tribute to Antiochus alone should have that tribute remitted.

Having laid down these outlines for the general settlement, they sent the ten commissioners out to Asia to join the consul Gnaeus.

After these arrangements had already been made, the Rhodians came again before the senate concerning Soli in Cilicia. Because of kinship, they said, it was fitting for them to care for that city.

For the people of Soli, like the Rhodians, were colonists from Argos, and on that basis they proved the kinship between the two peoples to be brotherly.

Therefore they said it was just that the people of Soli should receive freedom from the Romans through Rhodian good offices.

When the senate heard this, it summoned Antiochus's envoys and at first ordered that Antiochus should give up all Cilicia. But when Antipater and his party refused, on the ground that this went beyond the treaty, the senate turned again to the case of Soli by itself.

Since the king's envoys pressed their opposition strongly, the senate dismissed them, called in the Rhodians, explained the objections raised by Antipater's party, and added that it would endure anything if the Rhodians had firmly resolved on this point.

But the Rhodian envoys, pleased by the zeal of the senate, said they would ask for nothing further; so the matter remained where it stood.

Then, just as the ten commissioners and the other envoys were about to set out, the Scipios and Lucius—the victors over Antiochus in the naval battle—sailed into Brundisium from Italy.

After some days they entered Rome and celebrated triumphs.

## Book 21 Chapter 25: The Aetolian War

Affairs in Greece.

Amynander, king of the Athamanians, thinking that he had now securely recovered his kingdom, sent envoys to Rome and also to the Scipios in Asia—for they were still around Ephesus.

He partly defended himself for seeming to have regained his position through Aetolian help, partly accused Philip, but chiefly begged to be received again into alliance.

The Aetolians, believing they had found a favorable moment to recover Amphilochia and Aperantia, decided to campaign in those regions.

Nicander the general gathered the levy of the whole nation and invaded Amphilochia.

Since most of the inhabitants willingly came over to them, they moved on into Aperantia. When that region also joined them voluntarily, they campaigned next in Dolopia.

The people there at first made some show of resistance out of loyalty to Philip, but when they considered what had happened to the Athamanians and Philip's weakness there, they quickly changed their minds and went over to the Aetolians.

Since events had gone so well, Nicander led the army home, thinking Aetolia now secured by the peoples and places just mentioned, so that no one could any longer harm their territory.

But just when this had happened and the Aetolians were growing proud of their success, news arrived of the battle in Asia, in which they learned that Antiochus had been utterly defeated. Their spirits collapsed again.

And when Damoteles arrived from Rome and announced that the war still remained solely against them, and that Marcus and his army had crossed over to attack them, they fell into complete helplessness and did not know how to deal with the danger pressing upon them.

So they decided to send to the Rhodians and the Athenians, asking and urging them to go as envoys to Rome on their behalf, to soften Roman anger, and to find some release for the evils now surrounding Aetolia.

At the same time they sent envoys of their own again to Rome—Alexander, called Isius, Phaeneas, Chalepus, Alypus the Ambraciot, and Lycopus.

## Book 21 Chapter 26: Fulvius Aims to Fight at Ambracia

When envoys from Epirus came to the Roman general, he consulted with them about the campaign against the Aetolians.

They advised him to march on Ambracia—since the Ambraciots at that time belonged politically with the Aetolians.

They argued that if the Aetolians chose to accept battle, the country around that city offered the best possible ground for the armies to fight on, and that if they lost heart,

the city itself was well situated for a siege. The region had abundant supplies for building siege works, and the river Aratthus, flowing past the city, would help both the needs of the camp in summer and the safety of the siege operations.

Since the envoys seemed to be advising well, the general broke camp and led the army through Epirus against Ambracia.

When he arrived and the Aetolians did not dare meet him, he went around inspecting the city and energetically pushed on the siege.

Meanwhile the Aetolian envoys who had been sent to Rome were intercepted near Cephallenia by Sibyrtus son of Petraeus and brought down to Charadrus.

At first the Epirotes decided to place them at Buchetus and guard them carefully. But after some days they demanded ransom, because they were at war with the Aetolians.

It happened that Alexander was the richest of all the Greeks, while the others, though not poor, were far inferior to him in wealth.

At first the Epirotes ordered each man to pay five talents. The others did not altogether reject this, since they valued their safety above all else.

But Alexander said he would never agree, because the money was far too much, and he spent sleepless nights mourning to himself at the thought of paying five talents.

The Epirotes, foreseeing what might happen and fearing that the Romans might learn they had detained men traveling as envoys to them and then order their release, lowered the demand and now asked three talents apiece.

The others gladly agreed, gave guarantees, and went home. But Alexander said he would not pay more than one talent, and even that was too much.

In the end he gave up hope and remained in custody, an old man with property worth more than two hundred talents; I think he would rather have died than pay the three talents.

So strong in some men is the drive and eagerness to grasp more.

Yet on this occasion chance itself aided his greed, so that because of the turn events took, his irrationality won praise and approval from everyone.

For a few days later letters arrived from Rome ordering the men's release, and so he alone was freed without ransom.

When the Aetolians heard how his affair had turned out, they appointed Damoteles once again as envoy to Rome.

But when he had sailed as far as Leucas and learned that Marcus was advancing through Epirus with his army against Ambracia, he gave up all hope in the embassy and returned to Aetolia.

## Book 21 Chapter 27: Siege of Ambracia

The Aetolians, besieged by Marcus the Roman consul, met the assault of the engines and battering rams with spirited resistance.

For after securing his camp, Marcus organized the siege on a large scale. He brought three works up through the level ground against the quarter called the Pyrrheium, separate from one another but parallel, and a fourth against the Asclepieion and a fifth against the acropolis.

Since the attack was pressed vigorously at all these points at once, the expectation of what was coming became terrifying to the men inside.

While the rams struck hard at the walls and the hooked poles dragged away the battlements, the defenders tried to counter these devices by letting fall upon the rams, from cranes, masses of lead and stone and oak beams wrapped in tow.

Against the hooks they fastened iron grapnels and dragged them inside the wall, so that the shaft was shattered against the battlements and they gained control of the hooked irons.

Most of all they fought by making sallies with great courage, sometimes attacking by night the men sleeping at the works, sometimes openly assailing those on daytime duty, and so they delayed the siege.

Nicander was operating outside and sent five hundred cavalry into the city. These broke through the enemy entrenchment lying between and slipped into the city.

He had instructed them that on the agreed day they were to sally out, while he himself would join in and support the danger.

They rushed boldly out of the city and fought gallantly, but Nicander failed them, whether because he was frightened by the danger or because he thought the business occupying him elsewhere was necessary; so the attempt miscarried.

## Book 21 Chapter 28: Smoking Out the Enemy

The Romans, working constantly with their battering rams, kept bringing down one part of the wall after another.

Even so, they could not force their way into the city through the breaches, because the defenders worked energetically at rebuilding inside and the Aetolians fought bravely on the fallen sections.

Therefore, at a loss, the Romans turned to mining and underground tunnels.

They secured the central one of the three existing works and carefully covered the shaft with screens, then drove forward a gallery parallel to the wall for nearly two plethra.

From this they began to dig continuously, night and day in relays.

For several days they escaped the notice of those inside while carrying the earth out through the shaft.

But when the pile of excavated soil became large and visible from the city, the leaders of the besieged vigorously dug a trench inside, parallel to the wall and to the gallery opposite the towers. When it had reached a sufficient depth,

they set a continuous row of thin bronze vessels along the side nearest the wall, and walking by them in the trench listened for the sound of digging from outside.

Once they had marked the spot where one of the vessels signaled by sympathetic vibration, they dug an underground cross-passage from within toward the existing trench under the wall, aiming to meet the enemy head-on.

This was quickly achieved, because the Romans had not only reached the wall underground but had already propped up a considerable stretch of wall on both sides of the mine, and so the two sides encountered one another.

At first they fought underground with pikes; but because neither side could do much, since both protected themselves with shields and wicker screens,

someone then suggested to the defenders that they place at the width of the tunnel a large jar, bore a hole through its bottom, insert an iron tube as long as the vessel, fill the whole jar with fine feathers, and put a very small fire under it near the rim.

Then they were to fit over the mouth an iron lid full of holes and carefully push it into the mine with its opening facing the enemy.

When it came close to the enemy, they were to pack the rim all around, leaving only two holes on either side, through which they could thrust pikes so as to keep the enemy from approaching the jar.

After that, taking a bellows such as smiths use and fitting it to the iron tube, they were to blow hard on the fire among the feathers, gradually pulling the tube outward as the feathers burned farther in. Everything was done exactly this way.

The result was a great quantity of smoke, and because of the nature of feathers it was particularly acrid, all of it driven into the enemy tunnel.

So the Romans suffered badly and were in great difficulty, being unable either to stop the smoke or to endure it in the mine.

As the siege was thus being prolonged, the Aetolian general decided to send envoys to the Roman commander.

## Book 21 Chapter 29: Athens, Rhodes, and Athamania Intercede

At this time the envoys from Athens and Rhodes came to the Roman camp to lend their help toward a settlement.

King Amynander of the Athamanians also arrived, eager to rescue the Ambraciots from the troubles surrounding them, Marcus having granted him safe conduct because of the circumstances.

For he had a close attachment to the Ambraciots, since he had spent a long time in exile in that city.

A few days later some men also came from the Acarnanians bringing Damoteles and his companions, because Marcus, when he learned of what had happened to them, had written to the people of Thyrium to send the men on to him.

Once all these had assembled, negotiations for peace were energetically pursued.

Amynander, true to his purpose, kept pressing the Ambraciots, urging them to save themselves, and warning that destruction was not far off if they did not take better counsel for themselves.

Since he repeatedly approached the wall and spoke to them in this way, the Ambraciots decided to invite him into the city.

The Roman general allowed the king to enter, and once inside he discussed the present situation with the Ambraciots.

Meanwhile the envoys from Athens and Rhodes took hold of the Roman commander and, by speaking to him in many different ways, tried to soften his anger.

Someone also suggested to Damoteles and Phaeneas that they should attach themselves to Gaius Valerius and work on him.

He was the son of the Marcus who had first concluded alliance with the Aetolians, and the maternal half-brother of Marcus, the general then in command; besides this, he was an energetic young man and especially trusted by the commander.

When Damoteles and his companions appealed to him, he thought the matter touched him personally and that it was his duty to stand up for the Aetolians, so he threw all his zeal and ambition into rescuing the nation from the evils around it.

With every effort thus being brought to bear from all sides, the matter was finally brought to completion.

For the Ambraciots, persuaded by the king, put themselves in the hands of the Roman commander and surrendered the city, on condition that the Aetolians should be allowed to depart under truce.

They made this their first stipulation in order to keep faith with their allies.

## Book 21 Chapter 30: End of the Aetolian War

So Marcus agreed to make peace with the Aetolians on these terms.

He was to receive two hundred Euboic talents at once, and three hundred more in six years, fifty each year.

All Roman prisoners and deserters in Aetolian hands were to be restored to the Romans within six months, without ransom.

The Aetolians were not to retain within their federal state, nor later admit into it, any city that, after Lucius Cornelius crossed over, had either been taken by the Romans or had entered friendship with them.

The Cephallenians were to be excluded from these terms altogether.

These were the main heads in which the settlement was then sketched out; but first the Aetolians had to approve them, and after that they had to be referred to Rome.

The Athenians and Rhodians stayed where they were, awaiting the Aetolian decision, while Damoteles and his companions returned and explained to the Aetolians what had been granted.

In general the terms pleased them, for everything was better than they had expected. They hesitated for some time only over the cities that had formerly belonged to their federation, but in the end they agreed to the proposals.

Marcus then took over Ambracia. He allowed the Aetolians to depart under truce, but he carried off from the city the statues, portrait-statues, and paintings, which were numerous because Ambracia had been a royal residence of Pyrrhus.

He was also given a crown valued at one hundred and fifty talents.

After settling these matters he marched into inland Aetolia, amazed that no one from the Aetolians came to meet him.

When he reached the place called Amphilochian Argos, one hundred and eighty stades from Ambracia, he encamped there.

There Damoteles and his companions met him and explained that the Aetolians had resolved to ratify the agreement made through them. So they separated, the Aetolians going home and Marcus returning to Ambracia.

Once there, he began preparations to transport the army across to Cephallenia,

while the Aetolians appointed Phaeneas and Nicander as envoys and sent them to Rome about the peace.

For none of the terms already mentioned had any force at all unless the Roman people also approved them.

## Book 21 Chapter 31: The Treaty With the Aetolians

So these men set out on the mission, taking with them the Rhodians and the Athenians.

Marcus likewise sent Gaius Valerius and some other friends to press the matter of peace.

But when they arrived in Rome, Roman anger against the Aetolians was renewed because of King Philip.

For Philip, believing that the Aetolians had unjustly taken Athamania and Dolopia from him, sent to his friends and urged them to share his anger and refuse the peace.

Therefore when the Aetolians first entered, the senate refused to hear them; but when the Rhodians and Athenians pressed the matter, it gave way and listened.

In particular Leon son of Hicesias seemed to have spoken well in other respects and to have used a comparison especially suited to the case.

He said that Roman anger against the Aetolians was understandable, because although the Aetolians had received many benefits from Rome, instead of repaying them with gratitude they had brought Roman supremacy into great danger by igniting the war with Antiochus.

Yet the senate was mistaken in one respect,

in directing its anger against the mass of the people. In states, he said, what happens to the multitude is very like what happens at sea.

The sea by its own nature is always calm and settled, and in general such that it would never disturb those who approach and use it.

But when violent winds fall upon it and force it into unnatural motion, then nothing becomes more terrible or more fearsome than the sea. That, he said, was exactly what had now happened in Aetolia.

“As long as they remained undisturbed, they were the most loyal of all Greeks to you and the surest partners in action.

But when Thoas and Dicaearchus blew in from Asia, and Menestas and Damocritus from Europe, and threw the masses into confusion, forcing them against their nature in all they said and did,

then, in their bad judgment, they wished to harm you but instead became the cause of disaster to themselves.

Therefore you should be implacable toward those men, but pity the many and make peace with them, knowing that once they have become themselves again—and now, beyond all else, have been saved by you—they will once more prove the most loyal of all Greeks.”

After the Athenian spoke in this way, he persuaded the senate to make peace with the Aetolians.

## Book 21 Chapter 32: Terms of the Treaty

Since the council so resolved, and the people also voted in agreement, the settlement was ratified. The detailed terms of the treaty were as follows.

“The people of the Aetolians shall maintain the rule and supremacy of the Roman people in good faith.

They shall not allow enemies to pass through their land or cities against the Romans or their allies and friends, nor shall they supply them by public decree with anything.

And whenever the Romans are at war with anyone, the people of the Aetolians shall also be at war with them.

The Aetolians shall restore all Roman and allied deserters, runaway slaves, and prisoners,

except those who, after being captured in war, returned to their own country and were later captured again, and except those who became enemies of Rome at the time when the Aetolians were fighting on Rome's side; these are to be handed over within one hundred days from the swearing of the oaths to the Roman official in Corcyra.

If some are not discovered within that time, then whenever they are found they shall be handed over without deceit; and after the oaths they are not to be allowed to return to Aetolia.

The Aetolians shall pay at once to the commander in Greece two hundred Euboic talents of silver, of no worse quality than Attic silver, though if they wish they may pay one third of the amount in gold, reckoning one mina of gold for ten minae of silver.

And from the day on which the oaths are sworn, for the first six years they shall pay fifty talents each year, depositing the money in Rome. The Aetolians shall also give forty hostages to the commander,

not younger than twelve and not older than forty years, for a term of six years, chosen by the Romans, excepting the general, the cavalry commander, the public secretary, and anyone who has already served as a hostage in Rome. These hostages are to be delivered in Rome.

If any of the hostages die, they shall replace him with another.

Cephallenia shall not be included in the treaty.

Of whatever lands, cities, or persons the Aetolians once controlled, if under Lucius Quinctius and Gnaeus Domitius or later they were taken or entered friendship with Rome, the Aetolians shall not bring into their federation any of those cities or the people in them.

The city and territory of Oeniadae shall belong to the Acarnanians.”

When the oaths on these terms had been sworn, the peace was complete; and the affairs of the Aetolians, and in general of the Greeks, took this form.

Fulvius carried out a night attack, seized the part of the citadel, and brought the Romans inside.

## Book 21 Chapter 32c

What is noble and what is advantageous seldom run together, and rare are the men able to unite and harmonize them with each other.

For in most cases we all know that what is noble avoids the character of immediate profit, and profit avoids the character of what is noble.

Yet Philopoemen made this his aim and accomplished it. For it was noble to bring the captive exiles back to Sparta, and advantageous to humble the Spartan state by killing those who had served as bodyguards to the tyranny.

And seeing that money is the source from which every monarchy recovers itself, and being by nature a shrewd and statesmanlike man, he took measures to prevent any importation of funds gathered from outside.

Affairs in Asia.

At the very time when the embassies in Rome were dealing with the treaty with Antiochus and the settlement of Asia as a whole, and while the Aetolian nation was at war in Greece, the war in Asia against the Galatians also came to its conclusion. It is that narrative that now begins.

## Book 21 Chapter 34: MoagĕTes of Cibyra

Moagetes was tyrant of Cibyra, a cruel and treacherous man,

and he deserves remembrance not in passing, but with deliberate notice worthy of his character.

When the Roman consul Gnaeus approached Cibyra and Helvius had been sent ahead to discover what attitude he meant to take, Moagetes sent envoys begging that the countryside not be ravaged, since he was a friend of the Romans and would do whatever was ordered.

At the same time he also offered a crown worth fifteen talents.

On hearing this, Helvius said he would refrain from ravaging the territory, but told him to negotiate with the commander himself about the whole matter, since he was following close behind with the army.

After this Moagetes sent not only envoys but also his own brother along with them. When Gnaeus met them on the march, he spoke to the envoys in a strained and bitter tone,

saying that Moagetes was not only the least friendly to Rome of all the dynasts in Asia, but that in his whole strength and conduct he deserved to be stripped of power and punished.

Terrified by this display of anger, the envoys gave up the rest of their instructions and merely asked him to grant an interview.

When he consented, they returned that day to Cibyra.

On the next day the tyrant came out with his friends, plainly and humbly dressed, without pomp, lamenting in his speech his own helplessness and the weakness of the cities he ruled, and asking Gnaeus to accept the fifteen talents.

For he ruled Cibyra, Syleium, and the city in the Marsh.

Gnaeus, astonished at his shamelessness, said nothing else to him except that if he did not gladly pay five hundred talents, he would not merely ravage the countryside, but would besiege and plunder the city itself.

Then Moagetes, terrified at what was coming, begged him not to do anything of the kind. Little by little he kept increasing the money offered,

and in the end persuaded Gnaeus, after taking one hundred talents and ten thousand medimni of wheat, to admit him to friendship.

## Book 21 Chapter 35: Pacification of Pamphylia

At the time when Gnaeus was crossing the river called Colobatus, envoys came to him from the city named Sinda and begged for help.

They said that the Termessians, after bringing in Philomelus, had laid waste their territory and plundered their city, and were now besieging the acropolis, where all the citizens together with their wives and children had taken refuge.

When Gnaeus heard them, he promised gladly that he would help, and, thinking the opportunity a lucky stroke, directed his march toward Pamphylia.

When he came near Termessus, he made terms of friendship with them on receiving fifty talents, and did the same with the Aspendians.

He also received the envoys from the other Pamphylian cities, dealt with each as policy required in their interviews, relieved the people of Sinda from the siege, and then once more resumed his march against the Galatians.

## Book 21 Chapter 36: Conquest of Pisidia

After taking the city of Cyrmasa and a great quantity of spoil, Gnaeus broke camp again.

As they marched along the lake, envoys came from Lysinoe and surrendered themselves to Roman protection.

Gnaeus accepted them, invaded the territory of the Sagalassians, drove off a large amount of booty, and waited to see what decision the men of the city would make.

When envoys came to him, he received them and, after taking a crown worth fifty talents, twenty thousand medimni of barley, and twenty thousand medimni of wheat, admitted them to friendship.

## Book 21 Chapter 37: The Gauls of Asia

Gnaeus, the Roman commander, sent envoys to the Galatian Eposognatus, asking him to negotiate with the Galatian kings.

Eposognatus in turn sent envoys to Gnaeus and urged the Roman commander not to strike first or lay hands on the Tolistobogian Galatians,

since Eposognatus himself would go to their kings and speak with them about friendship and was confident that he could persuade them to take a proper course in every respect.

As the Roman consul Gnaeus advanced, he bridged the river Sangarius, which was deep and difficult to cross.

While he was encamped by the river, Galli came from Attis and Battacus, priests at Pessinus of the Mother of the Gods,

wearing breast-images and sacred emblems, and declaring that the goddess announced victory and power to him.

Gnaeus received them kindly.

Later, when Gnaeus was near the little town called Gordium, envoys came from Eposognatus announcing that he had gone and spoken with the Galatian kings,

but that they would not agree to any friendly arrangement at all. Instead, they had gathered their children, women, and all their possessions on the mountain called Olympus and stood ready for battle.

## Book 21 Chapter 38: A Gallic Woman Takes Vengeance

Chiomara, the wife of Ortiagon, was among the prisoners taken together with the other women when Gnaeus and the Romans defeated the Galatians in Asia.

The centurion who took her used his opportunity in a soldier's fashion and violated her.

He was a man enslaved alike to pleasure and to money; yet greed finally got the better of him, and when a large amount of gold was agreed upon as ransom for the woman, he led her away to release her, with a river lying between the two sides.

When the Galatians had crossed, paid him the gold, and were taking back Chiomara, she signaled with a nod that one of them should strike down the Roman as he embraced her and was taking leave with affected courtesy.

The man obeyed, cut off his head, and she picked it up, wrapped it in her folds, and rode away.

When she came to her husband and laid the head before him, he marveled and said, “Woman, fidelity is a fine thing.” “Yes,” she answered, “but it is finer that only one man who has slept with me should be alive.”

Polybius says that he himself met and spoke with this woman at Sardis and admired both her spirit and her intelligence.

## Book 21 Chapter 39: Attempted Treachery By the Gauls

After the Romans had won their victory over the Galatians and were encamped near the city of Ancyra, with Gnaeus the commander preparing to advance farther,

envoys came from the Tectosages asking Gnaeus to leave his forces where they were, but on the following day to come himself to the space between the camps. Their kings, they said, would also come and discuss terms of settlement.

Gnaeus agreed and came at the appointed time with five hundred cavalry, but the kings did not appear.

After he had returned to his own camp, the envoys came again, making excuses for the kings and asking him to come once more, since this time they would send leading men to discuss the whole business.

Gnaeus said he would come, but stayed in his own camp and instead sent Attalus and some military tribunes with three hundred cavalry.

The Galatian envoys arrived as agreed and discussed the situation, but at the end they said they had no power either to conclude the matter or to ratify anything that had been proposed.

They declared that the kings would come the next day and settle everything, if only Gnaeus the commander also came to them.

When Attalus and his companions promised that Gnaeus would come, they then separated.

But the Galatians were using these delays and maneuvering against the Romans because they wanted to move some of their dependents and property beyond the river Halys and, above all, if possible to get the Roman commander into their hands—or, failing that, at least to kill him.

With that plan fixed, they watched the next day for the Romans to appear, having a thousand cavalry ready.

Gnaeus heard Attalus's report and, persuaded that the kings would come, rode out as usual with five hundred cavalry.

It happened that on the previous days the Roman parties sent out for wood and fodder had gone in this same direction, taking advantage of the cavalry escort that accompanied those going to the conference.

The same thing happened on this occasion too, and since many had gone out, the military tribunes also ordered the cavalry who usually escorted the foragers to go in that direction.

By this chance, what was needed for the danger at hand came to pass of itself.

## Book 21 Chapter 41: Philopoemen Combines What is Right and What is Expedient

Olympiad 147, year 4. Affairs in Asia.

At this time, while Gnaeus the Roman commander was wintering at Ephesus in the last year of the Olympiad in question, embassies came both from the Greek cities in Asia and from many others, bringing crowns to Gnaeus because he had defeated the Galatians.

For all who lived on this side of Taurus rejoiced at Antiochus's defeat not so much because some thought they were released from tribute, others from garrisons, and everyone generally from royal commands, as because the fear inspired by the barbarians had been taken away and they seemed delivered from the barbarians' violence and lawlessness.

Musaeus also came from Antiochus, and envoys from the Galatians, wanting to learn on what terms they might make friendship.

So too envoys came from Ariarathes, king of Cappadocia. For he also, having shared Antiochus's hopes and taken part in the battle against the Romans, was fearful and perplexed about his own fate.

Therefore he repeatedly sent embassies, wishing to learn what payment or what action might obtain pardon for his mistake.

The commander praised all the embassies from the cities and received them kindly before dismissing them.

To the Galatians he replied that once he had welcomed King Eumenes, he would then conclude arrangements with them. To Ariarathes' envoys he said that if they paid six hundred talents, they might have peace.

To Antiochus's envoy he arranged that he would come with the army to the borders of Pamphylia to receive the two thousand five hundred talents and the grain the king owed his soldiers under the agreements previously made with Lucius.

After this he purified the army, and since the season now allowed it, he broke camp with Attalus, reached Apamea on the eighth day, stayed there three days, and on the fourth marched on again with forced marches.

On the third day after that he came to the place appointed for meeting Antiochus's people and encamped there.

When Musaeus and his companions met him and begged him to stay because the wagons and pack animals bringing the grain and money were delayed, he agreed and remained three days.

When the supplies arrived, he measured out the grain to the army and handed the money to one of the military tribunes with instructions to convey it to Apamea.

## Book 21 Chapter 42

When Gnaeus learned that the commander of the garrison stationed by Antiochus at Perga was neither withdrawing the garrison nor leaving the city himself, he marched there with his army.

As he approached the city, the officer in command came out to meet him and begged that no judgment be passed before he was heard.

He said that he was simply doing what duty required: Antiochus had entrusted the city to him in good faith, and he was guarding it until the man who had entrusted it made clear what he should do; up to the present, no one had informed him at all.

Therefore he asked for thirty days, so that he might send and ask the king what course to take.

Gnaeus, seeing that Antiochus was behaving honorably in all other respects, allowed him to send and inquire of the king; and after some days, when the answer came, he surrendered the city.

At about this time the ten commissioners and King Eumenes sailed to Ephesus, just as summer was beginning; after taking two days to recover from the voyage, they went up to Apamea.

When Gnaeus learned of their arrival, he sent his brother Lucius with four thousand men to the Oroandians, in a show of force, to collect the money still owed under the agreed terms.

He himself broke camp with the rest of the army and hurried on, eager to join Eumenes and the commissioners.

When he reached Apamea and found both the king and the ten, he sat in council with them about affairs.

They decided first to ratify the oaths and treaty with Antiochus, concerning which there is no need to say more at length, since one can form one's judgment from the written terms themselves.

## Book 21 Chapter 43: Settlement of Asia

The detailed provisions were as follows: Antiochus and the Romans were to remain friends for all time, provided he carried out the treaty.

King Antiochus and those under his authority were not to allow enemies of the Romans and their allies to pass through their territory or to supply them in any way.

The same obligation applied in reverse to the Romans and their allies with regard to Antiochus and those under him.

Antiochus was not to make war on the islanders or on peoples in Europe.

He was to evacuate the cities and territory specified.

His soldiers were to take nothing out except the weapons they were carrying; if they had removed anything else, they were to restore it to the same cities.

They were not to receive from the realm of King Eumenes either soldiers or anyone else.

If any of Antiochus's troops were in the cities being taken over by the Romans, they were to be returned to Apamea.

But any persons from Antiochus's kingdom who were with the Romans and their allies were to be free either to remain where they were, if they wished, or to run away.

Antiochus and those under him were to restore the slaves of the Romans and their allies, as well as prisoners and deserters, and any captives they might have received from any source.

Antiochus was also to hand over, if it was in his power, Hannibal son of Hamilcar the Carthaginian, Mnasilochus the Acarnanian, Thoas the Aetolian, the Chalcidians Eubulidas and Philo, and any Aetolians who had held federal office.

He was to surrender all the elephants at Apamea and to keep no others in the future.

He was likewise to surrender his warships and all their tackle and equipment, and thereafter keep no more than ten decked ships; he was not to possess any vessel rowed by more than thirty oars, nor any single-banked warship for wars begun by himself.

Nor were his ships to sail west of the Calycadnus and the Sarpedonian promontory, except when carrying tribute, envoys, or hostages.

Antiochus was not to be allowed to levy mercenaries from territory subject to Rome, nor to receive fugitives from it.

Any houses belonging to Rhodians or their allies in territory subject to King Antiochus were to remain Rhodian property, as they had been before the outbreak of war.

Any debts owed to them were still to be legally recoverable, and any property taken from them was to be returned once traced. The Rhodians were also to continue, as before the war, exempt from dues.

If Antiochus had given to others any of the cities he was now bound to restore, he was to withdraw his garrisons and men from those places too. If people later wished to desert to him, he was not to receive them.

Antiochus was to pay the Romans twelve thousand talents of the best Attic silver over twelve years, one thousand each year, each talent weighing not less than eighty Roman pounds, and also five hundred and forty thousand modii of grain.

He was also to pay King Eumenes three hundred and fifty talents over the first five years, seventy each year at the season when he also paid the Romans,

and, in place of the grain as valued by Antiochus himself, one hundred and twenty-seven talents and one thousand two hundred and eight drachmas, which Eumenes agreed to accept in satisfaction.

Antiochus was to give twenty hostages, replacing them every three years with others, none younger than eighteen and none older than forty-five.

If any payment fell short, the deficit was to be made good in the following year.

If any of the cities or peoples whom Antiochus was forbidden to attack should themselves first make war on him, he might lawfully fight them.

But he was not to exercise sovereignty over such peoples or cities, nor attach them to himself as friends.

Any injuries committed by either side against the other were to be submitted to arbitration.

If both parties by common agreement wished to add anything to the treaty or remove anything from it, they were free to do so.

## Book 21 Chapter 44: The Roman Commissioners Arrive at Ephesus

When the oaths on these terms had been sworn, the commander at once sent Quintus Minucius Thermus and his brother Lucius—who had only just brought back the money from the Oroandians—into Syria.

They were instructed to receive the king's oaths and confirm the particular clauses of the treaty.

He also sent dispatches to Quintus Fabius, commander of the fleet, ordering him to sail once more to Patara, take over the ships lying there, and burn them.

## Book 21 Chapter 45: Treaty With Antiochus

Manlius the proconsul, after exacting three hundred talents from Ariarathes, made him a friend of the Romans.

## Book 21 Chapter 46: The Romans Burn Antiochus's Ships at Patara

At Apamea the ten commissioners and Gnaeus, the Roman commander, heard everyone who had come before them. In cases where men disputed about territory, money, or some other matter, they assigned to them cities acknowledged by both sides as places in which the disputed claims were to be judged. Their general settlement, however, was as follows.

All autonomous cities that had formerly paid tribute to Antiochus but had remained loyal to Rome were released from tribute; while those that had paid assessed contributions to Attalus were ordered to pay the same tribute to Eumenes.

If any cities had revolted from friendship with Rome and fought on Antiochus's side, they were ordered to pay to Eumenes the tribute formerly assigned to Antiochus.

The Colophonians living at Notium, the Cymaeans, and the people of Mylasa were left tax-free.

To the Clazomenians they added as a gift the island called Drymussa, and to the Milesians they restored the sacred land from which they had earlier withdrawn because of the wars.

They also favored the Chians, Smyrnaeans, and Erythraeans in other ways and assigned to each of them the territory each presently desired and believed rightfully theirs, in recognition of the goodwill and zeal they had shown during the war.

They restored to the Phocaeans their ancestral constitution and the territory they had formerly possessed.

After that they dealt with the Rhodians, giving them Lycia and Caria up to the river Maeander, except Telmessus.

As for King Eumenes and his brothers, they had already made as much provision for them as they could in the treaty with Antiochus, and they now added in Europe the Chersonese, Lysimacheia, and the forts and territory adjacent to them that Antiochus had ruled.

In Asia they gave him Hellespontine Phrygia, Greater Phrygia, the Mysians whom Prusias had previously detached from him, Lycaonia, Milyas, Lydia, Tralles, Ephesus, and Telmessus.

These were the gifts they assigned to Eumenes. But regarding Pamphylia, since Eumenes claimed it lay on this side of Taurus while Antiochus's envoys claimed the opposite, they referred the matter to the senate.

When most of the necessary and important arrangements had thus been completed, they broke camp and marched toward the Hellespont, wishing on the way to secure matters still further in regard to the Galatians.
