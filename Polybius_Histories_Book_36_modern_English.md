# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXXVI

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 36 Chapter 1: The Third Punic War

Some may perhaps ask why I have not made use of dramatic speeches in narrating a subject and events of such importance.

That is in fact what most historians do: they arrange the arguments on both sides and present the speeches that could have been delivered.

As for myself, I have shown in many places in my history that I do not reject this practice altogether, since I have often reported speeches and political statements made by statesmen.

But that I do not choose to do this in every case will now be clear: for it would not be easy to find a subject more striking than this one, or material more abundant and better suited to comparison.

Indeed, no form of composition could be more convenient for me.

Yet I do not think it right for statesmen, on every question brought before them, to be ready with arguments and long elaborations, but rather to speak in the way appropriate to the particular occasion;

and in the same way I do not think it right for historians to display their skill for the amusement of their readers, but rather to devote all their effort to discovering and recording what was truly said, and, among such words, only those that are most timely and most essential.

## Book 36 Chapter 2: The Romans Find a Justification for War

This principle having long since been firmly fixed in everyone's mind, the Romans were looking for a suitable occasion and a decent pretext for outsiders.

They were in fact especially concerned with this point, and rightly so.

For the way in which a war is entered upon, as Demetrius says, makes victory seem more glorious and defeat more secure; but if the pretext is shabby and unworthy, the opposite result follows.

So at that time, while they were disputing among themselves about how outsiders would judge the matter, they nearly gave up the war.

## Book 36 Chapter 3: War With Carthage Resolved Upon

While the Carthaginians had long been deliberating how they ought to answer the Roman demand, the people of Utica forestalled their plan by surrendering their city to the Romans, and this left the Carthaginians in utter helplessness.

For there was still one hope left to them: if they also submitted and placed themselves under Roman control, they thought they would certainly win Roman favour;

for they had never done this even in their greatest extremity, when they had been beaten and the enemy stood at their walls, and had never before surrendered their country.

But they lost the benefit of this idea because the Uticans had got there first.

For now nothing would seem novel or surprising to the Romans if the Carthaginians merely did the same thing.

So, with only two evils left to choose from — to accept war bravely or to surrender their independence — they held a long and anxious secret debate in the council-house and appointed two envoys with full powers, instructing them to do what seemed best for the country's advantage in the circumstances.

These envoys were Gisco, called Strytanus, Hamilcar, Misdes, Gillimas, and Mago.

When they arrived in Rome from Carthage, they found that war had already been decided and that the generals had actually set out with their forces.

Since circumstances no longer allowed them any room for negotiation, they made an unconditional surrender.

## Book 36 Chapter 4: Meaning of Surrender

We have spoken before about what surrender to Roman control means, but it is necessary to remind the reader briefly here as well.

Those who give themselves up to Roman control first surrender their territory and the cities in it, along with all the men and women living in that land and in those cities.

Likewise they surrender rivers, harbours, sanctuaries, and tombs — in short, everything — so that the Romans become complete masters of all these things, while those who surrender are left masters of nothing at all.

When the Carthaginians had made such a surrender and were then summoned into the council-house, the commander conveyed the Senate's decision: since they had acted wisely, the Senate granted them liberty and the protection of their laws, and likewise all their territory and all their other property, both public and private.

The Carthaginians were pleased to hear this, believing that, since they had had to choose between two miserable alternatives, the Senate had treated them well by allowing them the most necessary and important things.

But then the commander explained that they would receive these concessions only if they sent three hundred hostages to Lilybaeum within thirty days — sons of men from the council and the senate — and obeyed whatever orders the consuls might give them.

For a time they were at a loss as to what those orders would be, but they set out at once, eager to report the matter to their countrymen as quickly as possible.

When they reached Carthage and explained everything in detail to the citizens,

the people thought that, in everything else, the envoys had acted with proper caution; but the fact that there had been no mention at all of the city itself filled them with great alarm and distress.

## Book 36 Chapter 5: Speech of Mago Brettius

At this moment, they say that Mago Brettius spoke with manliness and sound judgment.

He said that, as it seemed, the Carthaginians had two occasions on which to deliberate about themselves and their country, and that they had let one of them pass by.

For now, by God, there was no point in wondering what the consuls would order them to do, or why the Senate had made no mention of the city itself, but had spoken only when they were surrendering themselves.

Once they had done that, they must clearly understand that they would have to accept every order, unless it turned out to be utterly outrageous and beyond expectation.

If that was not the course they chose, then they must consider once more whether they would rather await war in their own land and suffer whatever horrors it might bring, or, in fear of the enemy's approach, submit willingly to whatever commands were imposed.

Since everyone, because of the war at hand and the uncertainty of the future, was inclined to obey what was ordered, it was decided to send the hostages to Lilybaeum.

Accordingly they immediately selected three hundred of the young men and sent them off with loud lamentation and tears, every one being accompanied by his nearest relatives and friends, and the women especially setting the whole scene ablaze with grief.

When they reached Lilybaeum, the consuls handed them over to Quintus Fabius Maximus, who happened to be the commander in Sicily at that time.

He conveyed them safely to Rome and had them all confined together in the dockyard of the six-banked ships.

## Book 36 Chapter 6: The Roman Army In Africa

Once the hostages had been disposed of there, the generals brought their fleet to the citadel of Utica.

When this reached Carthage, the city was in a state of utter tension and fear because no one knew what to expect next.

Nevertheless, they decided to send envoys to learn from the consuls what they should do, and to make clear that everyone was ready to obey whatever was ordered.

When the envoys reached the Roman camp and the council had been summoned, they entered and spoke according to their instructions.

The senior consul praised their purpose and willingness to comply and ordered them to hand over all their weapons and missiles without trickery or deceit.

The envoys said they would do what had been ordered, but asked them to consider what would happen if they gave up their weapons and the Romans then took them and sailed away from the country. Nevertheless, they surrendered them.

Thus it became clear how vast the city's resources were: they handed over to the Romans more than two hundred thousand weapons and two thousand catapults.

## Book 36 Chapter 7: Panic at the Envoys' Report

In short, they had no idea at all what was about to be demanded of them.

But from the very look on the envoys' faces they suspected the worst and immediately fell into every kind of lamentation and crying.

All the senators, crying out in horror, were left as though paralyzed by the shock.

Then, as the report quickly spread through the people, there was no more restraint: some rushed at the envoys, as though they were the guilty cause of the city's disasters,

others turned their anger on the Italians who had been seized in the city, and still others hurried toward the gates of the city.

## Book 36 Chapter 8: Character of Hamilcar Phameas

Hamilcar, also called Phameas, the Carthaginian general, was in the prime of life, strong in body, and, most importantly for war, an excellent and daring horseman.

Those who were jealous of Scipio tried to disparage his actions.

Phameas, however, seeing the Roman pickets, was not without courage; still, he avoided close fighting with Scipio. On one occasion he drew near the reserves, took up position behind the brow of a hill, and remained there for a considerable time.

The Roman maniples withdrew to a hill; and when everyone had given his opinion, Scipio said:

"When men are deliberating from scratch, they should take even greater care to avoid suffering harm than to do harm to their enemies."

It should not be surprising that I give a more detailed account of Scipio's actions and report everything he said with precision.

For when Marcus Porcius Cato heard in Rome of Scipio's achievements, he said, in effect: "What have you heard? He alone has the breath of wisdom in him; the rest are but shadows."

## Book 36 Chapter 9

In matters concerning the Carthaginians, after the Romans had defeated them, and in matters concerning the so-called Pseudo-Philip in Greece, there were many and varied opinions; at first these opinions concerned the Carthaginians, and afterward again the Pseudo-Philip.

The judgments and discussions about the Carthaginians were in fact divided and uncertain.

Some approved of the Romans, saying that they had acted wisely and practically in securing their supremacy.

For, they argued, it was sound policy for men who think far ahead to remove the danger hanging over them and to make their rule secure for their own country by taking back a city that had often disputed control with them and might still, if circumstances allowed, dispute it again.

Others opposed this view, saying that the Romans had not set out with that intention to acquire supremacy, but had gradually slipped into a lust for power like that of the Athenians and Spartans, moving more slowly than those states but arriving at the same end through the appearances they displayed.

For, they said, in the past they had always made war openly and continued until they had prevailed and forced their opponents to admit that they must obey Rome and do what was ordered.

But now, they argued, they had first shown their real purpose by their handling of Perseus and by uprooting the kingdom of the Macedonians from its foundations, and had now completed that same design in the case of the Carthaginians.

For since nothing irreparable had happened on account of them, they had resolved in a harsh and irreversible way against them, willing to accept and endure anything in order to carry out what had been ordered.

Others again said that the Roman nation was in general a statesmanlike people, and that this was one of their distinguishing traits and something of which the Romans rightly prided themselves: namely, that they fought their wars in a straightforward and honorable way, not using night attacks or ambushes, and rejecting everything that came about by deceit or trickery, claiming for themselves only those dangers that were open and face-to-face.

But now, they said, they had managed everything concerning the Carthaginians by deceit and trickery, advancing little by little with one proposal and then another concealment, until they had taken away from the allies every hope of helping them.

This was more fitting, they said, for a monarchic style of action than for a political and Roman one, and it came close, by right reason, to impiety and breach of faith. Some men, however, argued against this too.

For if the Romans had handled things this way before the Carthaginians had surrendered themselves and placed their country under Roman control, gradually revealing one demand and concealing another, they would reasonably have seemed guilty of the charges brought against them.

But since the Carthaginians had themselves surrendered, so that the Romans might decide whatever seemed best about them, and since the Romans then, within that authority, issued and ordered what they thought fit, the act could no longer be called close to impiety or even, scarcely, to breach of faith; and some said it was not injustice at all.

For there are three classes of wrong into which every accusation naturally falls, and the Romans' conduct did not fit any of them.

For impiety is wrongdoing against the gods, parents, or the dead; breach of faith is acting contrary to sworn and written agreements;

and injustice is what is carried out contrary to law and custom. But the Romans were guilty of none of these things in the present case: they had not sinned against the gods, the parents, or the dead; nor had they violated oaths or agreements — on the contrary, they accused the Carthaginians of violating them.

Nor did they overthrow laws or customs or private trust: for when the Carthaginians had handed themselves over, the Romans had simply done what they wished, since those surrendering were no longer obeying orders, and had thus brought necessity upon them.

## Book 36 Chapter 10

So much, then, was said about the Romans and the Carthaginians.

As for the Pseudo-Philip, at first the story seemed hardly believable: a sort of airborne Philip had appeared in Macedonia, contemptuous not only of the Macedonians but also of the Romans, and having no reasonable basis at all for his claim.

For the real Philip, as everyone knew, had died in Alba in Italy, about eighteen years old, two years after Perseus.

But three or four months later, when a report arrived that he had defeated the Macedonians in battle beyond the Strymon in the district of the Odomantians, some accepted the report, though most still disbelieved it.

Not long afterward another report arrived, saying that he was defeating the Macedonians in battle on this side of the Strymon and had control of all Macedonia; and when letters and envoys came from the Thessalians to the Achaeans asking for help because danger threatened them too, the event seemed astonishing and incredible.

For there was no appearance of probability or any reasonable explanation for what had happened. Such, then, were the feelings about these matters.

## Book 36 Chapter 11

When letters from Manilius reached the Achaeans in the Peloponnese, informing them that they would be doing well to send Polybius of Megalopolis at once to Lilybaeum, since he was needed for public business, the Achaeans decided to send him in accordance with the consul's instructions.

But we thought it right, in many ways, to obey the Romans, and so set aside all other concerns and sailed off when summer was beginning.

When we reached Corcyra and found there letters from the consuls that had arrived for the Corcyraeans, in which they explained that the Carthaginians had already handed over their hostages to them and were entirely ready to obey their orders,

we thought that the war was over and that we no longer had any need at all, and so we sailed back again to the Peloponnese.

## Book 36 Chapter 12

One should not be surprised if at times we refer to ourselves by name, and at other times by more general expressions, as when we say, "after I had said this"

or again, "when we had agreed." For since we are so deeply involved in the events that are later to be narrated, it is necessary to use references to ourselves, so that we do not keep repeating our name and become tedious through tautology, nor again say "I" and "for my part" at every turn and fall into an irritating manner.

Rather, by using all such forms and changing among them as the occasion requires, we should avoid, as far as possible, the heavy and burdensome habit of speaking too much about ourselves.

For such speech is naturally unwelcome, though often necessary when there is no other way to make the matter clear.

And indeed, as it happened, we were helped in this respect by a kind of fortunate accident: up to our own time no one, so far as we know, had inherited precisely the same name as ours.

## Book 36 Chapter 13

Through an unusual coincidence, the images of Callicrates were being brought in under cover of darkness while those of Lycortas were on the same day being carried out into the light, exactly as the original arrangement had intended.

This compelled everyone to remark that one must never use prosperity insolently against one's neighbors, knowing that this is especially the habit of Fortune: to overturn, by one’s own devices and laws, the very men who established them.

The natural love of novelty in human beings is itself enough to bring about every kind of change.

## Book 36 Chapter 14

The Romans sent envoys to restrain Nicomedes from his rashness and to prevent Attalus from making war on Prusias, and they appointed Marcus Licinius,

a man crippled by gout and completely unable to use his feet, then Aulus Maninus, who had such large scars across his head from a roof tile falling on him that it was astonishing he survived, and Lucius Malloelus, who was thought by all Romans to be the dullest of men.

Since the business required speed and boldness, those appointed seemed utterly unfitted for the task.

For this reason, it is said that Marcus Porcius Cato remarked in the Senate that Prusias would arrive not only already dead, but that Nicomedes too would grow old in his kingdom before the embassy could ever get there.

For how could they possibly move quickly, or what could their embassy accomplish if it had neither feet nor head nor heart?

## Book 36 Chapter 15

King Prusias, though repellent in appearance and, by reasoned judgment, not without some better qualities, was in outward form only half a man and in military matters ignoble and womanish.

For he was not merely cowardly, but also a stranger to hardship and altogether unmanly in both body and soul throughout his whole life.

This is the last thing one wishes to find in kings, and especially in the nation of the Bithynians.

He was also accompanied by a great deal of wantonness in bodily appetites.

He was wholly untrained in education, philosophy, and the questions studied in those fields.

In short, he had no conception at all of what the good is, and lived a barbarous life like Sardanapalus, both by day and by night.

Accordingly, once the people under his rule caught a little hope, they immediately formed an unalterable resolve not only to think differently from the king, but also to seek punishment from him.

## Book 36 Chapter 16

Masinissa, king of the Numidians in Libya, was the best and happiest of the kings of our own time.

He reigned for more than sixty years and was exceptionally healthy and long-lived, having reached ninety years of age.

He was also the strongest man of his time in bodily vigor: when he had to stand, he would stand on the same spot for a whole day, and when he sat down, he would not rise again.

He endured the hardships of cavalry service continuously, day and night, and suffered nothing from them.

A proof of his bodily strength: when he was ninety and about to die, he left behind a four-year-old son named Stembanus, whom Micypsa later adopted, along with nine other sons.

Because of the goodwill they had for one another, he preserved his whole life and left his kingdom free from every plot and every domestic stain.

But the greatest and most godlike thing about him was this: Numidia had previously been useless and was thought by nature incapable of producing cultivated crops,

yet he was the first and only one to show that it could yield all kinds of cultivated produce just as well, having set out, for each of his sons, extensive and fully fruitful estates in separate allotments.

One might fairly and rightly say this upon his death.

Scipio arrived at Cirta on the third day after the king's death and settled everything properly.

Polybius records that Masinissa died at ninety, leaving behind a four-year-old child who had been born to him.

## Book 36 Chapter 17

Polybius says: in criticizing those who attribute events, whether public affairs or private reversals, to Fortune and Fate, I now wish to distinguish this matter as far as the method of factual history allows.

Where a human being cannot, God knows, discover the causes because they are either impossible or extremely difficult to grasp, one may perhaps have recourse to God and Fortune — for example, in the case of extraordinary and continuous rains or snowstorms, or, by contrast, droughts and frosts that ruin the crops, or similarly with recurring pestilences and other things like these, whose cause is not easy to discover.

Therefore, in such matters, since we cannot understand them, it is reasonable to follow the common opinion and, by prayer and sacrifice, seek to appease the divine and ask the gods what words or actions will be best for us and may bring an end to the evils that afflict us.

But where it is possible to discover the cause from which and through which the event arose, I do not think one should refer such matters to the divine.

Let me explain. In our own times, all Greece was overcome by childlessness and, in general, by a shortage of people, as a result of which the cities were deserted and scarcity followed, although there had been neither continuous wars nor pestilential outbreaks.

If someone were to advise us, in this matter, to ask the gods what we should say or do in order to become more numerous and better govern our cities, would he not seem foolish, since the cause is obvious and the remedy lies in ourselves?

For human beings had turned toward vanity and greed, and still more toward idleness, and did not wish either to marry or, if they did marry, to rear the children they had, but sought to raise only one or two, and hardly even those, so that they might leave them wealthy and pampered. Thus the evil grew quickly without being noticed.

For when there was only one child or two, and one was carried off by war and another by disease, it was obvious that the homes would be left empty; and, as with beehives, when the swarms are gone, the cities too, little by little, become helpless because of their emptiness.

For such things there is no need to ask the gods how we are to be freed from the harm.

Any chance person will say that we can best help ourselves by changing our habits; or, if not, by making laws so that children will be reared. On such matters there is no need of diviners or portents.

The same argument applies also in particular cases.

But in those matters where the causes are hidden or hard to grasp, one must investigate; and one of these was what happened to the Macedonians.

For the Macedonians had received many and great benefits from the Romans: collectively, they had all been freed from monarchical commands and taxes and had plainly exchanged slavery for freedom; and individually, as cities, they had again been released from great internal strife and civil slaughter through Roman favor.

Yet because of the so-called Pseudo-Philip, many among them ... [fragmentary text follows] ... they saw many of their own people in a very short time driven into exile, tortured, and killed, whereas in the earlier days, under the kings who were truly kings, they had already experienced ... [fragmentary text continues] ... they were angered at the Roman encampment itself and regretted their former wrongs against themselves ... [fragmentary text continues].

From these things, who would not be puzzled by what happened? For the cause of them is hard to discover.

Therefore, in such a situation, one might say that the event was a divine affliction and that the wrath of the gods had overtaken all the Macedonians. This will become clear from what follows.
