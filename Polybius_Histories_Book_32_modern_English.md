# Polybius — The Histories, Book XXXII

Modern English translation of the Greek text.

## Book 32 Chapter 1: The Senate Breaks its Alliance with Ptolemy Philometor

Olympiad 155, year 1. Affairs in Italy.

At this time envoys arrived from Ariarathes, bringing a crown worth ten thousand gold pieces and setting forth the king's policy toward Roman affairs, [with part of the text missing], and they brought Tiberius and his colleagues forward as witnesses to these things.

When these men confirmed the statement, the senate accepted the crown with great gratitude and sent back in return the most distinguished of the gifts that it was accustomed to bestow, namely a staff and an ivory chair.

The senate therefore dismissed these envoys before the winter was over.

After them Attalus arrived, when the consuls had already entered office, and the Galatians whom Prusias had sent accused him.

After hearing them and many others from Asia, the senate not only cleared Attalus of the slanders but even sent him away with increased marks of favor.

For in proportion as they had become estranged from the king and were at odds with Eumenes, to that same degree they cultivated Attalus and sought to raise him higher.

## Book 32 Chapter 2: Massanissa Harasses the Carthaginians

Envoys also came from King Demetrius, led by Menochares, bringing to Rome a crown worth ten thousand gold pieces and at the same time bringing with them the man who had laid hands on Gnaeus.

The senate was uncertain for a long time how it ought to deal with this business.

Even so, it accepted both the envoys and the crown; but it did not accept the men who were being brought in custody.

And yet Demetrius had sent not only Leptines, who had been the actual murderer of Gnaeus, but also Isocrates.

This man was one of those grammarians who gave public lectures; by nature he was talkative, theatrical, and full of self-importance, and he had already made himself offensive even to the Greeks, since men like Alcaeus in his *Comparisons* had neatly mocked and ridiculed him.

But when he reached Syria and conceived contempt for everyone there, he was no longer content to discourse only on his own literary pursuits.

He even began to pronounce judgments on affairs of state, declaring that Gnaeus had suffered what was just, and that the other envoys too ought to have perished, so that not even the man who reported what had happened would have been left for the Romans, and they might thus cease from their arrogant commands and their unrestrained domination.

By throwing out such reckless talk, he fell into the misfortune already mentioned.

## Book 32 Chapter 3: Prusias Accuses Eumenes

Something happened in connection with the men just mentioned that deserves to be remembered.

After Leptines had laid hands on Gnaeus, he at once went about openly in Laodicea, saying that he had acted justly and had done what he did with the approval of the gods.

When Demetrius took over affairs, Leptines approached the king and urged him not to be afraid because of the murder of Gnaeus, and not to devise any harsh measures against the Laodiceans.

For, he said, he himself would go to Rome and teach the senate that he had done this deed with the approval of the gods.

So, because of his readiness and zeal, he was led along without chains and without any confinement.

Isocrates, on the other hand, once he was implicated in the affair, lost his mind completely. And when a collar and chain were put around his neck, he rarely even accepted food and in the end abandoned all care for his body.

Thus there arrived at Rome a marvelous spectacle, at which anyone looking would have admitted that, both in body and in soul, there is nothing more terrifying than a human being once he has turned savage.

For his appearance had become strangely dreadful and beastlike, since for more than a year he had had no dirt removed, no nails pared, and no hair cut.

And the expression and movement of his eyes created such an impression of his mind that anyone who saw him would sooner have approached any wild animal than that man.

Leptines, however, remained firm in his original purpose. He was ready to enter the senate, and to all who met him he freely confessed the deed and even specified that nothing troublesome would happen to him from the Romans.

In the end his expectation proved accurate. For the senate, as it seems to me, supposed that it would appear to the masses to be taking vengeance for the murder if it took custody of the guilty men and punished them, and therefore it very nearly refused to accept them, while keeping the charge itself untouched.

In this way it retained the power to make use of the accusations whenever it chose.

Accordingly it gave Demetrius this answer: that he would obtain the senate's favor if he gave it full satisfaction in accordance with its sovereign authority.

Envoys also came from the Achaeans, led by Xenon and Telecles, on behalf of the accused men and especially for the sake of Polybius and Stratius.

For time had by now consumed almost all the others, including those worthy of remembrance.

The envoys came with instructions that were generally deferential, so as not to enter into rivalry with the senate on any point.

But even after they entered and delivered the appropriate speeches, they accomplished nothing. On the contrary, the senate decided to stand by its existing position.

## Book 32 Chapter 4: Demetrius and Ariarathes

Greek Affairs.

Because Lyciscus the Aetolian was a disturber and a noisy agitator, but after he had been killed the Aetolians from that point onward were of one mind and in harmony, once a single man had been removed,

so great, it seems, is the power resident in the natures of human beings, that not only camps and cities but even whole national organizations and the broadest divisions of the inhabited world may, through the virtue or vice of one man, at one time experience the greatest evils and at another the greatest goods.

And so Lyciscus, though he was an utterly evil man, nevertheless ended his life well, with the result that many quite reasonably reproach Fortune because she sometimes bestows upon the worst of men the prize of a good death, which ought to belong to the good.

## Book 32 Chapter 5: Ambassadors from Ariarathes

Conditions in Aetolia were in good order, since civil strife there had been extinguished after the death of Lyciscus.

And when Mnasippus of Coroneia also died, the situation in Boeotia improved; the same happened in Acarnania when Chremas was removed from the scene.

For it was almost as though some purification of Greece had taken place, when its accursed men passed out of the world of the living.

In that same year Charops of Epirus also happened to die at Brundisium.

But affairs in Epirus were still unsettled and troubled, as they had been in the preceding years, because of the brutality and lawlessness of Charops, from the time when the war with Perseus was brought to an end.

For after Lucius Anicius and Lucius Aemilius had condemned some of the leading men to death and carried others off to Rome, all those who had come under even the slightest suspicion,

Charops then obtained full power to do whatever he wished, and there was no outrage he did not commit,

some by his own hand and others through his friends, since he himself was still very young, and the worst and most reckless men had gathered around him because they could enrich themselves from other people's property.

And what served as a reserve force and gave weight to the belief that he was acting with some justification and with Roman approval was his previous association with the men already mentioned, and besides them with Myrton, an elderly man, and his son Nicanor,

men otherwise moderate in character and thought to be friends of Rome, who for a long time had kept themselves far from every sort of injustice, but somehow at that moment gave themselves over to strengthening and sharing in the lawless acts of Charops.

After this man had openly murdered some in the marketplace, killed others in their own houses, and had still others assassinated in the countryside and on the roads by sending agents after them, and after he had seized the estates of all the dead, he introduced another scheme.

He posted up as exiles those whose property made them worth targeting, not only men but women as well.

By keeping this fear alive, he continually extorted money, dealing with the men himself and the women through his mother Philotis.

She too proved highly suited to the role, and was able to assist in acts of violence with more force than one would expect from a woman.

## Book 32 Chapter 6: Isocrates Comes to Rome as Ambassador

After they had squeezed as much money as possible out of everyone, men and women alike, they nevertheless brought all those who had been proscribed before the people.

And most of the people in Phoenice, partly out of fear and partly because they had been won over by Charops and his circle, condemned all those who had been denounced, not to exile but to death, on the charge that their sympathies were alien to Rome.

So all these people fled, while Charops set out for Rome, taking money with him and bringing along Myrton and his associates, because he wanted to have his lawlessness sealed with the senate's approval.

At that moment there appeared both a splendid example of Roman policy and a splendid sight for all the Greeks who were staying there, especially for those who had been summoned.

For Marcus, who was chief priest and was enrolled first in the senate, and Lucius, the conqueror of Perseus, who possessed the greatest credit and influence, when they learned what Charops had done in Epirus, forbade Charops to enter their houses.

When this became widely known, all the visiting Greeks were overjoyed, applauding the Romans' hatred of wickedness.

After this, when Charops entered the senate, it did not agree to what he was asking and did not wish to give a definite reply,

but said that it would give instructions to the envoys who were being sent out to examine what had happened.

But Charops, after withdrawing, concealed that answer, and instead wrote home a report adapted to his own interests, saying that the Romans approved what he was doing.

## Book 32 Chapter 7: Isocrates the Grammarian

Olympiad 155, year 2. Affairs in Italy.

Envoys came from the Athenians, and from the Achaeans came Thearidas and Stephanus on behalf of the Delians.

For after the Romans had given the Delians the answer that, once Delos had been granted to the Athenians, they themselves must withdraw from the island,

though they might carry away their possessions, the Delians moved to Achaea, obtained citizenship there, and wished to obtain justice from the Athenians under the agreement between the Athenians and the Achaeans.

But since the Athenians claimed that this jurisdiction did not apply to them, the Delians asked the Achaeans for the right of reprisal against the Athenians.

It was on this matter that they had come on embassy at that time, and they received the answer that the arrangements lawfully made by the Achaeans concerning the Delians were to remain valid.

## Book 32 Chapter 8: Legacy of L. Aemilius Paulus

II. Affairs at Pergamum.

King Eumenes was crippled in bodily strength, but by the brilliance of his spirit he still held out,

a man who in most respects was second to none of the kings of his own age, and in the greatest and noblest matters was greater and more distinguished than they were.

For in the first place, when he inherited from his father a kingdom narrowed down to a few small and meager towns, he made his own power a match for the greatest dynasties of his age,

relying not chiefly on fortune or on sudden reversals, but on intelligence, hard work, and his own action.

Second, he showed the greatest eagerness for honor, and benefited more Greek cities than any of the kings of his time, while also attaching more private individuals personally to himself.

Third, although he had three brothers close to him in age and active in affairs, he kept all of them obedient to him, serving as his supporters and preserving the dignity of the kingship.

Such a thing one would rarely find to have happened.

## Book 32 Chapter 9: Scipio the Younger and Polybius

Olympiad 155, year 3. I. Affairs in Italy.

Because the Issaeans had repeatedly sent embassies to Rome and made it clear that the Delmatae were injuring their territory and the cities allied with them,

namely Epetium and Tragurium, and because the Daorsi were making the same complaint,

the senate sent envoys under Gaius Fannius to inspect conditions in Illyria, and especially those concerning the Delmatae.

While Pleuratus was alive, these people had obeyed him; but after his death, when Gentius succeeded to the kingship, they broke away from him, made war on their neighbors, and subdued the cities next to them, some of which even paid them tribute. This tribute consisted of livestock and grain.

So Fannius and his party set out on this mission.

## Book 32 Chapter 10: Polybius Responds to Scipio's Speech

King Ariarathes arrived in Rome while it was still summer.

Then, after Sextus Julius and Lucius Aurelius had entered office as consuls, he conducted private interviews, shaping both his presentation and his demeanor to fit the situation before him.

Envoys from Demetrius were also present, headed by Miltiades, prepared for either line of argument; for they were ready both to defend themselves against Ariarathes and, out of hostility, to accuse him.

Orophernes too had sent envoys, headed by Timotheus and Diogenes, bringing a crown for Rome and intending to renew friendship and alliance, but above all to join in the case against Ariarathes, defending some points and attacking others.

In these private interviews, however, the party of Diogenes and Miltiades made the stronger impression, since they were several men set against one, and because in the rest of their presentation they had the advantage of seeming to represent successful men against one who had suffered a reverse.

In the explanation of the facts too they were far superior.

For they had the audacity to say absolutely anything on any subject, paying no regard to the truth, while what they said could not be challenged because there was no one present to answer them.

So, with falsehood winning the day almost without a struggle, they thought the affair was proceeding exactly as they wished.

## Book 32 Chapter 11: Character of the Younger Scipio

II. Affairs in Asia.

Many men, through their craving for ever more and their overbearing spirit, have been ensnared by wealth; Orophernes, king of Cappadocia, was caught in this trap, ruined, and driven from his kingdom.

But after summarizing his downfall, I shall bring the narrative back to the usual order that I follow throughout this work.

For just now I passed over events in Greece and anticipated the events in Asia, namely those in Cappadocia, because Ariarathes' departure from Italy and his return to take up affairs there did not admit of any reasonable division.

Therefore I return to describe what happened in Greece at the same time.

Among these events there occurred a peculiar and extraordinary affair connected with the city of the Oropians.

I shall summarize the whole business by going back over some points and anticipating others, so that, since the matter is not especially notable in itself, I may not make the narrative trivial and obscure by reporting it piecemeal in separate stretches of time.

For when even the whole story can scarcely seem worth close attention to the hearer, who among those eager to learn would pay attention to disconnected parts told at intervals?

For the most part, when they are successful, people are generally of one mind; but in failure, becoming irritated by events, they turn touchy and hard to deal with even toward their friends.

This is what happened in the case of Orophernes too: when events turned against him, he and Theotimus began blaming one another. [Text missing.]

Polybius says in Book 32 that after ruling Cappadocia for only a short time and casting aside the customs of his fathers, Orophernes introduced a life of revelry and theatrical extravagance.

## Book 32 Chapter 12: Scipio's Generosity to his Mother

Attalus, the brother of Eumenes, when he took power, first gave proof of his own purpose and policy by restoring Ariarathes to his kingdom.

## Book 32 Chapter 13: Scipio's Liberality

Olympiad 155, year 4. I. Affairs in Italy.

When Gaius Fannius and his colleagues returned from Illyria and reported that the Delmatae were so far from making any amends for the charges brought against them by those who said they were being continually wronged, that they would not even grant them a hearing at all, declaring that they had nothing in common with the Romans;

and further explained that no lodging or provisions had been given them, and that the Delmatae had even taken away by force the horses they had obtained from another city;

and that they had been ready to come to blows, had they not yielded to the circumstances and made their departure with great restraint and calm;

when the senate heard this report, it was seriously angered by the disobedience and rudeness of the Delmatae, but above all it judged the moment favorable for making war on them, for several reasons.

For these parts of Illyria, the regions stretching toward the Adriatic, had been left entirely unvisited by the Romans

ever since they drove out Demetrius of Pharos; and besides, they did not want the people of Italy to grow soft in any way through a long peace.

For this was then the twelfth year since the war with Perseus and the settlement in Macedonia. Therefore they resolved

to begin a war against the people just mentioned, in order both, as it were, to renew the drive and eagerness of their own people, and by striking the Illyrians with fear to force them to obey Roman commands.

These, then, were the reasons why the Romans went to war with the Delmatae; but to outsiders they presented the war as one undertaken because of the outrage committed against their envoys.

## Book 32 Chapter 14: Scipio's Manliness

At this time envoys arrived from Epirus, both from those who held Phoenice and from those who had been expelled, and they pleaded their case in person.

The senate replied that it would give instructions on these matters to the envoys being sent to Illyria with Gaius Marcius.

## Book 32 Chapter 15: Scipio's Courage

II. War between Prusias and Attalus.

After defeating Attalus, Prusias advanced against Pergamum, prepared a lavish sacrifice, and offered it at the sanctuary of Asclepius.

After sacrificing oxen and obtaining favorable omens, he then returned to camp.

But on the next day he drew up his army against the Nikephorium, destroyed all the temples and sacred precincts of the gods, and plundered the statues and even the stone images.

At last he even carried off the statue of Asclepius itself, an exceptional work by Phyromachus,

and took it away for himself, though the day before he had poured libations before it, sacrificed oxen to it, and prayed to it, asking, as one would expect, that the god be gracious and favorable to him in every way.

I have said elsewhere, when speaking of Philip, that such states of mind are a kind of madness.

For to sacrifice at one and the same time and try by such acts to appease the divine power, bowing down and extravagantly flattering the sacrificial tables and altars—as Prusias used to do, dropping to his knees and acting in a soft, unmanly way—

and at the same time to ravage those same holy things and, by destroying them, display one's insolence toward the gods—how could anyone fail to call this the work of a frenzied anger and a mind driven out of all reason?

And that is exactly what happened with Prusias then. Having accomplished nothing brave in his assaults, but having behaved basely and without courage in his dealings both with gods and with men, he moved his army on to Elaea.

And after testing Elaea and making some attacks, but being unable to achieve anything because Sosander, the king's companion, had entered the city with soldiers and blocked his attempts, he marched off to Thyatira.

On the way back he violently plundered the sanctuary of Artemis at the Sacred Village.

In the same way, near Temnus, he not only plundered the precinct of Apollo Cynneius, but also destroyed it with fire.

After doing this he returned home, having made war not only on men but on the gods as well.

And on the return journey Prusias' infantry also suffered terribly from famine and dysentery, so that the vengeance that met him at once for these acts seemed to have been sent by heaven. [Text breaks.]

## Book 32 Chapter 16: Scipio's Success Due to his Character

After his defeat by Prusias, Attalus chose his brother Athenaeus and sent him with Publius and his party to explain to the senate what had happened.

For when Andronicus had come to Rome and reported the enemy's first advance,

the men there paid no attention, but suspected that Attalus, wishing to be the one to attack Prusias himself, was laying the groundwork for excuses and trying to prejudice opinion against him in advance.

And when Nicomedes and Antiphilus and the other envoys sent by Prusias insisted that none of this was true, the stories told about Prusias were believed even less.

But after some time, when the matter became clearer, the senate, being uncertain because of the conflicting reports, sent Lucius Apuleius and Gaius Petronius as envoys to inspect how matters stood between the kings just mentioned.
